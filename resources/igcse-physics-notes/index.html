Topic 2.1 · Thermal Physics

The Kinetic Particle Model of Matter

Everything is made of particles that never stop moving. This one idea explains the properties of solids, liquids and gases, what temperature really measures, why gases exert pressure and why there is a lowest possible temperature.

Solids, liquids and gases

PropertySolidLiquidGas
ShapeFixedTakes the shape of its containerSpreads out to fill its container
VolumeFixedFixedNot fixed: fills whatever space is available
Can it be compressed?No (almost not at all)No (almost not at all)Yes, easily
Can it flow?NoYesYes

The particle model

SolidLiquidGas
ArrangementRegular pattern (lattice)IrregularRandom
SeparationVery close, touchingClose, mostly touchingFar apart (about 10× further than in a liquid)
MotionVibrate about fixed positionsMove around, sliding past each otherMove quickly in random directions
Arrangement Separation Motion — describe all three for full marks.

Supplement Forces and distances between particles

  • Solid: strong forces of attraction hold particles in fixed positions, so the solid keeps its shape.
  • Liquid: particles have enough energy to move out of fixed positions, but the attractive forces are still strong enough to keep them close together. That is why a liquid flows but keeps its volume.
  • Gas: particles are far apart and the forces between them are negligible (except when they collide), so a gas spreads out and is easy to compress.

Temperature and particle energy

  • When a substance is heated, its particles gain kinetic energy and move (or vibrate) faster.
  • The temperature of a substance is a measure of the average kinetic energy of its particles. Higher temperature = faster particles on average.

Gas pressure

Gas particles move randomly at high speed. Every time one hits a wall of its container it bounces off and exerts a tiny force. Billions of these collisions every second add up to a steady force on each area of wall, and that is gas pressure.

  • Heat the gas (fixed volume): particles move faster, so they hit the walls more often and harder. Pressure increases.
  • Squash the gas (fixed temperature): the same particles are in a smaller space, so they hit the walls more often. Pressure increases.

Supplement Explaining pressure with force

Each collision changes the momentum of a particle, so the wall exerts a force on the particle and the particle exerts an equal force on the wall. Pressure is this total force divided by the area of the wall (p = F / A). More frequent or harder collisions mean a larger total force on the same area, so a higher pressure.

Absolute zero and the Kelvin scale

  • Cooling a substance slows its particles down. At absolute zero (−273 °C) the particles have the least possible kinetic energy, so nothing can be colder.
  • The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero. One kelvin is the same size as one degree Celsius.
T (in K) = θ (in °C) + 273
Worked example
Convert 27 °C and 0 K into the other scale.
27 °C → 27 + 273 = 300 K
0 K → 0 − 273 = −273 °C (absolute zero)
Common Mistake
Writing “300 °K”. Kelvin has no degree sign: write 300 K.

Supplement Pressure and volume of a gas

For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, pressure and volume are inversely proportional: halve the volume and the pressure doubles.

pV = constant   or   p1V1 = p2V2

Why: in a smaller volume the particles travel a shorter distance between collisions with the walls, so they hit the walls more often. At the same temperature they hit with the same average force, so the total force per unit area (pressure) rises.

Worked example
A syringe holds 60 cm3 of air at 100 kPa. The plunger is pushed in slowly (temperature stays the same) until the volume is 20 cm3. Calculate the new pressure. [3]
p1V1 = p2V2
100 × 60 = p2 × 20
p2 = 6000 ÷ 20 = 300 kPa
Check: the volume became 3× smaller, so the pressure should be 3× bigger. ✓
Examiner Tip
Volumes and pressures can stay in any units (cm3, kPa) as long as both sides of p1V1 = p2V2 use the same units. Always state “constant temperature” if asked for the conditions.

Brownian motion

If you look at smoke in air (or pollen grains in water) through a microscope, the specks jiggle around in a jerky, random way. This is Brownian motion.

  • It is caused by the specks being hit by the invisible, fast-moving air (or water) molecules from all sides.
  • It is evidence that the particles of a gas or liquid are moving randomly.

Supplement Why the specks move

The smoke particles are much larger and heavier than air molecules. At any instant, more molecules happen to hit one side than the other, so there is an unbalanced force and the speck moves a little. The direction keeps changing at random. Because a light, fast-moving molecule can move a massive speck, the molecules must be moving very fast.

Common Mistake
Saying the smoke particles are the air molecules. You can see the smoke specks; the air molecules hitting them are far too small to see.

Quick Checklist

  • I can describe the properties of solids, liquids and gases and their particle arrangement, separation and motion.
  • I can explain the forces between particles in each state (Supplement).
  • I can link temperature to the average kinetic energy of particles.
  • I can explain gas pressure using particle collisions, and how it changes with temperature and volume.
  • I can convert between °C and K and state the value of absolute zero.
  • I can use pV = constant and explain it using particles (Supplement).
  • I can describe Brownian motion and explain what it shows (Supplement: why the specks move).
Topic 2.2 · Thermal Physics

Thermal Properties and Temperature

What happens when things are heated: they expand, their temperature rises by an amount that depends on the material, and at the right temperatures they melt, boil or evaporate.

Thermal expansion

Most substances expand when heated. The particles do not get bigger: they move or vibrate more, so they take up more space.

StateHow much it expandsSupplement Why
SolidLeastStrong forces hold the particles tightly; they only vibrate more.
LiquidMoreForces are weaker, so particles move further apart as they gain energy.
GasMostForces between particles are negligible, so nothing holds them back.

Everyday examples

  • Gaps in bridges and railway tracks leave room for the metal to expand on hot days without buckling.
  • Overhead power cables are hung slack so they don’t snap when they contract in cold weather.
  • Liquid-in-glass thermometers: the liquid expands up a narrow tube as temperature rises.
  • Loosening a tight metal lid by running it under hot water: the metal lid expands more than the glass jar.

Specific heat capacity

  • Heating an object increases its internal energy (the total energy of its particles), which raises its temperature.
  • Different materials need different amounts of energy to warm up by the same amount. Water needs a lot; metals need much less.

Supplement Definition and equation

Specific heat capacity (c) is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C.

c = ΔE ÷ (m Δθ)   or   ΔE = m c Δθ
ΔE
Energy supplied, in J
m
Mass, in kg
c
Specific heat capacity, in J/(kg °C)
Δθ
Temperature change, in °C
Worked example
A kettle heats 0.50 kg of water from 20 °C to 100 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/(kg °C). Calculate the energy needed. [3]
Δθ = 100 − 20 = 80 °C
ΔE = m c Δθ = 0.50 × 4200 × 80
ΔE = 168 000 J (168 kJ)

Measuring specific heat capacity

1
Set upMeasure the mass of a metal block (or water in a beaker). Put an electric heater and a thermometer in holes in the block, and wrap it in insulation.
2
HeatRecord the starting temperature. Switch on the heater for a measured time, using a joulemeter (or voltmeter, ammeter and stopwatch: E = VIt) to find the energy supplied.
3
CalculateRecord the highest temperature reached. Use c = ΔE ÷ (m Δθ).
Examiner Tip
The measured value is usually too high, because some energy escapes to the surroundings instead of heating the block. Insulation reduces this error. Say this if asked to explain why the result differs from the textbook value.

Melting, boiling and evaporation

  • While a substance is melting or boiling, energy is still going in but its temperature stays the same. The energy is used to overcome the forces between particles, not to speed them up.
  • For pure water at standard atmospheric pressure: melting point 0 °C, boiling point 100 °C.
  • Condensation (gas → liquid) and solidification (liquid → solid): particles lose energy, slow down and the attractive forces pull them closer together.
  • Evaporation happens at the surface of a liquid at any temperature. Only the more energetic particles near the surface have enough energy to escape.
  • Evaporation causes cooling: the fastest particles leave, so the average kinetic energy of those left behind is lower, and temperature measures average kinetic energy.

Supplement Boiling vs evaporation

BoilingEvaporation
Only at the boiling pointAt any temperature
Throughout the liquid (bubbles form)Only at the surface
Needs a continuous energy supplyUses energy from the liquid itself, so the liquid cools

Supplement What makes evaporation faster

  • Higher temperature: more particles have enough energy to escape.
  • Larger surface area: more particles are at the surface.
  • Air moving over the surface (wind, a fan): escaped particles are carried away before they can fall back in.

Cooling something by evaporation: when a liquid evaporates from an object (sweat on skin, water on a clay pot), the liquid cools, and energy flows from the warmer object into the liquid. The object cools down.

Model answer
Explain why you feel cold when you step out of a swimming pool on a windy day. [3]
Water on the skin evaporates. The most energetic water molecules escape from the surface, so the average kinetic energy of the remaining water falls and it becomes colder. Energy then flows from the skin into the cooler water, cooling the skin. The wind removes escaped molecules so evaporation is faster, causing more cooling.

Quick Checklist

  • I can describe thermal expansion of solids, liquids and gases and give everyday examples.
  • I can explain the relative amounts of expansion using particles (Supplement).
  • I can link a rise in temperature to an increase in internal energy.
  • I can define specific heat capacity and use ΔE = mcΔθ (Supplement), and describe how to measure it.
  • I can explain why temperature stays constant during melting and boiling, and recall 0 °C and 100 °C for water.
  • I can explain evaporation and why it causes cooling.
  • I can compare boiling and evaporation and list the factors that affect evaporation (Supplement).
Topic 2.3 · Thermal Physics

Transfer of Thermal Energy

Thermal energy always flows from hotter to colder places, by conduction, convection or radiation. Expect “explain using particles” questions and applications such as cooking pans, fridges and the Earth’s temperature.

Conduction

Conduction is the transfer of thermal energy through a material, mainly solids, without the material itself moving.

  • Good thermal conductors: metals (copper, aluminium, iron).
  • Poor conductors (insulators): plastic, wood, glass, air, wool. Materials that trap air, such as foam and fleece, are especially good insulators.
  • Liquids and gases are poor conductors because their particles are far apart.

Showing which material conducts best

Attach a pin to the end of equal-sized rods of different metals with wax, then heat the other ends equally. The pin falls first from the best conductor.

Supplement How conduction works

  • Lattice vibrations (all solids): particles at the hot end vibrate more strongly and pass this vibration on to their neighbours, one by one. This is slow.
  • Free (delocalised) electrons (metals only): electrons at the hot end gain kinetic energy and move quickly through the metal, colliding with particles further away and transferring energy. This is much faster, which is why metals are the best conductors.

Convection

Convection is the transfer of thermal energy by the movement of a liquid or gas (a fluid). It cannot happen in solids.

1
Fluid near the heater warms upIts particles move faster and spread further apart, so the fluid expands.
2
It becomes less dense and risesThe same mass now takes up more volume.
3
Cooler, denser fluid sinksIt moves in to replace the rising fluid, is heated in turn and rises.
4
A convection current formsA continuous loop of moving fluid carries energy around.

Showing convection: drop a crystal of potassium manganate(VII) at the bottom edge of a beaker of water and heat gently below it. The purple colour rises above the flame, moves across the top and sinks on the far side.

Common Mistake
“Heat rises” earns no marks. It is the warmer, less dense fluid that rises.

Thermal radiation

  • Thermal radiation is infrared electromagnetic radiation. All objects emit it, and hotter objects emit more.
  • It needs no medium, so it can travel through a vacuum. That is how energy reaches us from the Sun.
SurfaceEmitting infraredAbsorbing infraredReflecting infrared
Dull blackBest emitterBest absorberWorst reflector
Shiny white or silverWorst emitterWorst absorberBest reflector

Comparing surfaces

Fill identical cans painted different colours (or a Leslie cube with four different faces) with the same volume of hot water. Measure the infrared from each surface with an infrared detector at the same distance, or record how fast each can cools. The dull black surface emits most and cools fastest.

Supplement Rate of radiation and the Earth’s temperature

  • The rate at which an object emits radiation increases with its surface temperature and its surface area.
  • An object stays at a constant temperature when it absorbs energy at the same rate as it emits it. If it absorbs faster, it warms up; if it emits faster, it cools down.
  • The Earth absorbs radiation from the Sun and emits infrared back into space. Its temperature depends on the balance. Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere absorb some of the outgoing infrared and re-emit some back towards the Earth. More greenhouse gas means less energy escapes, so the balance is reached at a higher temperature. On a cloudy night, clouds trap more infrared, so it stays warmer than on a clear night.

Consequences of thermal energy transfer

ExamplePhysics behind it
Saucepan: metal base, plastic or wooden handleMetal conducts energy to the food quickly; the handle is an insulator so you don’t get burnt.
Freezer compartment at the top of a fridgeAir cooled at the top becomes denser and sinks, setting up a convection current that cools the whole fridge.
Room heater placed low downWarm air rises from it and convection currents carry energy round the room.
Car radiatorHot coolant flows through a thin metal radiator with a large surface area; air passing over it carries energy away by convection.
Houses in hot countries painted whiteWhite reflects most of the Sun’s infrared, so less is absorbed.
Wearing several thin layersAir trapped between layers is a poor conductor and can’t move to form convection currents.
Examiner Tip
For “explain” questions, name the process and say what the material or design does to it, e.g. “the shiny surface is a poor emitter, so it reduces energy lost by radiation”.

Quick Checklist

  • I can name good and poor thermal conductors and describe an experiment to compare them.
  • I can explain conduction by lattice vibrations and free electrons (Supplement).
  • I can explain convection using density changes and describe how to show a convection current.
  • I can describe thermal radiation as infrared that needs no medium, and compare black and shiny surfaces.
  • I can explain how surface temperature, area and the absorb/emit balance affect temperature, including the Earth’s (Supplement).
  • I can explain everyday applications of conduction, convection and radiation.
Topic 3.1 · Waves

General Properties of Waves

The vocabulary and the one equation that every other wave topic uses, plus what happens when waves reflect, refract and diffract.

What a wave does

Waves transfer energy from one place to another without transferring matter. The particles of the medium vibrate about fixed positions; they don’t travel with the wave.

Wavelength (λ)
Distance between two neighbouring crests (or any two matching points). Unit: m
Amplitude
Maximum distance of a point from its rest position (centre line). Unit: m
Frequency (f)
Number of waves passing a point per second. Unit: hertz, Hz
Wave speed (v)
Distance travelled by the wave per second. Unit: m/s
Crest / trough
The highest / lowest points of a transverse wave
Wavefront
A line joining matching points on a wave, e.g. all the crests. Drawn as lines one wavelength apart
wave speed = frequency × wavelength    v = f λ
Worked example
A water wave has a frequency of 2.5 Hz and a wavelength of 0.40 m. Calculate its speed. Then find the wavelength of a radio wave of frequency 100 MHz (speed 3.0 × 108 m/s).
v = fλ = 2.5 × 0.40 = 1.0 m/s
λ = v ÷ f = 3.0 × 108 ÷ 100 × 106 = 3.0 m
Common Mistake
Forgetting to convert prefixes: kHz × 103, MHz × 106, GHz × 109. Also, the source sets the frequency, so frequency does not change when a wave moves into a new medium; speed and wavelength do.

Transverse and longitudinal waves

TransverseLongitudinal
Vibrations are…Perpendicular (at 90°) to the direction the wave travelsParallel to the direction the wave travels
FeaturesCrests and troughsCompressions (particles close together) and rarefactions (particles spread out)
ExamplesAll electromagnetic waves (including light), water waves, seismic S-wavesSound, seismic P-waves
Examiner Tip
On a slinky: move your hand side to side for a transverse wave, and push and pull along its length for a longitudinal wave. Questions often describe exactly this.

Reflection, refraction and diffraction

A ripple tank shows these effects with water waves. A light above it projects the wavefronts onto a screen below.

  • Reflection: waves bounce off a flat barrier. The angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence, and the wavelength stays the same.
  • Refraction: waves change speed when they move into a different medium (in a ripple tank, into shallower water where they slow down). The wavelength gets shorter and, if the waves arrive at an angle, they change direction.
  • Diffraction: waves spread out when they pass through a gap or round the edge of an obstacle. The wavelength stays the same.

Supplement How much do waves diffract?

  • Diffraction is greatest when the gap is about the same size as the wavelength. A gap much wider than the wavelength gives only a little spreading at the edges.
  • Longer wavelengths diffract more round obstacles. That is why you can hear someone round a corner (sound wavelengths are around a metre) but can’t see them (light wavelengths are less than a millionth of a metre).

Quick Checklist

  • I can state that waves transfer energy without transferring matter.
  • I can define wavelength, amplitude, frequency, wave speed, crest, trough and wavefront.
  • I can use v = fλ, including unit prefixes.
  • I can compare transverse and longitudinal waves with examples.
  • I can describe reflection, refraction and diffraction in a ripple tank.
  • I can explain how gap size and wavelength affect diffraction (Supplement).

Light: Reflection and Refraction

How light behaves at mirrors and at the boundary between air and glass or water, including total internal reflection and optical fibres. Ray diagrams must be drawn with a ruler and accurate angles.

Key words

Normal
A line drawn at 90° to a surface where the ray hits it (draw it dashed)
Angle of incidence (i)
Angle between the incoming ray and the normal
Angle of reflection
Angle between the reflected ray and the normal
Angle of refraction (r)
Angle between the refracted ray and the normal
Common Mistake
Measuring angles from the surface instead of from the normal. Every angle in this topic is measured from the normal.

Reflection in a plane mirror

Law of reflection: angle of incidence = angle of reflection.

The image in a plane mirror is:

  • the same size as the object
  • the same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front
  • virtual (it can’t be shown on a screen, because the light only appears to come from it)
  • upright, and laterally inverted (left and right swapped)

Supplement Drawing the image in a plane mirror

1
Find the imageMark the image point the same perpendicular distance behind the mirror as the object is in front.
2
Draw the reflected raysDraw straight lines from the image point to the eye. Where each line crosses the mirror, draw it solid in front of the mirror and dashed behind it (the dashed part is virtual).
3
Draw the incident raysJoin the object to each point where the rays meet the mirror. Add arrows pointing from object to mirror to eye.

Refraction

Light changes speed when it passes from one material into another. If it hits the boundary at an angle, this makes it change direction: this is refraction.

  • Air → glass or water: light slows down and bends towards the normal (r is smaller than i).
  • Glass or water → air: light speeds up and bends away from the normal.
  • A ray that hits the boundary along the normal (i = 0) changes speed but does not change direction.

Investigating refraction with a glass block

Draw round a rectangular block on paper. Shine a narrow ray from a ray box into one side, mark the ray going in and coming out with crosses, then remove the block and join the points. Draw the normal and measure i and r with a protractor. Repeat for several angles of incidence.

Supplement Refractive index

The refractive index (n) of a material tells you how much it bends (and slows) light. It has no units, and the larger it is, the more the light bends. Glass is about 1.5; water is about 1.33.

n = sin i ÷ sin r

Here i is the angle in air and r is the angle in the material.

Worked example
Light enters a plastic block with an angle of incidence of 50°. The angle of refraction is 31°. Calculate the refractive index of the plastic.
n = sin 50° ÷ sin 31° = 0.766 ÷ 0.515 = 1.49

Total internal reflection

  • When light travels from glass (or water) towards air, some is refracted out and some is reflected back.
  • As the angle of incidence increases, the refracted ray bends further from the normal. At the critical angle (c), the refracted ray travels along the boundary (angle of refraction = 90°).
  • If the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle, all the light is reflected back inside: total internal reflection (TIR).
Examiner Tip
TIR needs two conditions: (1) light travelling from a denser to a less dense medium (e.g. glass to air), and (2) angle of incidence greater than the critical angle. Many answers lose a mark by giving only one.

Supplement Critical angle and optical fibres

n = 1 ÷ sin c
Worked example
Glass has a refractive index of 1.50. Calculate its critical angle.
sin c = 1 ÷ n = 1 ÷ 1.50 = 0.667
c = sin−1(0.667) = 42°

Optical fibres are thin, flexible strands of very clear glass. Light entering one end hits the inside surface at an angle greater than the critical angle, so it is totally internally reflected again and again and comes out of the far end with very little loss.

  • Communications: carrying telephone, cable TV and broadband signals as pulses of light or infrared.
  • Medicine: an endoscope uses bundles of fibres to carry light into the body and an image back out, so doctors can look inside without major surgery.

Quick Checklist

  • I can define normal, angle of incidence, angle of reflection and angle of refraction.
  • I can state the law of reflection and describe the image in a plane mirror.
  • I can construct a ray diagram for a plane mirror image (Supplement).
  • I can describe refraction through a glass block and say which way light bends.
  • I can use n = sin i ÷ sin r (Supplement).
  • I can explain total internal reflection and the critical angle.
  • I can use n = 1 ÷ sin c and describe uses of optical fibres (Supplement).

Supplement Virtual images and the magnifying glass

  • When the object is closer to the lens than F, the rays leaving the lens spread apart and never meet. Extend them backwards with dashed lines: they appear to meet behind the object. That is where the virtual image is.
  • A virtual image can’t be projected on a screen. A magnifying glass uses a converging lens this way to give an upright, enlarged image.

Dispersion of light

  • When white light passes through a glass prism, it is refracted and splits into a spectrum of colours: this is dispersion.
  • Each colour refracts by a slightly different amount: violet bends the most, red the least.
  • The seven colours, in order: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet (“ROY G BIV”).

Supplement Colour, frequency and wavelength

  • From red to violet, frequency increases and wavelength decreases. Red has the longest wavelength of visible light; violet has the shortest.
  • Monochromatic light has a single frequency (one colour), e.g. light from a laser.

  • I can explain how lenses correct short and long sight (Supplement).

  • I can link the colours of the spectrum to frequency and wavelength and define monochromatic (Supplement).
Topic 3.3 · Waves

The Electromagnetic Spectrum

The family of waves from radio to gamma: the order, what they have in common, what each one is used for, and the harm some of them can do. Learn the order first; most marks follow from it.

Rich Men In Vegas Use X-ray Glasses — radio to gamma.

Properties all EM waves share

  • They are all transverse waves.
  • They can all travel through a vacuum.
  • They all travel at the same speed in a vacuum: 3.0 × 108 m/s (about the same in air).
  • They all transfer energy, and can be reflected and refracted.

Uses

WaveUses
Radio wavesRadio and television broadcasting, astronomy, radio-frequency identification (RFID) tags
MicrowavesSatellite TV, mobile phones, microwave ovens
InfraredElectric grills and heaters, remote controls, intruder alarms, thermal imaging cameras, optical fibres
Visible lightSeeing, photography, lighting
UltravioletSecurity marking (invisible ink that glows under UV), detecting fake bank notes, sterilising water
X-raysMedical imaging of bones, airport security scanners
Gamma raysSterilising food and medical equipment, detecting and treating cancer

Harmful effects

WaveDanger
MicrowavesHeat the inside of the body (internal heating of body tissue)
InfraredSkin burns
UltravioletDamage to surface cells and eyes, leading to skin cancer and eye conditions
X-rays and gamma raysMutations or damage to cells in the body, which can lead to cancer
Examiner Tip
Higher frequency means more energy and generally more danger. UV, X-rays and gamma are ionising, so they can damage DNA. Be specific: “UV can cause skin cancer”, not “UV is dangerous”.

EM waves in communication

  • Microwaves: mobile phones, wireless internet and satellite communication. They pass easily through the atmosphere to and from satellites.
  • Radio waves: Bluetooth and radio broadcasting. Longer radio waves diffract around hills and buildings, so they reach areas without a direct line of sight.
  • Visible light and infrared: optical fibres for cable TV and high-speed broadband, because the glass is very transparent to them and they can carry a lot of data.

Supplement Analogue and digital signals

  • An analogue signal varies continuously and can take any value (like the sound wave from your voice).
  • A digital signal has only two values: on and off (1 and 0).
  • Sound can be sent as either. To send it digitally, the analogue signal is converted into a code of 1s and 0s.

Benefits of digital signals:

  • As any signal travels, it gets weaker and picks up noise (random unwanted changes). A digital signal can be regenerated: it only has to be recognised as a 1 or a 0, so the noise is removed and a clean copy is sent on. When an analogue signal is amplified, the noise is amplified with it.
  • This means digital signals can travel longer distances with no loss of quality, and data can be sent faster.

Quick Checklist

  • I can list the EM spectrum in order and say which way frequency and wavelength change.
  • I can state the properties shared by all EM waves, including the speed 3.0 × 108 m/s.
  • I can give at least two uses of each type of EM wave.
  • I can describe the harmful effects of microwaves, infrared, UV, X-rays and gamma rays.
  • I can explain which EM waves are used for different kinds of communication.
  • I can compare analogue and digital signals and explain the benefits of digital (Supplement).
Topic 3.4 · Waves

Sound

How sound is produced and travels, how pitch and loudness relate to the wave, how to measure the speed of sound, and how ultrasound is used to see inside things.

How sound is made and travels

  • Sound is produced by vibrating objects (a guitar string, a loudspeaker cone, your vocal cords).
  • Sound is a longitudinal wave: the particles of the medium vibrate back and forth parallel to the direction the wave travels.
  • It travels as a series of compressions (particles squashed together, higher pressure) and rarefactions (particles spread out, lower pressure).
  • Sound needs a medium (solid, liquid or gas) and cannot travel through a vacuum. If air is pumped out of a jar containing a ringing bell, the sound fades even though you can still see the bell vibrating.

Hearing, pitch and loudness

  • The range of human hearing is about 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz (20 kHz).
  • Higher frequency = higher pitch.
  • Larger amplitude = louder sound.
  • An echo is a reflected sound wave.
Examiner Tip
On an oscilloscope trace, more waves across the screen means higher frequency (higher pitch); taller waves mean larger amplitude (louder). Compare the traces carefully: questions often change only one of these.

The speed of sound

  • In air, the speed of sound is about 330–350 m/s.
  • Sound travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids and slowest in gases.

Measuring the speed of sound in air (echo method)

1
Set upStand a measured distance (e.g. 50 m, using a tape measure) from a large wall.
2
Clap and timeClap in time with the echoes so each clap coincides with the echo of the one before. Time 20 claps with a stopwatch.
3
CalculateTime for one clap = total ÷ 20. The sound travelled to the wall and back, so speed = 2 × distance ÷ time for one clap.

Another method: two people stand a measured distance apart. One makes a loud sound with a visible action (bangs two blocks together) and the other starts a stopwatch on seeing it and stops it on hearing it. Light arrives almost instantly, so speed = distance ÷ time.

Worked example
A student stands 85 m from a cliff and hears the echo of a shout 0.50 s later. Calculate the speed of sound.
Distance travelled = there and back = 2 × 85 = 170 m
speed = distance ÷ time = 170 ÷ 0.50 = 340 m/s
Common Mistake
Forgetting to double the distance in echo questions. The sound goes to the wall and back.

Supplement Ultrasound

Ultrasound is sound with a frequency above 20 kHz, too high for humans to hear. It partly reflects at boundaries between different materials, and the time taken for the echo to return shows how deep the boundary is.

  • Non-destructive testing: finding cracks or flaws inside metal parts (such as aircraft wings or rail tracks) without damaging them. A crack reflects some ultrasound back early.
  • Medical scanning of soft tissue: e.g. scanning an unborn baby. Unlike X-rays, ultrasound is not ionising, so it is safe.
  • Sonar: ships send pulses of ultrasound down and time the echo from the sea bed or a shoal of fish to find the depth.
Worked example
A ship sends an ultrasound pulse to the sea bed. The echo returns after 0.80 s. The speed of sound in sea water is 1500 m/s. Calculate the depth of the sea. [3]
Total distance = speed × time = 1500 × 0.80 = 1200 m
This is there and back, so depth = 1200 ÷ 2 = 600 m

The Nuclear Model of the Atom

What atoms are made of, how we know the nucleus exists, how to read nuclide notation, and how nuclear fission and fusion release energy.

The structure of the atom

  • An atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus at its centre, made of protons and neutrons.
  • Electrons (negatively charged) orbit the nucleus. Most of the atom is empty space.
  • An atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so it is neutral overall.
  • An atom that loses electrons becomes a positive ion; one that gains electrons becomes a negative ion.

Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same number of protons) with different numbers of neutrons, so different nucleon numbers. Example: carbon-12 (6 neutrons) and carbon-14 (8 neutrons). Some isotopes are unstable and radioactive.

Topic 5.2 · Nuclear Physics

Radioactivity

The three types of nuclear radiation, how they are detected, how nuclei change when they decay, how to work with half-life, and how to use radioactive sources safely.

Detecting radiation and background radiation

  • Ionising nuclear radiation is measured with a detector such as a Geiger–Müller (GM) tube connected to a counter. The count rate is given in counts per second or counts per minute.
  • Background radiation is the low level of radiation around us all the time. Its sources include:
    • radon gas in the air (from rocks and soil)
    • rocks and buildings
    • food and drink
    • cosmic rays from space

Supplement Correcting for background

Measure the background count rate with no source present, then subtract it from every reading taken with the source. This gives the corrected count rate due to the source alone.

The three types of radiation

  • Radioactive decay is spontaneous (nothing triggers it) and random (you can’t predict which nucleus will decay next, or when).
  • It happens because the nucleus is unstable. Emitting radiation makes it more stable.

To identify a source in an experiment: if the count drops when paper is added, it emits alpha. If it drops when aluminium is added (but not paper), beta. If neither makes much difference, gamma.

Supplement Deflection in electric and magnetic fields

  • Alpha and beta are charged, so they are deflected by electric and magnetic fields, in opposite directions.
  • Beta is deflected more than alpha because a beta particle has a much smaller mass.
  • Gamma is uncharged, so it is not deflected.
  • In an electric field, alpha bends towards the negative plate and beta towards the positive plate.

Supplement Why their ionising effects differ

Ionisation happens when radiation knocks electrons off atoms. Alpha particles have a large charge (+2) and are relatively large and slow, so they interact strongly with nearly every atom they pass. They lose all their energy quickly and travel only a short distance. Beta particles have less charge and move faster, so they ionise less. Gamma rays have no charge and rarely interact, so they ionise least and penetrate furthest.

  • In alpha and beta decay the number of protons changes, so the nucleus becomes a different element.
  • Gamma emission does not change the proton or nucleon number. The nucleus just loses energy.

  • Isotopes can be unstable because they have too many neutrons (or too few), or because the nucleus is too heavy.
  • In beta decay, a neutron changes into a proton and an electron. The proton stays in the nucleus and the electron is emitted as the beta particle. That is why Z goes up by 1 but A stays the same.

Check every decay equation: the top numbers must add up to the same total on both sides, and so must the bottom numbers. Then use the new proton number to name the new element from the periodic table given.

Half-life

The half-life of a radioactive isotope is the time taken for half the nuclei in a sample to decay. Equivalently, it is the time taken for the count rate (activity) to fall to half its value.

Worked example
A sample has a count rate of 800 counts/min. Its half-life is 3 hours. What is the count rate after 12 hours?
Number of half-lives = 12 ÷ 3 = 4
800 → 400 → 200 → 100 → 50 counts/min
Worked example Supplement
A detector reads 520 counts/min near a source. The background count rate is 40 counts/min. After 10 days the reading is 160 counts/min. Find the half-life.
Corrected count rates: 520 − 40 = 480, and 160 − 40 = 120
480 → 240 → 120 is 2 half-lives in 10 days
Half-life = 10 ÷ 2 = 5 days
Common Mistake
Thinking a sample is completely gone after two half-lives. After 1 half-life ½ is left, after 2 half-lives ¼, after 3 half-lives ⅛: it never quite reaches zero. In Supplement questions, also remember to subtract background before halving.

Uses of radioactive isotopes

The best isotope for a job depends on the type of radiation (how penetrating it is) and its half-life.

Safety

  • Ionising radiation can kill cells, cause mutations (damage to DNA) and lead to cancer.
  • Keep exposure as low as possible:
    • Reduce exposure time: use the source for as short a time as possible.
    • Increase distance: handle sources with long tongs and never point them at people.
    • Use shielding: store sources in lead-lined boxes and use lead or concrete barriers.

Supplement Explaining the safety precautions

  • Shorter time: fewer particles or rays reach the body, so less ionisation and lower risk.
  • Greater distance: radiation spreads out (and alpha and beta are absorbed by air), so less reaches the body.
  • Shielding: dense materials like lead absorb the radiation before it reaches people.
  • Alpha sources are most dangerous inside the body (if swallowed or breathed in), because alpha is so strongly ionising. Outside the body, skin stops it.

The Earth and the Solar System

Why we have days, seasons and phases of the Moon, what is in the Solar System and how it formed, and how gravity controls the orbits. Expect short recall questions plus calculations with orbital speed and the time light takes to travel.

The Earth, the Sun and the Moon

MotionTime takenWhat it explains
The Earth spins on its axisAbout 24 hoursDay and night, and why the Sun appears to move across the sky from east to west each day. The Sun isn’t moving round us: we are turning.
The Earth orbits the SunAbout 365 daysThe seasons, because the Earth’s axis is tilted (see below)

Seasons

The Earth’s axis is tilted (by about 23°) and always points the same way in space as the Earth orbits the Sun. When your hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun, it is summer: the Sun is higher in the sky, its energy is more concentrated on each square metre of ground and the days are longer. Six months later your hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun and it is winter.

Common Mistake
Saying summer happens because the Earth is closer to the Sun. The distance hardly changes. It is the tilt that matters, which is why the two hemispheres have opposite seasons at the same time.

Phases of the Moon

  • The Moon gives out no light of its own. We see it because it reflects sunlight.
  • The half of the Moon facing the Sun is always lit. As the Moon orbits the Earth, we see different amounts of that lit half.
  • New Moon: the Moon is between the Earth and the Sun, so its lit side faces away from us and we see almost nothing.
  • Full Moon: the Earth is between the Sun and the Moon, so we see the whole lit half.
  • The cycle from new Moon to full Moon and back takes about a month, showing that the Moon orbits the Earth once a month.

What is in the Solar System

  • One star: the Sun.
  • Eight planets orbiting the Sun. In order from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.
  • Minor planets orbiting the Sun, including dwarf planets such as Pluto, and asteroids in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter.
  • Moons (natural satellites), which orbit planets.
  • Smaller bodies, including comets: lumps of ice and dust with very stretched (elliptical) orbits.

How the Solar System formed (the accretion model)

1
A cloud of gas and dustThe Solar System began as a huge cloud of gas and dust in space, which collapsed under its own gravity.
2
A spinning disc formsMost of the material fell to the centre and formed the Sun. The rest formed a spinning disc around it.
3

Light travelling across the Solar System

Distances in space are so large that even light takes time to cross them. Use speed = distance ÷ time with the speed of light, 3.0 × 108 m/s.

Worked example
The Earth is 1.5 × 1011 m from the Sun. How long does sunlight take to reach the Earth?
time = distance ÷ speed = 1.5 × 1011 ÷ 3.0 × 108
time = 500 s (just over 8 minutes)

Gravity and orbits

  • The gravitational attraction of the Sun is the force that keeps the planets, minor planets and comets in orbit.
  • Planets, minor planets and comets move in elliptical orbits (stretched circles). Most planets’ orbits are very nearly circular; comets’ are very stretched.

Using planetary data

Exam questions often give a table of data about the planets (distance from the Sun, orbital time, density, surface temperature, gravitational field strength) and ask you to spot patterns. Look for:

  • The further a planet is from the Sun, the longer its orbit takes and (generally) the colder its surface. Venus is the exception for temperature: its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps energy.
  • The inner rocky planets have higher densities than the outer gas giants.
  • Planets with more mass have a stronger gravitational field at their surface (Jupiter’s is the strongest).
Examiner Tip
When describing a pattern from data, quote numbers from the table to back it up, and mention any planet that doesn’t fit the pattern.

Supplement Gravitational fields

  • The gravitational field strength at the surface of a planet depends on the planet’s mass.
  • The field around a planet (or the Sun) gets weaker as the distance increases.
  • The Sun contains most of the mass of the Solar System (over 99%). This is why everything orbits the Sun.
  • Further from the Sun, its gravitational field is weaker, so the planets’ orbital speeds are lower and their orbits take much longer.

Supplement Orbital speed

2πr is the distance round a circular orbit, so this is just speed = distance ÷ time.

Worked example
The Earth orbits the Sun at an average radius of 1.5 × 1011 m once every 365 days. Calculate its average orbital speed. [3]
T = 365 × 24 × 60 × 60 = 3.15 × 107 s
v = 2πr ÷ T = 2 × π × 1.5 × 1011 ÷ 3.15 × 107
v = 3.0 × 104 m/s (30 km/s)
Common Mistake
Leaving T in days or hours, or r in km. Convert to seconds and metres first (km × 1000).

Supplement Speed in an elliptical orbit

  • An object in an elliptical orbit (such as a comet) moves fastest when it is closest to the Sun and slowest when it is furthest away.
  • Why (conservation of energy): as the comet moves towards the Sun, it loses gravitational potential energy, which is converted to kinetic energy, so it speeds up. As it moves away, kinetic energy is converted back to gravitational potential energy, so it slows down. The total energy stays the same.
  • In a circular orbit the distance from the Sun stays the same, so the speed is constant, but the velocity changes all the time because the direction of motion keeps changing.

Topic 6.2 · Space Physics

Stars and the Universe

What the Sun is and what powers it, how stars are born and die, how big the Universe is, and the evidence that it began with the Big Bang.

The Sun

  • The Sun is a star of medium size.
  • It is made mostly of hydrogen and helium.
  • It radiates most of its energy as infrared, visible light and ultraviolet. Visible light is the peak; infrared has longer wavelengths and ultraviolet shorter.
  • Stars are powered by nuclear reactions that release energy. In stable stars like the Sun, the reaction is the fusion of hydrogen nuclei into helium.

Galaxies and the light-year

  • A galaxy is made up of many billions of stars.
  • The Sun is one of the stars in our galaxy, the Milky Way.
  • Other stars in the Milky Way are much further from Earth than any planet. The nearest, Proxima Centauri, is about 4 light-years away.
  • The Milky Way is one of many billions of galaxies in the Universe. Its diameter is about 100 000 light-years.

A light-year is the distance light travels through space in one year: about 9.5 × 1015 m.

Common Mistake
A light-year is a distance, not a time, even though it has “year” in the name.
Worked example
Show that one light-year is about 9.5 × 1015 m.
One year = 365 × 24 × 60 × 60 = 3.15 × 107 s
distance = speed × time = 3.0 × 108 × 3.15 × 107 = 9.5 × 1015 m

Supplement The life cycle of a star

1
NebulaA star forms from an interstellar cloud of gas and dust, mostly hydrogen, which collapses under gravity.
2
ProtostarAs the cloud collapses, its temperature rises. A protostar is unstable: it is still collapsing and heating up.
3
Stable starWhen the centre is hot enough, hydrogen fusion begins. The star is stable when the inward force of gravity is balanced by the outward force due to the high temperature in its centre. It stays like this for most of its life.
4
Running out of hydrogenWhen most of the hydrogen in the core has been used up, the star swells. A star like the Sun becomes a red giant; a much more massive star becomes a red supergiant.
5
Smaller stars (like the Sun)The red giant throws off its outer layers as a planetary nebula, leaving a small, hot core called a white dwarf.
6
Massive starsThe red supergiant explodes as a supernova, leaving a neutron star or, for the most massive stars, a black hole.
7
RecyclingA supernova throws out a nebula containing hydrogen and new, heavier elements. This material may later form new stars with orbiting planets. The Solar System formed from material like this.
Common Mistake
Mixing up the two nebulas. A planetary nebula comes from a red giant (smaller stars) and has nothing to do with planets. A supernova comes only from massive stars (red supergiants).

The expanding Universe and redshift

  • Redshift is an increase in the observed wavelength of electromagnetic radiation from stars and galaxies that are moving away from us. The light is shifted towards the red end of the spectrum.
  • Light from distant galaxies is redshifted compared with light from the same elements on Earth. So the galaxies are moving away from us.
  • This shows the Universe is expanding and supports the Big Bang Theory: the Universe began from a single point a long time ago and has been expanding ever since.

Supplement Cosmic microwave background radiation (CMBR)

  • The CMBR is microwave radiation of a specific frequency that is detected coming from every direction in space.
  • It was produced shortly after the Universe formed, when the Universe was extremely hot. As the Universe expanded, the wavelength of this radiation was stretched until it reached the microwave region.
  • It is further evidence for the Big Bang.

Supplement The Hubble constant and the age of the Universe

  • The speed v at which a galaxy is moving away is found from the change in wavelength of its light (its redshift).
  • The distance d to a far galaxy can be found from the brightness of a supernova in it: supernovae of a particular type all have about the same true brightness, so a dimmer one is further away.
  • The further away a galaxy is, the faster it is moving away. The ratio is the Hubble constant, H0, currently estimated at about 2.2 × 10−18 per second.
H0 = v ÷ d

On a graph of speed (y-axis) against distance (x-axis) for galaxies, the gradient is H0.

age of the Universe ≈ d ÷ v = 1 ÷ H0

Why: if a galaxy has always moved away at speed v, then d ÷ v is the time it has taken to travel distance d from the point where everything started. Because d ÷ v is the same for every galaxy, everything was together at a single point at that time. This is evidence that the Universe expanded from a single point.

Worked example
A galaxy is moving away from the Earth at 6.6 × 106 m/s. Taking H0 = 2.2 × 10−18 /s, find (a) its distance and (b) the age of the Universe in years.
(a) d = v ÷ H0 = 6.6 × 106 ÷ 2.2 × 10−18 = 3.0 × 1024 m
(b) age = 1 ÷ H0 = 1 ÷ 2.2 × 10−18 = 4.5 × 1017 s
In years: 4.5 × 1017 ÷ 3.15 × 107 = 1.4 × 1010 years (about 14 billion years)

What examiners keep seeing
The examiner reports keep listing the same slips: forgetting to square the speed in ½mv2, leaving a mass in grams, writing efficiency as just “output ÷ input” without the words “useful energy (or power)”, and dropping the unit or the power of ten in the final answer.
0625/43/M/J/24 Q2(c)(i) · May/June 2024 · [1 mark]

Source material: Fig. 2.1 shows solar-powered traffic warning lights. … The efficiency of the solar cell is 22%.

State what is meant by 22% efficiency.

22% of the total energy (power) input to the solar cell is transferred as useful energy (power) output; the other 78% is wasted.

Why this scores
One mark for linking 22% to the useful output as a fraction of the total input. The examiner report notes that answers which just say “22% of the energy is used” are not precise enough: say useful output compared with total input.
0625/42/F/M/23 Q2(c) · February/March 2023 · [2 marks]

A crane lifts a container 48 m vertically upwards. The mass of the container is 30 000 kg. Calculate the energy transferred to the gravitational potential energy stored in the container.

ΔEp = mgΔh
ΔEp = 30 000 × 9.8 × 48
ΔEp = 14 112 000 J = 1.4 × 107 J

Why this scores
One mark for the equation with the values substituted and one for the answer with its unit. The examiner report says weaker answers lost the second mark through the wrong power of ten or no unit: 1.4 × 107 J (or 14 MJ) is fine, “1.4 × 107” alone is not.
0625/41/M/J/21 Q2(b) · May/June 2021 · [3 marks]

Source material: Fig. 2.1 shows a wooden trolley of mass 1.2 kg at rest on the rough surface of a bench. … The trolley moves with an initial speed of 0.065 m / s.

As the trolley moves across the rough surface, it slows down and stops. Explain, in terms of the work done, the energy change that takes place as the trolley slows down.

Friction between the trolley and the rough bench acts against the motion, so work is done against friction as the trolley moves along.

The energy for this work comes from the trolley’s kinetic energy store, so its kinetic energy decreases to zero.

The work done against friction transfers this energy to the thermal (internal) energy store of the trolley, the bench and the surrounding air, which warm up very slightly.

Why this scores
One mark each for work done against friction, kinetic energy decreasing to zero, and thermal energy produced. The examiner report says many candidates described the energy change but never used the phrase “work done”, or only defined work done without linking it to friction: the question asks for it, so say it.
0625/42/O/N/22 Q3(b)(ii) · October/November 2022 · [4 marks]

Source material: Fig. 3.1 shows a small water turbine driven by a tidal flow of water to generate electrical power.

The mass of water passing through the turbine each second is 6.0 × 103 kg. The speed of the water is 2.0 m / s. 40% of the kinetic energy of the water is converted to electrical energy. Calculate the electrical power generated.

Kinetic energy of the water passing through each second:
Ek = ½mv2 = ½ × 6.0 × 103 × 2.02 = 12 000 J

This is transferred every second, so the input power is P = E ÷ t = 12 000 ÷ 1 = 12 000 W

Electrical power = 40% of 12 000 W = 0.40 × 12 000 = 4800 W

Why this scores
Marks for ½mv2, turning energy per second into power, taking 40% and the final 4800 W. The examiner report lists the usual slips: not squaring the speed, taking 60% instead of 40%, and giving the unit as J instead of W.
0625/43/O/N/21 Q2(a) · October/November 2021 · [4 marks]

Source material: The load of 12 kg is raised to a height of 1.7 m above the ground. … The time taken for the load to fall to the ground is 1200 seconds. The load falls at constant speed.

Calculate the rate of transfer of gravitational potential energy as the load falls to the ground.

Step 1: energy transferred
ΔEp = mgΔh = 12 × 10 × 1.7 = 204 J (this paper takes g = 10 N/kg)

Step 2: rate of transfer (power)
P = ΔE ÷ t = 204 ÷ 1200
P = 0.17 W

Why this scores
Marks build up through mgh, the value of the energy (204 J), dividing by 1200 s and the final 0.17 W. “Rate of transfer” means energy per second, so the unit is W (J/s is also accepted); the examiner report says answers in J or m/s lost the final mark.
0625/42/O/N/23 Q1(c) · October/November 2023 · [4 marks]

The car decelerates from 13.0 m / s to 0 m / s at a constant deceleration. The mass of the car is 1350 kg. The car travels 13 m in 2.0 s as it decelerates. Show that the work done by the car as it decelerates is approximately 1.1 × 105 J.

Deceleration: a = Δv ÷ t = (13.0 − 0) ÷ 2.0 = 6.5 m/s2

Braking force: F = ma = 1350 × 6.5 = 8775 N

Work done: W = Fd = 8775 × 13 = 114 075 J ≈ 1.1 × 105 J

Check: the work done equals the kinetic energy lost, ½mv2 = ½ × 1350 × 13.02 = 114 075 J. ✓

Why this scores
One mark each for W = Fd, F = ma, the force 8775 N and the unrounded work 114 075 J. In a “show that” question the examiner report rewards equations written in symbols and no early rounding; using g as the acceleration was a common error. The kinetic-energy route also scores, but only if you state that work done = change in kinetic energy.

1.7.3 Energy Resources

These questions test how each energy resource is used to generate electricity, where its energy originally comes from (usually the Sun, through nuclear fusion), and its advantages and disadvantages; examiners reward a specific point followed by its consequence, not vague words such as “natural” or “eco-friendly”.

What examiners keep seeing
Examiners report that many candidates call a resource renewable because “it is natural” or “it comes from the Sun”, which earns nothing: say it is replaced and will not run out. When asked for resources that do not come from the Sun, wave, hydroelectric, biomass and fossil fuels are common wrong answers: the right ones are geothermal, tidal and nuclear.
0625/42/O/N/24 Q3(c) · October/November 2024 · [1 mark]

Tidal energy and wind energy are both renewable energy resources. Suggest one reason why tidal energy is a more useful energy resource than wind energy. Ignore the costs of construction and maintenance.

Tides are predictable and reliable: they come in and go out at known times every day, whereas the wind can drop or stop without warning, so the output of a wind turbine cannot be relied on.

Why this scores
One mark for the idea that tides are regular, predictable or reliable (or the reverse point about wind). Comparing the two directly, as here, makes the reason clear.
0625/41/M/J/21 Q9(c) · May/June 2021 · [2 marks]

Nuclear fusion in the Sun is the source of most but not all of the resources that are used to generate electrical energy on Earth. State two resources for which nuclear fusion in the Sun is not the source.

1. Geothermal energy

2. Tidal energy

Why this scores
One mark for each correct resource; nuclear (fission) energy would also be accepted. The examiner report notes that wave energy was a common wrong answer: waves are caused by winds, which come from the Sun heating the Earth. Do not write “tidal wave”, which means a tsunami.
0625/42/O/N/21 Q4(a) · October/November 2021 · [2 marks]

A power station uses wind energy to generate electricity. State and explain whether this method of generating electricity is renewable.

Statement: renewable.

Explanation: the wind is constantly replaced (the Sun keeps heating the atmosphere), so using it to turn turbines does not use it up and it will not run out.

Why this scores
One mark for “renewable” and one for the idea that the resource is replaced or will not run out. The examiner report says answers such as “it is natural”, “it comes from the Sun” or just repeating “renewable” did not earn the explanation mark.
0625/43/O/N/25 Q3(b) · October/November 2025 · [2 marks]

Describe how useful energy may be obtained from geothermal resources.

Cold water is pumped deep underground, where it is heated by hot rocks and turns into steam.

The steam returns to the surface and turns a turbine that drives a generator to produce electricity (or it is used directly to heat buildings).

Why this scores
One mark for water being heated by hot rocks underground and one for how the steam is used (turning a turbine and generator, or heating buildings). Name the hot rocks: “heat from the Earth” on its own is too vague.
0625/41/O/N/23 Q5(a) · October/November 2023 · [3 marks]

Source material: Many methods of generating electrical power involve the use of water.

Describe one method of generating electrical power from energy stored in water.

Hydroelectric power: water is held behind a dam in a high reservoir, where it has a store of gravitational potential energy.

When the water is released it flows downhill through pipes, and its gravitational potential energy is transferred to kinetic energy.

The fast-moving water turns a turbine, and the turbine drives a generator, which generates electricity.

Why this scores
The mark scheme gives one mark each (up to three) for naming the method, how the water’s energy is released, the transfer between energy stores, the water turning a turbine, and the turbine driving a generator. This answer makes five creditworthy points, so it is safe even if one is judged too brief.
0625/42/M/J/20 Q3(b) · May/June 2020 · [4 marks]

State and explain one advantage and one disadvantage of heating the water in a solar panel compared with heating the water in a coal-burning boiler.

Advantage: a solar panel burns no fuel.

Explanation: no carbon dioxide (a greenhouse gas) or other polluting gases are released, so it does not add to global warming or air pollution, unlike burning coal.

Disadvantage: it does not work at night and heats much less water on cloudy days.

Explanation: the panel only works when sunlight falls on it, so hot water may not be available when it is needed, whereas a coal boiler can be used at any time.

Why this scores
One mark for each valid point and one for an explanation that follows from it. Each explanation must give the consequence, compared with the coal boiler; stating “renewable” on its own, without saying why that matters, is too vague to earn the explanation mark.

1.8 Pressure

These questions test p = F/A and the liquid-pressure equation Δp = ρgΔh; examiners reward the equation written first, weight (not mass) used as the force, and careful handling of atmospheric pressure.

What examiners keep seeing
Mixing up total pressure and pressure due to the liquid: to find a depth you must subtract atmospheric pressure first (the examiner report says many candidates added it instead). Using mass instead of weight in p = F/A is the other common slip.
0625/43/M/J/23 Q2(a)(i) · May/June 2023 · [1 mark]

Define pressure.

Pressure is the force per unit area (p = F ÷ A).

Why this scores
One mark for force per unit area. The examiner report says “force on a certain area” or “force acting on an object” does not score: say unit area, or write the equation.
0625/43/M/J/23 Q2(a)(ii) · May/June 2023 · [2 marks]

Describe how pressure in a liquid varies with its depth and with its density.

Depth: the pressure increases as the depth increases (it is proportional to depth).

Density: at the same depth, a denser liquid gives a greater pressure (Δp = ρgΔh).

Why this scores
One mark for each correct variation. The examiner report says weaker answers thought pressure decreases as density increases; learning Δp = ρgΔh avoids this.
0625/41/M/J/21 Q3(b)(ii) · May/June 2021 · [2 marks]

Source material: Fig. 3.1 shows a device that uses liquid pressure to lift heavy boxes. … The boxes are lifted by pumping oil into the cylinder.

As the boxes are lifted, the depth of the oil increases. Explain why the pump must exert an increasing pressure on the oil as the depth of the oil increases.

Pressure in a liquid increases with depth (Δp = ρgΔh), so the deeper column of oil adds extra pressure at the bottom, where the pump pushes the oil in.

The pump must overcome this extra pressure from the weight of the oil to keep the upward force on the piston constant and keep lifting the boxes.

Why this scores
One mark for linking the greater depth of oil to a greater pressure, one for the pump having to overcome it to keep the force constant. The examiner report says many candidates gave the first point but did not explain why the pump has to push harder.
0625/43/M/J/20 Q2(b)(ii) · May/June 2020 · [2 marks]

Source material: Fig. 2.1 shows a submarine. The submarine is fully submerged in the sea. … The atmospheric pressure is 100 kPa and the total pressure on the top surface of the submarine is 500 kPa.

A hatch (an opening door) on the top surface of the submarine has an area of 0.62 m2. Calculate the downward force on the hatch due to the total pressure on the top surface of the submarine.

p = F ÷ A, so F = pA
F = 500 × 103 × 0.62
F = 3.1 × 105 N

Why this scores
One mark for F = pA with the numbers in, one for 3.1 × 105 N. The question asks for the total pressure, so use 500 kPa (not 400 kPa) and convert kPa to Pa.
0625/43/M/J/21 Q2(b) · May/June 2021 · [3 marks]

A diver dives to a depth below the surface of the sea where the total pressure is 3.0 × 105 Pa. The atmospheric pressure is 1.0 × 105 Pa. The density of the sea water is 1030 kg / m3. Calculate the depth of the diver below the surface of the sea.

Pressure due to the water = 3.0 × 105 − 1.0 × 105 = 2.0 × 105 Pa
Δp = ρgΔh, so Δh = Δp ÷ (ρg)
Δh = 2.0 × 105 ÷ (1030 × 10) (this paper takes g = 10 N/kg)
depth = 19 m

Why this scores
One mark for p = ρgh, one for subtracting atmospheric pressure before rearranging, one for 19 m. The examiner report says the most common mistake was adding the atmospheric pressure instead of subtracting it.
0625/42/M/J/23 Q3(b) · May/June 2023 · [4 marks]

Source material: Fig. 3.2 shows a side view of the pond with a layer of ice floating freely on the water.

The surface area of the pond is 5.0 m2. The mass of the ice is 690 kg. The density of water is 1000 kg / m3. Point X is 0.45 m below the ice. Calculate the pressure at point X due to the ice and the water.

Weight of ice = mg = 690 × 9.8 = 6762 N
Pressure due to ice = F ÷ A = 6762 ÷ 5.0 = 1352 Pa
Pressure due to water = ρgh = 1000 × 9.8 × 0.45 = 4410 Pa
Total = 1352 + 4410 = 5762 Pa
pressure at X = 5.8 × 103 Pa

Marks for the pressure due to the water, the weight of the ice, adding the two pressures, and 5.8 × 103 Pa. The examiner report says weaker answers found only the water pressure, subtracted the two, or used the mass of the ice instead of its weight.

2.1 Kinetic Particle Model of Matter

These questions test the particle model: arrangement and motion in solids, liquids and gases, Brownian motion, how gas particles cause pressure, absolute zero and pV = constant; examiners reward answers that mention particles (or molecules) explicitly and use precise phrases such as “more frequent collisions with the walls”.

What examiners keep seeing
“More collisions” does not score: examiners want collisions that are more frequent and (for a temperature rise) harder, with a larger change in momentum. The examiner reports also note that many candidates write a learnt answer about heating a gas when the question asks something else, such as simply how a gas exerts pressure.
0625/42/M/J/23 Q4(b)(ii) · May/June 2023 · [2 marks]

Explain what is meant by the term absolute zero. Refer to particles in your answer.

Absolute zero (0 K, −273 °C) is the lowest possible temperature.

At absolute zero the particles have the least possible kinetic energy, so they cannot be slowed down any further and nothing can be cooled below this temperature.

Why this scores
One mark for the particles having the least kinetic energy and one for “lowest possible temperature”. The examiner report says stronger answers gave both; a common wrong answer was that absolute zero is the temperature at which water freezes.
0625/43/M/J/23 Q3(a)(ii) · May/June 2023 · [3 marks]

Describe, in terms of the motion and arrangement of particles, the structures of solids and gases.

Solids: the particles are packed closely together in a regular pattern, in fixed positions. They vibrate about these fixed positions.

Gases: the particles are far apart and randomly arranged. They move quickly and freely in random directions.

Why this scores
Any three of the mark-scheme points score: solid particles vibrate, are in fixed and close positions; gas particles move freely and quickly, are random and widely separated. The examiner report says weaker answers did not mention particles at all; cover both motion and arrangement for each state.
0625/42/O/N/25 Q4(a)(i) · October/November 2025 · [3 marks]

Source material: Fig. 4.1 shows gas trapped in a cylinder by a piston. … The volume of gas is 240 cm3. The piston is pushed to the left and is held in its new position.

The pressure of the gas increases from 1.0 × 105 Pa to 1.4 × 105 Pa. The temperature of the gas remains constant. Calculate the volume of the gas when the piston is in its new position.

p1V1 = p2V2 (fixed mass of gas at constant temperature)
1.0 × 105 × 240 = 1.4 × 105 × V2
V2 = (1.0 × 105 × 240) ÷ (1.4 × 105) = 171 cm3 = 170 cm3

Why this scores
One mark for pV = constant with the values substituted, one for the rearrangement and one for 170 cm3. Quick check: the pressure went up, so the volume must go down. Keeping the volume in cm3 is fine because both sides use the same units.
0625/43/M/J/22 Q4(a)(ii) · May/June 2022 · [4 marks]

Source material: Fig. 4.1 shows apparatus used to observe the motion of smoke particles (Brownian motion). … The smoke particles are seen as bright specks of light when looking through the microscope.

Explain, in terms of forces and the motion of air molecules, the cause of the motion of the smoke particles.

The air molecules are very small and light compared with the smoke particles, and they move very fast in random directions.

Each smoke particle is bombarded by air molecules from all sides.

At any instant, more molecules hit one side of a smoke particle than the other, so the collisions exert an unbalanced (resultant) force on it and it moves.

The direction of this resultant force keeps changing at random, so the smoke particle moves in a jerky, random path.

Why this scores
Four marks for: air molecules bombard the smoke particles; they are small (low mass); they move very fast; they move randomly; the collisions give an unbalanced force. The examiner report warns against mixing up the two: keep “air molecules” for the invisible particles doing the hitting and “smoke particles” for the specks you can see.
0625/42/F/M/21 Q4(a) · February/March 2021 · [4 marks]

In terms of the momentum of molecules, explain how a gas exerts pressure on the walls of its container.

The gas molecules move randomly at high speed and keep colliding with the walls of the container.

When a molecule hits a wall and bounces back, its velocity reverses, so its momentum (mv) changes.

Force is the rate of change of momentum, so each collision means the wall exerts a force on the molecule, and the molecule exerts an equal and opposite force on the wall.

Many collisions every second add up to a total force on the wall; pressure is this total force divided by the area of the wall (p = F/A).

Why this scores
One mark each for: molecules hit the walls; their momentum changes; force = rate of change of momentum; pressure = force ÷ area. The examiner report says many candidates explained why pressure rises when a gas is heated instead, a learnt answer to a different question that scores little here.
0625/43/O/N/23 Q4(b) · October/November 2023 · [4 marks]

The temperature of a fixed mass of gas at constant volume changes from 300 K to 400 K. State and explain, in terms of particles, the effect on the pressure of the gas.

Statement: the pressure of the gas increases.

Explanation: at the higher temperature the gas particles have more kinetic energy, so they move faster.

The particles therefore collide with the walls more frequently, and each collision has a larger change in momentum, so it exerts a larger force on the wall.

A larger total force acts on the same area of wall, so the pressure (force ÷ area) increases.

Why this scores
One mark for “increases”, one for faster particles (more kinetic energy) and two from: more frequent collisions, harder collisions (larger force or momentum change), pressure = force ÷ area. The examiner report says “more collisions” on its own was not credited: it must be more frequent collisions with the walls.

2.2 Thermal Properties and Temperature

These questions test particle explanations of expansion and evaporation, the definitions of specific heat capacity and latent heat, and multi-stage energy calculations; examiners reward precise particle language and an equation written before the numbers.

What examiners keep seeing
In multi-stage calculations, candidates leave out the change-of-state energy or use one temperature change for the whole process: split it into stages and add them. In definitions, write “by 1 °C”, not “to 1 °C”, and include “per kg”.
0625/42/F/M/24 Q4(a) · February/March 2024 · [2 marks]

Define specific heat capacity.

The energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C (the energy transferred per unit mass per unit rise in temperature).

Why this scores
Both marks need the energy to be per unit mass (1 kg) and per unit temperature change (1 °C); leaving out the mass describes thermal capacity and scores only one mark. The examiner report notes answers that wrote “to 1 °C” instead of “by 1 °C”.
0625/41/M/J/21 Q5(b) · May/June 2021 · [2 marks]

Explain, in terms of molecules, why a liquid expands more than a solid when heated.

The attractive forces between the molecules of a liquid are weaker than the forces between the molecules of a solid.

So for the same energy supplied, less work has to be done to pull the liquid molecules apart, and they move further apart than the molecules in the solid.

Why this scores
One mark for weaker intermolecular forces in the liquid, one for linking this to a greater separation for the same energy input. The examiner report says arguments based only on liquids already having larger spacings were less successful, and never say the molecules themselves get bigger.
0625/42/M/J/24 Q4(a) · May/June 2024 · [2 marks]

State two ways that evaporation differs from boiling.

1  Evaporation happens only at the surface of the liquid; boiling happens throughout the liquid, with bubbles of vapour forming inside it.

2  Evaporation happens at any temperature; boiling happens only at one temperature, the boiling point.

Why this scores
One mark per difference. The examiner report warns that “only at the surface” and “no bubbles” are the same idea, so giving both as your two differences scores only one mark: the second point should be about temperature.
0625/43/O/N/22 Q5(a) · October/November 2022 · [4 marks]

Three identical dishes, A, B and C, contain an equal volume of water. Dish A is outside in sunlight and experiences no wind during the day. Dish B is outside in sunlight and experiences a strong wind during the day. Dish C is in a dark room. Water evaporates from each dish. After 12 hours, a student measures the volume of water in each dish. Dish C contains the largest volume of water and dish B contains the smallest volume of water. Explain, in terms of particles, why the three dishes have different volumes of water.

Evaporation happens when the most energetic (fastest) water molecules at the surface escape from the liquid.

In dishes A and B, energy from the Sun is absorbed by the water molecules, so their kinetic energy increases and more of them have enough energy to escape. Dish C gets no sunlight, so it has the lowest rate of evaporation and keeps the most water.

In dish B the wind carries molecules away once they have left the surface, so they cannot fall back into the water.

So dish B has the highest rate of evaporation and loses the most water.

Why this scores
Four marks: Sun’s energy absorbed by the molecules (more kinetic energy); faster molecules escape from the surface; the wind removes molecules after they leave so they do not re-enter; a correct comparison of evaporation rates. The examiner report says the wind does not give molecules energy or “blow off the top layer”, and “no water evaporates from C” is wrong: its rate is just lower.
0625/41/O/N/24 Q4(a) · October/November 2024 · [4 marks]

Describe an experiment to determine the specific heat capacity of aluminium. You may draw a diagram. Include in your answer: • the measurements made • any equations needed.

Take an aluminium block with two holes. Put an electric heater in one hole and a thermometer in the other, and wrap the block in insulation.

Measure the mass m of the block on a balance and its starting temperature. Switch on the heater for a measured time t (stopwatch), recording the heater power P (or the voltmeter reading V and ammeter reading I). Record the highest temperature reached and work out the temperature rise Δθ.

Energy supplied: E = Pt (or E = IVt)
Specific heat capacity: c = E ÷ (mΔθ)

Why this scores
Marks go for a workable way of heating the aluminium, a matching way of finding the energy supplied (E = Pt or E = IVt), at least one correct measurement (mass, temperature change or time) and the equation c = E/(mΔθ). Make sure the energy equation fits your method: an electric heater pairs with E = Pt.
0625/43/M/J/22 Q5(b)(ii) · May/June 2022 · [5 marks]

Source material: A bowl contains 500 cm3 of water at a temperature of 5.0 °C. … When the bowl is removed from the freezer, it contains ice at a temperature of –18.0 °C. The density of water is 1000 kg / m3.

The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J / (kg °C). The specific heat capacity of ice is 2100 J / (kg °C). The specific latent heat of fusion of water is 3.3 × 105 J / kg. Calculate the energy given out as the water cools from 5.0 °C to ice at –18.0 °C.

Mass of water: m = ρV = 1000 × 500 × 10−6 = 0.50 kg

Water cooling 5.0 °C → 0 °C: E1 = mcΔθ = 0.50 × 4200 × 5.0 = 10 500 J
Water freezing at 0 °C: E2 = mL = 0.50 × 3.3 × 105 = 165 000 J
Ice cooling 0 °C → −18.0 °C: E3 = mcΔθ = 0.50 × 2100 × 18.0 = 18 900 J

Total energy given out = 10 500 + 165 000 + 18 900 = 194 400 J = 1.9 × 105 J

Why this scores
One mark each for E = mcΔθ, E = mL, using 4200 with Δθ = 5, using 2100 with Δθ = 18, and the final answer. The examiner report says weaker answers left out the freezing stage, subtracted it instead of adding, or used one Δθ of 23 °C for the whole change.

2.3 Transfer of Thermal Energy

These questions test whether you can name the right transfer process and explain it with particles, electrons, density changes or infrared; examiners reward naming the process first, then the mechanism.

What examiners keep seeing
Answers drift into the wrong mechanism: lattice vibrations when the question asks about electrons, “heat rises” instead of “less dense air rises”, or a second process added when only the main one is asked for.
0625/42/O/N/24 Q5(c) · October/November 2024 · [1 mark]

Source material: On a sunny day, the temperatures of a black tarmac road and the air above the road increase.

State the main method of thermal energy transfer from the air immediately above the road to the rest of the air.

Convection.

Why this scores
One mark for convection. Conduction is the answer for the layer of air touching the tarmac (the previous part); after that it is the moving, less dense warm air that carries the energy upwards.
0625/43/O/N/25 Q5(d) · October/November 2025 · [2 marks]

The Earth’s surface absorbs incoming radiation from the Sun and also emits thermal radiation from its surface. Over the past 50 years, scientists estimate that the average temperature of the Earth’s surface has increased by approximately 0.75 °C. Explain what is causing this average temperature rise.

The amount of greenhouse gases, such as carbon dioxide and methane, in the atmosphere has increased (mainly from burning fossil fuels).

These gases absorb infrared emitted by the Earth’s surface and re-emit some of it back towards the surface, so less radiation escapes into space. The Earth now absorbs energy faster than it loses it, so its average temperature rises.

Why this scores
One mark for more greenhouse gases (carbon dioxide, methane or water vapour) in the atmosphere, one for less radiation escaping or more being re-emitted back to the surface. Vague answers such as “pollution” do not name the cause.
0625/43/M/J/24 Q5(a) · May/June 2024 · [3 marks]

Source material: Fig. 5.1 shows two containers, each filled with hot water. The outer surface of the metal container is hot.

Explain how electrons transfer thermal energy through the metal of the container.

Metals contain free (delocalised) electrons.

Electrons next to the hot water gain kinetic energy and move quickly through the metal lattice.

They collide with ions far away from the hot water, transferring energy to them, so energy reaches the outer surface.

Why this scores
Any three of: free/delocalised electrons; they gain energy at the hot side; they move through the metal; they collide with distant ions or atoms and pass on energy. The examiner report says lattice vibrations earn nothing here, and weaker answers had the electrons “vibrating” instead of moving through the metal.
0625/41/M/J/22 Q6(c)(ii) · May/June 2022 · [3 marks]

Source material: Fig. 6.1 shows a road next to the sea. The air above the road is heated by the warm road.

Sound waves from a television are diffracted through doorways. Light waves from a television are not diffracted through doorways. Suggest why light waves and sound waves behave differently in this situation.

Source material: Fig. 6.1 shows part of an optical fibre used in high-speed broadband communication.

0625/41/O/N/20 Q9(b) · October/November 2020 · [3 marks]

Describe and explain the action of optical fibres in communication technology. You may draw a diagram in your answer.

The signal (e.g. telephone or internet data) is coded as pulses of light or infrared, which are sent into one end of a thin glass fibre.

Why this scores

0625/41/O/N/22 Q6(b)(ii) · October/November 2022 · [4 marks]

  • a secondary coil of insulated copper wire with fewer turns than the primary, connected to the output.
Why this scores
One mark each for a (soft-)iron core, copper coils, and fewer turns on the secondary than the primary. The examiner report says you must label which coil is primary and which is secondary to get the last mark.
0625/41/M/J/23 Q8(a) · May/June 2023 · [4 marks]

Source material: A transformer in the town has a soft-iron core.

Explain the principle of operation of a simple iron-cored transformer.

An alternating current in the primary coil produces a magnetic field that keeps changing in size and direction.

The soft-iron core concentrates this changing field and carries it through the secondary coil.

So the secondary coil sits in a changing magnetic field (its turns are cut by the changing field lines).

This induces an alternating e.m.f. across the secondary coil.

Why this scores
Four marks, one per step: a.c. in the primary, changing magnetic field, the core transferring the field, and an e.m.f. induced in the secondary. The examiner report says “current in the primary” without “alternating” lost the first mark, and describing step-up and step-down did not answer the question.
0625/43/O/N/20 Q9(b) · October/November 2020 · [5 marks]

A computer contains a transformer. The input voltage to the transformer is 240 V. The output voltage from the transformer is 20 V and the output current is 2.3 A. The efficiency of the transformer is 90%. Calculate the input current to the transformer.

Output power: P = IV = 2.3 × 20 = 46 W
Efficiency = useful power output ÷ total power input, so input power = 46 ÷ 0.90 = 51 W
Input current: I = P ÷ V = 51 ÷ 240 = 0.21 A

Why this scores
Five marks: the efficiency equation, P = IV, 46 W output, 51 W input and 0.21 A. The examiner report says weaker candidates mixed up input and output power or treated the transformer as 100% efficient; writing the efficiency equation first protects those marks.

5.1 The Nuclear Model of the Atom

These questions test the structure of the atom, the alpha-scattering evidence for the nucleus, isotopes and how fission and fusion work; examiners reward precise words such as “nucleus” (not “atom”) and links between each observation and its conclusion.

What examiners keep seeing
Writing “atom” or “element” when the mark needs “nucleus”: examiners only credit fission as a nucleus splitting, and alpha-scattering conclusions must be about the nucleus, not about atoms or electrons.
0625/42/F/M/24 Q9(a)(i) · February/March 2024 · [1 mark]

Source material: An experiment directs alpha particles at a very thin sheet of gold foil.

Most of the alpha particles pass through the thin foil in a straight line. State the conclusion about atoms from this observation.

Most of an atom is empty space.

Why this scores
One mark for “most of the atom is empty space”. The examiner report notes that “lots of empty space between atoms” earns nothing: the space is inside each atom.
0625/43/O/N/22 Q10(b)(i) · October/November 2022 · [2 marks]

Describe what is meant by an isotope.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons in the nucleus (so a different nucleon number).

Why this scores
One mark for the same number of protons and one for a different number of neutrons. Do not talk about gaining or losing electrons: that describes ions, which the examiners saw confused with isotopes.
0625/42/F/M/23 Q9(a)(i) · February/March 2023 · [3 marks]

Source material: A nuclear power station has a reactor where controlled nuclear fission of uranium‑235 takes place.

Explain what is meant by nuclear fission.

A neutron is absorbed by a large, unstable nucleus such as uranium-235.

The nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei, and two or three neutrons are also released.

A large amount of energy is released (as kinetic energy of the products).

Why this scores
One mark each for the large unstable nucleus / neutron hitting it, the nucleus splitting into smaller nuclei, and the large energy release. The examiner report stresses that it must be a nucleus that splits, not an atom, isotope or element.
0625/41/M/J/22 Q9(b)(i) · May/June 2022 · [3 marks]

Source material: In a nuclear fusion reactor, a nucleus of hydrogen-2 fuses with a nucleus of hydrogen-3 at an extremely high temperature.

Explain why an extremely high temperature is needed when forcing these two nuclei together.

Both nuclei are positively charged, so they repel each other strongly.

To fuse, they must get extremely close together despite this repulsion.

At an extremely high temperature the nuclei have very high kinetic energy (move very fast), enough to overcome the repulsion and collide.

Why this scores
Marks for high temperature giving high kinetic energy, the nuclei repelling, and the reason (both positive / must come very close). Many candidates wrongly said the energy was needed to “split” the hydrogen nuclei.
0625/42/F/M/24 Q9(a)(ii) · February/March 2024 · [4 marks]

Source material: An experiment directs alpha particles at a very thin sheet of gold foil.

Some of the alpha particles are deflected through angles less than 90° and a few are deflected through 180°. State and explain two conclusions about the nuclei of atoms from this observation.

Conclusion 1: the nucleus is positively charged.
Explanation: alpha particles are positively charged, so they are deflected by a repulsive force when they pass close to the nucleus.

Conclusion 2: the nucleus is very small.
Explanation: only a very few alpha particles pass close enough to a nucleus to be deflected through large angles; most miss it.

Why this scores
Two marks for two conclusions about the nucleus and two for the matching explanations. Examiners penalised “like charges repel” without stating that alpha particles are positive, and answers about electrons deflecting the particles.
0625/42/O/N/21 Q11(a) · October/November 2021 · [4 marks]

Describe the composition and structure of a neutral atom of beryllium-8, which has a proton number of 4 and a nucleon number of 8.

The atom has a very small nucleus at its centre, with electrons orbiting around it.

The nucleus contains 4 protons and 8 − 4 = 4 neutrons.

The atom is neutral, so it has 4 electrons, the same as the number of protons.

Why this scores
One mark for the structure (small nucleus with orbiting electrons), one for protons and neutrons in the nucleus, and one each for 4 electrons and 4 neutrons. Weaker answers gave the numbers but forgot the structure that the question also asks for.

5.2.1 Radioactive Emissions and Decay

These questions test background radiation, the properties of alpha, beta and gamma, how they behave in fields, decay equations and safe handling; examiners reward answers that link each property to the situation in the question.

What examiners keep seeing
Stating a fact without applying it, e.g. “alpha is stopped by paper” when the question asks about a plastic box, or naming a safety precaution without saying how it reduces the radiation received.
0625/42/M/J/25 Q9(a)(i) · May/June 2025 · [1 mark]

State what is meant by background radiation.

Radiation that is always present in the environment, coming from natural sources such as radon gas, rocks and cosmic rays.

Why this scores
One mark for radiation that is always present / from natural sources. Examiners saw weaker candidates confuse it with half-life or with cosmic microwave background radiation.
0625/43/M/J/25 Q11(c) · May/June 2025 · [2 marks]

A different element decays by the emission of an alpha particle (α-particle). Give two reasons why α-particles are more strongly ionising than β-particles.

  1. An α-particle has a greater charge (+2) than a β-particle (−1).
  2. α-particles have greater kinetic energy than β-particles.
Why this scores
One mark per comparison. The examiner report says “energy” instead of kinetic energy, or “charge” without a comparison, was not enough.
0625/42/M/J/24 Q9(a)(i) · May/June 2024 · [2 marks]

Source material: Radioactive isotopes that emit ionising radiation are used in hospitals.

State and explain two safety precautions necessary for the use of these isotopes in medical procedures.

1 Staff stand behind lead or concrete shielding during the procedure, because the shielding absorbs the radiation before it reaches them.

2 Staff keep the time spent near the patient or source as short as possible, so less radiation is absorbed by their bodies.

Why this scores
Each mark needs a precaution AND its explanation. The examiner report notes that just saying it would “harm humans”, or describing school-laboratory rules, earned nothing.
0625/42/F/M/21 Q10(b) · February/March 2021 · [3 marks]

A nucleus of strontium-90 consists of 38 protons and 52 neutrons. Strontium-90 is radioactive and decays by β-emission to an isotope of yttrium. The symbol for strontium is Sr and the symbol for yttrium is Y. Write down the nuclide equation of this decay.

9038Sr → 9039Y + 0−1β

Check: top numbers 90 = 90 + 0; bottom numbers 38 = 39 + (−1).

Why this scores
One mark for nucleon number 90 on both sides, one for Sr 38 and Y 39, one for the β-particle written as 0 over −1. Common errors were using the neutron number (52) as the nucleon number and leaving the numbers off the β.
0625/41/O/N/20 Q7(c) · October/November 2020 · [3 marks]

A beam consists of α-particles, β-particles and γ-rays. Explain how a uniform magnetic field may be used to separate the α-particles, the β-particles and the γ-rays.

The beam is passed through the magnetic field at right angles to the field lines.

α- and β-particles are charged, so they are deflected. They have opposite charges, so they bend in opposite directions; the α-particles are deflected less because their mass is much greater.

γ-rays are uncharged, so they are not deflected and carry straight on.

Why this scores
Marks for the charged parts of the beam deflecting, α and β deflecting in opposite directions (or α deflecting less), and γ not deflecting. Explaining in terms of penetration instead of charge in a field gets no credit.
0625/42/M/J/21 Q11(b) · May/June 2021 · [3 marks]

The substance emits α-particles and γ-rays. The student suggests that it is safe to store the substance in a plastic container of thickness 2 mm. State and explain whether the student’s suggestion is correct.

Statement: the suggestion is not correct.

Explanation: 2 mm of plastic will absorb the α-particles, but γ-rays are highly penetrating and pass straight through it. The container should be lined with thick lead to reduce the γ-radiation escaping.

Why this scores
One mark for “incorrect”, one for γ passing through the plastic, one for extra detail (plastic stops α / lead needed). Link every point to the 2 mm plastic box, as the examiner report advises.

5.2.3 Half-life

These questions test the definition of half-life, calculations over whole numbers of half-lives (with background correction) and choosing an isotope by its half-life; examiners reward clear step-by-step working.

What examiners keep seeing
Forgetting to subtract the background count before halving, and to add it back on at the end; also defining half-life as the time for the “mass” or “source” to halve instead of the number of nuclei or the activity.
0625/43/M/J/25 Q11(a) · May/June 2025 · [1 mark]

Define the half-life of a radioactive source.

The time taken for half of the nuclei in a sample of the isotope to decay.

Why this scores
One mark for half the nuclei decaying (or the activity halving). “The time for the mass to halve” or “the source to halve” was judged insufficient.
0625/42/F/M/21 Q10(c) · February/March 2021 · [2 marks]

The half-life of radon-220 is 56 s. A sample of radon-220 is in a container. After 112 s the mass of radon-220 is 9.2 mg. Calculate the mass of the original sample.

number of half-lives = 112 ÷ 56 = 2

original mass = 9.2 × 2 × 2 = 37 mg

Why this scores
One mark for 2 half-lives, one for 37 mg. The common wrong answer was 18.4 mg (multiplying by 2 only); there is no need to convert to grams.
0625/42/O/N/20 Q9(b)(i) · October/November 2020 · [2 marks]

Source material: Radioactive tracers emitting γ-rays can be used in medicine. The half-life of the source of these γ-rays is 6 hours.

Explain why a source of γ-rays used in this way should not have a half-life shorter or longer than about 6 hours.

If the half-life were much shorter, the activity would fall too much before the tracer had time to spread round the body and the measurements were taken.

If it were much longer, the tracer would stay radioactive inside the patient for a long time, increasing the dose and the risk of cell damage.

Why this scores
One mark for each end: too short gives too little time for the investigation, too long leaves the patient exposed for a long time. Examiners saw answers about therapy rather than tracing.
0625/42/F/M/24 Q9(b)(ii) · February/March 2024 · [3 marks]

Source material: A source contains a radioactive isotope of strontium. This isotope decays by emission of β-particles. The half-life of this isotope is 29 years.

The initial mass of this isotope of strontium in the source is 25 µg. Calculate the mass of the strontium isotope that decays in 87 years.

number of half-lives = 87 ÷ 29 = 3

mass remaining = 25 ÷ 23 = 25 ÷ 8 = 3.1 µg

mass decayed = 25 − 3.1 = 22 µg

Why this scores
Marks for 3 half-lives, for 1/8 remaining (3.125 µg) and for the final 22 µg. Read the question: it asks for the mass that has decayed, so subtract from 25.
0625/43/O/N/23 Q9(a)(ii) · October/November 2023 · [3 marks]

Source material: For each application of radioactive isotopes, state and explain which type of radioactive emission is suitable and suggest an appropriate half-life for the isotope.

measuring the thickness of aluminium strips produced in a factory

Type of emission: beta.

Explanation: some β-particles pass through the thin aluminium and some are absorbed, and the amount absorbed depends on the thickness, so the count rate changes when the thickness changes. (α would all be stopped; γ would nearly all pass through.)

Half-life: several years (e.g. 30 years), so the count rate stays steady and the source does not need replacing often.

Why this scores
One mark each for beta, absorption depending on thickness, and a half-life in years. The examiner report says “long” or “big” is not enough, and many wrongly gave seconds or days.
0625/42/M/J/23 Q9(c) · May/June 2023 · [4 marks]

A radiation detector used in a laboratory detects a background count rate of 30 counts / min. A radioactive source is placed in front of the radiation detector. The initial reading on the detector is 550 counts / min. The half-life of the source is 25 minutes. Calculate the expected reading on the detector after 75 minutes.

count rate from the source = 550 − 30 = 520 counts/min

number of half-lives = 75 ÷ 25 = 3

source count rate after 75 min = 520 ÷ 23 = 520 ÷ 8 = 65 counts/min

reading on detector = 65 + 30 = 95 counts/min

Why this scores
Four marks: subtract background (520), 3 half-lives, 520 ÷ 8 = 65, then add the background back to get 95. Forgetting either the subtraction or the adding back was the most common mistake.

6.1 Earth and the Solar System

These questions test what the Solar System contains, the shape of orbits, how it formed and the orbital speed equation; examiners reward correct terms (elliptical, accretion disc) and careful unit conversion.

What examiners keep seeing
Leaving the orbital period in days or hours, or using the radius instead of the circumference (2πr), in v = 2πr/T; also writing “circular” when the syllabus answer is “elliptical”.
0625/42/F/M/23 Q10(b) · February/March 2023 · [1 mark]

State the shape of the orbits of the planets.

Elliptical (nearly circular ellipses).

Why this scores
One mark for elliptical. The examiner report says “circular” alone was not accepted.
0625/42/M/J/24 Q10(a) · May/June 2024 · [2 marks]

The Solar System includes the Sun and planets. State two other types of natural object that orbit the Sun.

  1. Comets
  2. Dwarf planets (minor planets) such as Pluto, or asteroids
Why this scores
One mark each for two of: minor/dwarf planets, comets, asteroids. Moons orbit planets, not the Sun directly, so avoid them.
0625/42/F/M/23 Q10(a) · February/March 2023 · [3 marks]

The time taken for Mars to orbit the Sun is 690 Earth days. The average orbital radius of Mars is 2.28 × 108 km. An Earth day is 24 h. Calculate the average orbital speed of Mars in km / s.

T = 690 × 24 × 60 × 60 = 5.96 × 107 s

v = 2πr ÷ T = (2 × π × 2.28 × 108) ÷ 5.96 × 107

v = 24 km/s

Why this scores
Marks for the equation, for converting 690 days into seconds, and for 24 km/s. Keep r in km because the answer is asked for in km/s; common errors were using r instead of 2πr and converting to hours only.
0625/41/M/J/25 Q9(c) · May/June 2025 · [3 marks]

State and explain the difference between the orbital speed of Jupiter and the orbital speed of the Earth.

Statement: Jupiter has a lower orbital speed than the Earth.

Explanation: Jupiter is much further from the Sun, and the Sun’s gravitational field strength decreases with distance, so a weaker gravitational pull acts on Jupiter and it orbits more slowly.

Why this scores
One mark for Jupiter being slower, one for it being further from the Sun, one for the Sun’s gravitational field being weaker further away. Trying to use v = 2πr/T without the periods led many to say Jupiter is faster.
0625/43/O/N/25 Q10(c) · October/November 2025 · [3 marks]

Explain why the speed of a comet changes as it travels around its orbit. Use the conservation of energy in your explanation.

The comet’s total energy (gravitational potential energy + kinetic energy) stays constant.

As it moves closer to the Sun, its gravitational potential energy decreases, so its kinetic energy increases.

More kinetic energy means a greater speed, so it is fastest nearest the Sun and slowest when it is furthest away.

Why this scores
One mark for constant total energy = GPE + KE, one for GPE decreasing and KE increasing closer to the Sun, one for more KE meaning greater speed.
0625/42/O/N/25 Q10(a) · October/November 2025 · [4 marks]

Fill in the gaps in the description of how an accretion model explains the formation of the Solar System. The planets nearest the Sun are small and … The planets furthest from the Sun are large and … All the planets were formed when a cloud of gas and dust collapsed due to … The rotation of material in the cloud forms an …

The planets nearest the Sun are small and rocky.

The planets furthest from the Sun are large and gaseous.

All the planets were formed when a cloud of gas and dust collapsed due to gravitational force (gravity).

The rotation of material in the cloud forms an accretion disc.

Why this scores
One mark for each gap: rocky, gaseous, gravitational force, accretion disc.

6.2 Stars and the Universe

These questions test the life cycle of stars, redshift, the Big Bang and CMBR, and calculations with the Hubble constant; examiners reward exact names of stages and full statements of what changes and why.

What examiners keep seeing
Vague wording: “the wavelength of the galaxy increases” instead of the wavelength of the light from the galaxy, “red giant” for a massive star (it is a red supergiant), or “neutron” instead of neutron star.
0625/42/M/J/24 Q11(b) · May/June 2024 · [1 mark]

Light observed from distant galaxies is redshifted. State the theory of the Universe that this observation supports.

The Big Bang Theory.

Why this scores
One mark for Big Bang. Hubble or Doppler were common wrong answers.
0625/42/F/M/23 Q10(c)(i) · February/March 2023 · [2 marks]

Source material: Light from a distant galaxy is redshifted.

Explain what is meant by redshift.

The observed wavelength of the light (electromagnetic radiation) from the galaxy is longer than the wavelength when it was emitted.

This happens because the galaxy is moving away from the Earth.

Why this scores
One mark for the increase in wavelength of the light, one for the galaxy moving away. Saying “the wavelength of the galaxy” was too vague for credit.
0625/42/M/J/24 Q11(c)(ii) · May/June 2024 · [2 marks]

Source material: Cosmic microwave background radiation (CMBR) is observed at all points in space.

Explain why this radiation is now in the microwave region of the electromagnetic spectrum.

Since the radiation was produced just after the Big Bang, the Universe has expanded.

The expansion has stretched (redshifted) the radiation, increasing its wavelength until it is now in the microwave region.

Why this scores
One mark for the expansion of the Universe, one for redshift / increased wavelength. Weaker answers just described properties of microwaves.
0625/41/M/J/24 Q9(c)(ii) · May/June 2024 · [3 marks]

Source material: Approximately 4.6 billion years ago, the Sun formed from an interstellar cloud of gas and became a stable star.

Describe and explain what happens as a protostar becomes a stable star.

As the protostar collapses, its core becomes hot enough for nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium to begin.

The gravitational force acts inwards; the high temperature in the core produces an outward force.

The star becomes stable when these two forces are balanced.

Why this scores
Any three of: fusion starts, gravitational force inwards, outward force due to high temperature, forces balanced. Balance force with force, not with “pressure” or “energy”, and spell fusion correctly so it cannot be read as fission.
0625/42/O/N/25 Q10(c) · October/November 2025 · [3 marks]

The Whirlpool galaxy is 2.3 × 107 light-years from the Earth. The current value of the Hubble constant is 2.2 × 10–18 per second. Calculate the speed at which the Whirlpool galaxy is moving away from the Earth.

d = 2.3 × 107 × 9.5 × 1015 = 2.2 × 1023 m

H0 = v ÷ d, so v = H0d = 2.2 × 10−18 × 2.2 × 1023

v = 4.8 × 105 m/s

Why this scores
Marks for converting light-years to metres (1 light-year = 9.5 × 1015 m), for v = H0d, and for 4.8 × 105 m/s. Forgetting the light-year conversion is the usual error.
0625/42/M/J/23 Q10(c) · May/June 2023 · [4 marks]

Fill in the gaps in the paragraph about a star much more massive than the Sun. The stage that follows the stable state in the life cycle of the star is the … stage. It then explodes as a supernova to form a …, this leaves behind a … or a ….

The stage that follows the stable state in the life cycle of the star is the red supergiant stage.

It then explodes as a supernova to form a nebula, this leaves behind a neutron star or a black hole.

Why this scores
One mark per gap. The examiner report lists the usual slips: leaving out “super” from red supergiant, writing planetary nebula, and writing “neutron” without “star”.
