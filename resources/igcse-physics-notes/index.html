Exam Guide: Papers, Timing and Command Words

What the three papers look like for the 2026–2028 exams, how long you get per mark, what each command word is asking for, what you must know by heart and how the practical papers are marked. Read this once before you start revising and again in the week before the exam.

The papers you will sit

Everyone takes three papers: one multiple-choice paper, one theory paper and one practical paper. Which multiple-choice and theory papers you sit depends on whether you are entered for Core or Extended.

  • Core (Papers 1, 3 and 5 or 6): grades C to G available. Cambridge recommends this route if you have studied the Core content only, or expect a grade D or below.
  • Extended (Papers 2, 4 and 5 or 6): grades A* to G available. Extended questions can test anything in the Core and the Supplement, so the parts marked Supplement in these notes matter only if you are Extended.
  • The theory and multiple-choice papers test knowledge with understanding (AO1, about 63%) and handling information and problem-solving (AO2, about 37%). The practical papers test only experimental skills (AO3).
  • All questions are compulsory. Calculators may be used in all parts of the examination.

Numbers, units and significant figures

  • Write numerical answers as decimals (or percentages where that makes sense), not fractions. Standard form is fine for very large or small values.
  • Round only the final answer. Keep the full value in your calculator between steps.
  • A calculated answer should have the same number of significant figures as the least precise data you used. If the data are 2.0 kg and 3.0 m/s, give 2 significant figures.
  • Use the decimal point as a dot on the line. Large numbers are printed in groups of three with a space, for example 4 256 789 (numbers from 1000 to 9999 have no space).
  • Papers use dm3 instead of litres. Use the units the question gives you, and only convert (for example cm to m, minutes to seconds) when the equation needs consistent units.

Practical papers: skills tested

Paper 5 and Paper 6 need the same skills and the same experimental contexts, and both test only AO3, so revise for them the same way. You can be asked about:

  • Contexts: measuring length, volume or force; small distances and short time intervals; derived quantities such as the extension per unit load of a spring, a resistance or an acceleration; testing the relationship between two variables (such as p.d. and the length of a wire); comparing angles of reflection or densities; heating and cooling; springs and balances; timing motion or oscillations; building and measuring electric circuits; optics with pins, mirrors, prisms, lenses and rectangular or semicircular blocks; and unfamiliar procedures with simple apparatus.
  • Apparatus you should recognise: metre and half-metre rulers graduated in mm, a stop-watch reading to 0.1 s, force meters, springs, masses and holders, pivots, a pendulum bob, a top-pan balance, measuring cylinders, a thermometer from −10 °C to 110 °C with 1 °C graduations, converging lenses of focal length 10 cm and 15 cm, optics pins, a protractor, ammeters, voltmeters, cells, resistors, lamps, diodes, LDRs and thermistors.
  • Planning: name the independent and dependent variables, say which variables to control and how, choose a suitable number and range of values, pick apparatus and say why, identify risks and precautions, and say how you would record and process the results.
  • Readings: read scales to the nearest half division, correct for zero errors, repeat readings where appropriate, and record them in a table to the precision of the instrument.
  • Interpreting: plot graphs with a best-fit line, find a gradient or intercept, spot anomalous results and form a conclusion from the data. Two results count as equal within the limits of experimental accuracy if they agree to within ±10%.
  • Evaluating: identify sources of error (measurement, random and systematic) and suggest specific improvements to the apparatus or method, such as “use a longer length of wire to reduce the percentage error”, not “be more careful”.

Words about measurement

You will not be asked to recite these definitions, but using the words correctly makes your evaluations clearer.

Accuracy
A measurement is accurate if it is close to the true value.
Precision
How close repeated measured values are to each other.
Anomaly
A value outside the general pattern of the results.
Independent variable
The variable you change.
Dependent variable
The variable you observe or measure.
Valid
The experiment tests what it says it tests: a fair test with the control variables kept constant.

Answer habits that earn marks

  • Calculations: equation first, then the numbers substituted, then the answer with its unit. A wrong final answer can still earn the method marks; a bare wrong number earns nothing.
  • Units before you substitute: convert minutes to seconds, cm to m, kW to W and g to kg where the equation needs it.
  • Precise words: mass and weight are different; so are speed and velocity, and current and p.d. Say “energy is transferred”, not “energy is used up”.
  • Vectors: give a direction with a velocity, force, momentum or acceleration when the question asks for it (“2.0 m/s to the right”).
  • Diagrams: draw rays and circuit wires with a ruler, put arrows on rays, use the syllabus circuit symbols, and draw normals as dashed lines at 90° to the surface.
  • Graphs: when you describe a graph, quote values from it (“the speed rises to 12 m/s in 8 s”).

Measuring length and volume

  • Rulers and metre rules measure lengths to the nearest millimetre (0.1 cm). Line the zero mark up with one end of the object, or read both ends and subtract.
  • Read the scale with your eye directly above (perpendicular to) the mark. Looking from an angle gives a parallax error.
  • Check for a zero error: worn rulers may not start exactly at the end, so start measuring from the 1 cm mark and subtract.
  • A measuring cylinder measures the volume of a liquid in cm3 (1 cm3 = 1 ml). Put it on a flat surface, bend down so your eye is level with the liquid surface, and read the bottom of the meniscus (the curved surface of water).
  • Choose the smallest cylinder that holds all the liquid: its scale divisions are finer, so the reading is more precise.

Measuring time intervals

InstrumentGood forMain limitation
Clock or watchLong intervals (minutes, hours)Only reads to the nearest second
Hand-operated stopwatchIntervals of a few seconds or moreYour reaction time (about 0.2–0.3 s) at the start and the stop
Digital timer with light gates or a data-loggerVery short intervals (fractions of a second)Needs setting up; no human reaction time, so much more accurate

A stopwatch may display 0.01 s, but your reaction time means the last digit is not reliable. A light gate starts and stops the timer automatically when an object breaks the beam.

Measuring multiples to get an average

When one thing is too small or too quick to measure accurately, measure many of them together and divide. The uncertainty of the measurement is shared out across all of them, so each value is much more accurate.

  • Thickness of one sheet of paper: measure the thickness of a stack of 100 sheets with a ruler, then divide by 100.
  • Diameter of a thin wire: wind 20 tight turns side by side around a pencil, measure the width of the coil, divide by 20.
  • Period of a pendulum: one oscillation is a complete swing from one side to the other and back again. The period is the time for one oscillation.

Timing the period of a pendulum

1
Use a fiducial markerPlace a mark (e.g. a pin or a line on card) behind the centre of the swing. Start and stop the stopwatch as the bob passes this mark, where it moves fastest and the moment is easiest to judge.
2
Time many swingsTime 20 complete oscillations, counting “0” as you start the watch.
3
Divide and repeatPeriod T = total time ÷ 20. Repeat the whole measurement and take the mean.
Worked example
A student times 20 oscillations of a pendulum and gets 31.6 s. A stack of 50 identical cards is 4.5 mm thick. Find the period and the thickness of one card. [3]
Period T = 31.6 ÷ 20 = 1.58 s
Thickness of one card = 4.5 ÷ 50 = 0.090 mm
Common Mistake
Counting a swing from one side to the other as one oscillation. That is only half an oscillation: the bob must come back to where it started.
Examiner Tip
If asked how to make a timing more accurate, the expected answers are: time many oscillations and divide, repeat and average, and use a fiducial marker. “Be more careful” scores nothing.

Supplement Scalars and vectors

  • A scalar quantity has magnitude (size) only.
  • A vector quantity has magnitude and direction. On a diagram it is drawn as an arrow: the length shows the size and the arrow shows the direction.

Supplement Adding two vectors at right angles

The resultant is the single vector that has the same effect as the two vectors together. At IGCSE you only add forces or velocities that are at 90° to each other.

  • By calculation: the two vectors and the resultant form a right-angled triangle. Use Pythagoras for the size and trigonometry (tan) for the direction.
  • By scale drawing: choose a scale (e.g. 1 cm = 1 N). Draw the first arrow, then draw the second arrow starting from the tip of the first. The resultant goes from the tail of the first to the tip of the second. Measure its length and convert with the scale; measure its angle with a protractor.
resultant = √(F12 + F22)    angle: tan θ = F2 ÷ F1
Worked example Supplement
A force of 12 N acts due east on a box and a force of 5.0 N acts due north. Calculate the size and direction of the resultant force. [3]
Resultant = √(122 + 5.02) = √169 = 13 N
tan θ = 5.0 ÷ 12 = 0.417, so θ = 23° north of east
Worked example Supplement
A boat points straight across a river at 4.0 m/s. The river flows at 3.0 m/s at right angles to this. Find the boat’s resultant velocity. [3]
Resultant speed = √(4.02 + 3.02) = √25 = 5.0 m/s
tan θ = 3.0 ÷ 4.0 = 0.75, so θ = 37° downstream from the direction the boat points
Examiner Tip
A velocity answer needs a direction as well as a size, otherwise it is only a speed. In a scale drawing, state your scale and draw it large (at least half a page) so the measured answer is accurate.

Speed and velocity

Speed is defined as the distance travelled per unit time.

v = s ÷ t
v
Speed, in m/s
s
Distance travelled, in m
t
Time taken, in s

Velocity is defined as speed in a given direction. Two cars at 20 m/s going opposite ways have the same speed but different velocities.

Speed often changes during a journey, so we use the average speed:

average speed = total distance travelled ÷ total time taken
Worked example
A cyclist rides 400 m in 50 s. Later, a bus travels 12 km in 15 minutes, stops, then travels a further 18 km in 25 minutes. Find the cyclist’s speed and the bus’s average speed in m/s. [4]
Cyclist: v = s ÷ t = 400 ÷ 50 = 8.0 m/s
Bus: total distance = 12 + 18 = 30 km = 30 000 m
Total time = 15 + 25 = 40 min = 2400 s (the question gives no time for the stop, so it is not included)
Average speed = 30 000 ÷ 2400 = 12.5 m/s
Common Mistake
Averaging the two speeds instead of using total distance ÷ total time. The two parts usually take different times, so the simple mean of the speeds is wrong. Also convert km to m and minutes to s before dividing if the answer must be in m/s.

  • The gradient of a straight section of a distance–time graph = speed. Pick two points far apart: speed = change in distance ÷ change in time.
  • The area under a speed–time graph = distance travelled. Split the area into rectangles (constant speed) and triangles (constant acceleration): triangle area = ½ × base × height.
  • You may be asked to sketch (shape only, labelled axes) or plot (points from a table, sensible scales, a best-fit line or smooth curve).

Supplement Acceleration

Acceleration is defined as the change in velocity per unit time.

a = Δv ÷ Δt
a
Acceleration, in m/s2
Δv
Change in velocity = final − initial, in m/s
Δt
Time taken for the change, in s
  • The gradient of a speed–time graph = acceleration.
  • A deceleration is a negative acceleration. If an object slows from 25 m/s to 0 in 5.0 s, a = (0 − 25) ÷ 5.0 = −5.0 m/s2, which is a deceleration of 5.0 m/s2.
Worked example
The graph above shows a car moving between two sets of traffic lights. (a) Calculate the total distance travelled. (b) Supplement Calculate the acceleration in the first 4 s and in the last 3 s. [5]
(a) First 4 s: ½ × 4 × 12 = 24 m
4 s to 10 s: 6 × 12 = 72 m
Last 3 s: ½ × 3 × 12 = 18 m
Total distance = 24 + 72 + 18 = 114 m
(b) First 4 s: a = Δv ÷ Δt = (12 − 0) ÷ 4 = 3.0 m/s2
Last 3 s: a = (0 − 12) ÷ 3 = −4.0 m/s2 (a deceleration of 4.0 m/s2)
Common Mistake
Reading the speed off a distance–time graph as if it were a speed–time graph (or the other way round). Always check the y-axis label first: a horizontal line means “stopped” on one and “constant speed” on the other.
Examiner Tip

Acceleration of free fall

The acceleration of free fall g for an object near the surface of the Earth is approximately constant and is approximately 9.8 m/s2. Without air resistance, every object falls with this acceleration, whatever its mass: a hammer and a feather dropped together on the Moon land at the same time.

Supplement Falling with and without air or liquid resistance

  • No air resistance (in a vacuum): the only force is the weight, so the object accelerates at a constant 9.8 m/s2. Its speed–time graph is a straight line.
  • With air resistance: the drag force grows as the object speeds up. The resultant force (weight − air resistance) gets smaller, so the acceleration gets smaller.
  • Eventually air resistance equals weight. The resultant force is zero, the acceleration is zero and the object falls at a constant speed called the terminal velocity.
  • In a liquid (for example a ball bearing dropped into a tall tube of oil) the drag is much larger, so the object reaches its terminal velocity quickly, and it is much lower than in air.
  • When a skydiver opens a parachute, air resistance suddenly becomes larger than the weight. The resultant force is upwards, so the skydiver decelerates until the forces balance again at a new, lower terminal velocity.
Model answer Supplement
A skydiver jumps from a plane. Describe and explain her motion until she reaches terminal velocity. [4]
At first her speed is low, so air resistance is small and the resultant force is almost equal to her weight: she accelerates at about 9.8 m/s2. As her speed increases, air resistance increases, so the resultant downward force decreases and her acceleration decreases. When air resistance equals her weight, the resultant force is zero, so she stops accelerating and falls at a constant speed, the terminal velocity.
Common Mistake
Saying the skydiver “slows down” as air resistance increases. She is still speeding up, just by less each second. She only slows down if air resistance becomes bigger than her weight (when the parachute opens).

Mass

  • A beam balance compares the weight of an object with the weight of known standard masses. When the beam balances, the weights on both sides are equal, so the masses are equal too (both sides are in the same gravitational field).
  • A beam balance gives the same reading on the Moon as on Earth, because both sides are affected equally by the weaker field. A newton meter on the Moon would read about one-sixth as much.
  • An electronic top-pan balance actually measures the force on its pan but is calibrated to display mass in g or kg.

Supplement Weight as the effect of a gravitational field

  • why weight is different on different planets: g is different, but m is not;
  • why an astronaut far out in space is almost weightless: the field strength there is very small;

Floating and sinking

  • An object floats in a liquid if its density is less than the density of the liquid.
  • It sinks if its density is greater than the liquid’s density.
  • For a hollow object (a ship, a ball) use its average density: total mass ÷ total volume including the air inside. That is how a steel ship floats.

What forces do: stretch and squash things, start, stop and steer objects. This section links forces to motion and is the base for momentum and energy later.

Changing size and shape: springs and stretching

Forces can change the size and shape of an object: stretching a spring, squashing a ball, bending a ruler. An object that returns to its original shape when the force is removed is called elastic.

  • Load = the force stretching the object (usually the weight of masses hung on it), in N.
  • Extension = stretched length − original length.

Investigating load and extension for a spring

3

4

Resultant force along a straight line

When several forces act along the same line, replace them with one resultant force: add forces acting in the same direction and subtract forces acting in opposite directions. Give the direction as well as the size.

Worked example

Backward forces: 120 + 60 = 180 N
Resultant = 300 − 180 = 120 N forwards

Forces and motion

  • An object either remains at rest or continues in a straight line at constant speed unless acted on by a resultant force. Balanced forces (resultant = 0) do not mean the object is stopped: it could be moving at a steady velocity.
  • A resultant force may change the velocity of an object by changing its speed (speeding up or slowing down) or its direction of motion, or both.

Turning Effect and Centre of Gravity

Why a long spanner loosens a nut more easily, how to balance a beam, and why low, wide objects are hard to knock over.

The moment of a force

The moment of a force is a measure of its turning effect about a pivot. The bigger the force, and the further it acts from the pivot, the bigger the turning effect.

  • Opening a door: the handle is far from the hinges, so a small push gives a large moment. Pushing near the hinges is much harder.
  • Spanner: a longer spanner turns a tight nut with less force.
  • Seesaw: a lighter child can balance a heavier one by sitting further from the pivot.
  • Wheelbarrow and bottle opener: long handles let a small effort produce a large turning effect.

The moment of a force is defined as:

moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot
moment
In N m (or N cm if the distance is in cm)
force
In N
distance
Perpendicular (90°) distance from the pivot to the line of the force, in m
Worked example
A force of 40 N is applied at right angles to the end of a 25 cm spanner. Calculate the moment about the nut. [2]
d = 25 cm = 0.25 m
moment = F × d = 40 × 0.25 = 10 N m

The principle of moments

When an object is balanced, the total clockwise moment about the pivot equals the total anticlockwise moment about the same pivot.

Worked example
A child of weight 300 N sits 2.0 m from the pivot of a seesaw. How far from the pivot, on the other side, must a 400 N child sit to balance it? [3]
Anticlockwise moment = 300 × 2.0 = 600 N m
For balance: clockwise moment = 600 N m, so 400 × d = 600
d = 600 ÷ 400 = 1.5 m

Supplement More than one force on each side

Add up all the clockwise moments and all the anticlockwise moments, each about the same pivot, then set the totals equal. A force that acts through the pivot has zero moment about it.

Worked example Supplement
A uniform metre rule is balanced on a pivot at its 50 cm mark. A 2.0 N weight hangs at the 10 cm mark and a 1.0 N weight at the 30 cm mark. Where must a 2.5 N weight be hung to balance the rule? [4]
The rule is uniform, so its own weight acts at the pivot and has no moment.
Anticlockwise moments = (2.0 × 40) + (1.0 × 20) = 80 + 20 = 100 N cm
Clockwise: 2.5 × d = 100, so d = 40 cm from the pivot
Position = 50 + 40 = 90 cm mark
Common Mistake
Using the scale reading (e.g. 10 cm) as the distance. The distance must be measured from the pivot: the 10 cm mark is 40 cm from a pivot at 50 cm.

Equilibrium

When there is no resultant force and no resultant moment, an object is in equilibrium. Its motion does not change: an object at rest stays at rest and does not start to turn. For a balanced beam this means: total upward force = total downward force (the pivot pushes up with a force equal to all the weights), and clockwise moments = anticlockwise moments.

Centre of gravity

The centre of gravity of an object is the point through which all of its weight appears to act. For a uniform, regular object (a metre rule, a square sheet) it is at the geometric centre.

Centre of gravity and stability

  • When an object is tilted, it turns about the edge of its base. If the vertical line through the centre of gravity still falls inside the base, the weight gives a moment that turns the object back: it is stable.
  • If that line falls outside the base, the moment of the weight turns it further over and it topples.
  • So an object is more stable if it has a low centre of gravity and a wide base. Examples: racing cars are low and wide; a Bunsen burner has a heavy, wide base; a bus is tested on a tilt platform; a desk lamp has a heavy base.

Common Mistake
Saying an object is stable “because it is heavy”. Stability depends on the position of the centre of gravity and the width of the base, not just the weight.

Explosions and recoil

Before an explosion, everything is at rest, so the total momentum is zero. Afterwards the pieces move off with equal and opposite momenta, still adding to zero. This is why a gun recoils and a rocket is pushed forwards by its exhaust gases.

Rifle must have 8.0 kg m/s backwards: v = 8.0 ÷ 4.0 = 2.0 m/s backwards

For a fixed change in momentum, a longer time means a smaller force. This is the physics behind many safety features:

  • Crumple zones, airbags and seat belts make the passenger stop over a longer time, so the force on them is smaller.
  • Crash mats, cycle helmets and padded gloves squash on impact, increasing the stopping time.
  • Bending your knees when you land from a jump does the same.
Worked example Supplement
A 70 kg passenger moving at 15 m/s is brought to rest in a crash. Compare the average force on the passenger if the stop takes 0.010 s (hitting the dashboard) and 0.10 s (with an airbag). [4]
Δp = 70 × 15 = 1050 kg m/s in both cases
Dashboard: F = 1050 ÷ 0.010 = 105 000 N (1.1 × 105 N)
Airbag: F = 1050 ÷ 0.10 = 10 500 N (1.1 × 104 N)
The airbag makes the stopping time 10 times longer, so the force is 10 times smaller.
Examiner Tip
In “explain how an airbag reduces injury” answers, use the full chain: the change in momentum is the same, the airbag increases the time of the collision, so the rate of change of momentum is smaller, so the force on the person is smaller. “It cushions you” alone scores nothing.

Energy, Work and Power

Where energy is stored, how it moves from one store to another, and how to calculate work done and power. These ideas run through every other topic in physics.

Energy stores

Energy may be stored as:

StoreExample
KineticAny moving object: a car, a flowing river
Gravitational potentialAn object raised above the ground: water behind a dam
ChemicalFood, fuels, batteries
Elastic (strain)A stretched spring or rubber band, a drawn bow
NuclearThe nuclei of atoms, released in fission and fusion
ElectrostaticCharges that attract or repel, e.g. a charged balloon
Internal (thermal)A hot object: the energy of its particles

Energy transfers

Energy moves between stores in four ways:

  • By forces (mechanical work done): a person lifting a box, a car engine pushing the car forward.
  • By electrical currents (electrical work done): a battery driving a current through a motor or a lamp.
  • By heating: a hot plate warming a pan of water.
  • By waves: electromagnetic waves (light from the Sun, microwaves in an oven), sound waves and other waves (such as water waves).

A ball fallsGravitational potential store → kinetic store (by the force of gravity doing work)
A battery-powered torch is onChemical store of the battery → (electrical work) → light waves and the internal store of the surroundings

A catapult fires a stoneElastic store → (work done by the force of the band) → kinetic store of the stone
Water boils in a kettleElectrical work → internal store of the element → (heating) → internal store of the water

Conservation of energy

The principle of the conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed. It can only be transferred from one store to another. The total amount of energy stays the same.

  • In real devices some energy always ends up in a form we did not want, usually the internal (thermal) store of the surroundings. This energy is wasted (dissipated), not destroyed.
  • A flow diagram shows the stores and transfers in order, for example a hairdryer: electrical work → kinetic store of the fan + internal store of the heater + sound. The useful energy out + the wasted energy out = the energy in.

Supplement The Sun as the source

  • Radiation from the Sun is the main source of energy for all our energy resources except geothermal, nuclear and tidal.

Supplement Nuclear fusion

  • Energy is released in the Sun by nuclear fusion: light nuclei (hydrogen) join together to form a heavier nucleus (helium), releasing a great deal of energy.
  • Research is being carried out to investigate how energy released by nuclear fusion can be used to produce electrical energy on a large scale. Experimental fusion reactors exist, but reaching and containing the extremely high temperatures needed is very difficult, so no fusion power station yet supplies electricity to the grid.

Efficiency

No energy transfer is perfect. Efficiency describes how much of the energy put in ends up in the form we want. A more efficient device wastes less energy, usually as thermal energy to the surroundings. An LED lamp is more efficient than a filament lamp because more of its input energy becomes light and less heats the surroundings.

efficiency = (useful energy output ÷ total energy input) × 100%

Pressure

Why sharp knives cut, why snowshoes stop you sinking and why dams are thicker at the bottom: it all depends on how a force is spread over an area.

Definition and equation

Pressure is defined as force per unit area.

p = F ÷ A
p
Pressure, in pascals (Pa); 1 Pa = 1 N/m2
F
Force acting at right angles to the surface, in N
A
Area, in m2 (1 cm2 = 0.0001 m2)
Worked example
A woman weighs 600 N. (a) Standing in flat shoes, her total contact area with the floor is 0.040 m2. Calculate the pressure on the floor. (b) At one moment all her weight is on one stiletto heel of area 1.0 cm2. Calculate the pressure. [4]
(a) p = F ÷ A = 600 ÷ 0.040 = 15 000 Pa
(b) A = 1.0 cm2 = 1.0 × 10−4 m2
p = 600 ÷ 1.0 × 10−4 = 6.0 × 106 Pa: 400 times larger, which is why stiletto heels can dent wooden floors.
Common Mistake
Converting cm2 to m2 by dividing by 100. There are 100 × 100 = 10 000 cm2 in 1 m2, so divide by 10 000.

Pressure in everyday life

  • Bigger force, same area → bigger pressure. Pressing harder on a drawing pin pushes it in further.
  • Same force, smaller area → bigger pressure. Used when we want to cut or pierce:
    • A sharp knife has a very thin edge, so the pressure is large enough to cut.
    • A drawing pin, a nail and a needle have tiny points.
  • Same force, larger area → smaller pressure. Used to stop things sinking in or causing damage:
    • Snowshoes, skis and the wide feet of camels spread weight over soft snow or sand.
    • Tractors and tanks have wide tyres or caterpillar tracks so they do not sink into mud.
    • Buildings stand on wide foundations; a rucksack has wide straps so it does not dig into your shoulders.

Pressure in liquids

  • The pressure beneath the surface of a liquid increases with depth: the deeper you go, the greater the weight of liquid above pressing down.
  • At the same depth, the pressure is greater in a denser liquid (for example sea water compared with fresh water).
  • At any point, liquid pressure acts equally in all directions.
  • This is why dams are built thicker at the bottom, divers feel more pressure on their ears as they go deeper, and submarines need very strong hulls.

The Kinetic Particle Model of Matter

Everything is made of particles that never stop moving. This one idea explains the properties of solids, liquids and gases, what temperature really measures, why gases exert pressure and why there is a lowest possible temperature.

Solids, liquids and gases

PropertySolidLiquidGas

Changes of state

  • Melting: solid → liquid. Solidification (freezing): liquid → solid.
  • Boiling or evaporation: liquid → gas. Condensation: gas → liquid.

Changes straight from gas to solid or solid to gas are not required.

The particle model

SolidLiquidGas

Arrangement Separation Motion — describe all three for full marks.

Supplement Forces and distances between particles

  • Solid: strong forces of attraction hold particles in fixed positions, so the solid keeps its shape.
  • Liquid: particles have enough energy to move out of fixed positions, but the attractive forces are still strong enough to keep them close together. That is why a liquid flows but keeps its volume.
  • Gas: particles are far apart and the forces between them are negligible (except when they collide), so a gas spreads out and is easy to compress.

Temperature and particle energy

  • When a substance is heated, its particles gain kinetic energy and move (or vibrate) faster.
  • The temperature of a substance is a measure of the average kinetic energy of its particles. Higher temperature = faster particles on average.

Gas pressure

Gas particles move randomly at high speed. Every time one hits a wall of its container it bounces off and exerts a tiny force. Billions of these collisions every second add up to a steady force on each area of wall, and that is gas pressure.

  • Heat the gas (fixed volume): particles move faster, so they hit the walls more often and harder. Pressure increases.
  • Squash the gas (fixed temperature): the same particles are in a smaller space, so they hit the walls more often. Pressure increases.

Supplement Explaining pressure with force

Each collision changes the momentum of a particle, so the wall exerts a force on the particle and the particle exerts an equal force on the wall. Pressure is this total force divided by the area of the wall (p = F / A). More frequent or harder collisions mean a larger total force on the same area, so a higher pressure.

Absolute zero and the Kelvin scale

  • Cooling a substance slows its particles down. At absolute zero (−273 °C) the particles have the least possible kinetic energy, so nothing can be colder.
  • The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero. One kelvin is the same size as one degree Celsius.
T (in K) = θ (in °C) + 273
Worked example
Convert 27 °C and 0 K into the other scale.
27 °C → 27 + 273 = 300 K
0 K → 0 − 273 = −273 °C (absolute zero)
Common Mistake
Writing “300 °K”. Kelvin has no degree sign: write 300 K.

Supplement Pressure and volume of a gas

For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, pressure and volume are inversely proportional: halve the volume and the pressure doubles.

pV = constant   or   p1V1 = p2V2

Why: in a smaller volume the particles travel a shorter distance between collisions with the walls, so they hit the walls more often. At the same temperature they hit with the same average force, so the total force per unit area (pressure) rises.

Worked example
A syringe holds 60 cm3 of air at 100 kPa. The plunger is pushed in slowly (temperature stays the same) until the volume is 20 cm3. Calculate the new pressure. [3]
p1V1 = p2V2
100 × 60 = p2 × 20
p2 = 6000 ÷ 20 = 300 kPa
Check: the volume became 3× smaller, so the pressure should be 3× bigger. ✓
Examiner Tip
Volumes and pressures can stay in any units (cm3, kPa) as long as both sides of p1V1 = p2V2 use the same units. Always state “constant temperature” if asked for the conditions.

Brownian motion

If you look at smoke in air (or pollen grains in water) through a microscope, the specks jiggle around in a jerky, random way. This is Brownian motion.

  • It is caused by the specks being hit by the invisible, fast-moving air (or water) molecules from all sides.
  • It is evidence for the kinetic particle model: the particles of a gas or liquid are moving randomly and colliding with the specks.

Supplement Why the specks move

The smoke particles are much larger and heavier than air molecules. At any instant, more molecules happen to hit one side than the other, so there is an unbalanced force and the speck moves a little. The direction keeps changing at random. Because a light, fast-moving molecule can move a massive speck, the molecules must be moving very fast.

Common Mistake
Supplement Mixing up the words. Use molecules (or atoms) for the air or water, and microscopic particles (or specks) for the smoke or pollen. You can see the specks; the molecules hitting them are far too small to see.

Thermal Properties and Temperature

What happens when things are heated: they expand, their temperature rises by an amount that depends on the material, and at the right temperatures they melt, boil or evaporate.

Topical Past Papers for this topic18 real exam questions on Thermal Physics, with model answers.Practise →

Thermal expansion

Most substances expand when heated. The particles do not get bigger: they move or vibrate more, so they take up more space.

StateHow much it expandsSupplement Why
SolidLeastStrong forces hold the particles tightly; they only vibrate more.
LiquidMoreForces are weaker, so particles move further apart as they gain energy.
GasMostForces between particles are negligible, so nothing holds them back.

Everyday examples

  • Gaps in bridges and railway tracks leave room for the metal to expand on hot days without buckling.
  • Overhead power cables are hung slack so they don’t snap when they contract in cold weather.
  • Liquid-in-glass thermometers: the liquid expands up a narrow tube as temperature rises.
  • Loosening a tight metal lid by running it under hot water: the metal lid expands more than the glass jar.

Specific heat capacity

  • Heating an object increases its internal energy (the total energy of its particles), which raises its temperature.
  • Different materials need different amounts of energy to warm up by the same amount. Water needs a lot; metals need much less.
  • Supplement A rise in temperature means the average kinetic energy of all the particles in the object has increased.

Specific heat capacity (c) is the energy required per unit mass per unit temperature increase. In other words, the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C.

Melting, boiling and evaporation

  • While a substance is melting or boiling, energy is still going in but its temperature stays the same. The energy is used to overcome the forces between particles, not to speed them up.
  • For pure water at standard atmospheric pressure: melting point 0 °C, boiling point 100 °C.
  • Condensation (gas → liquid) and solidification (liquid → solid): particles lose energy, slow down and the attractive forces pull them closer together.
  • Evaporation happens at the surface of a liquid at any temperature. Only the more energetic particles near the surface have enough energy to escape.
  • Evaporation causes cooling: the fastest particles leave, so the average kinetic energy of those left behind is lower, and temperature measures average kinetic energy.

Supplement What makes evaporation faster

  • Higher temperature: more particles have enough energy to escape.
  • Larger surface area: more particles are at the surface.
  • Air moving over the surface (wind, a fan): escaped particles are carried away before they can fall back in.

Cooling something by evaporation: when a liquid evaporates from an object (sweat on skin, water on a clay pot), the liquid cools, and energy flows from the warmer object into the liquid. The object cools down.

Topic 2.3 · Thermal Physics

Transfer of Thermal Energy

Thermal energy always flows from hotter to colder places, by conduction, convection or radiation. Expect “explain using particles” questions and applications such as cooking pans, fridges and the Earth’s temperature.

Topical Past Papers for this topic18 real exam questions on Thermal Physics, with model answers.Practise →

Conduction

Conduction is the transfer of thermal energy through a material, mainly solids, without the material itself moving.

  • Good thermal conductors: metals (copper, aluminium, iron).
  • Poor conductors (insulators): plastic, wood, glass, air, wool. Materials that trap air, such as foam and fleece, are especially good insulators.
  • Most liquids and all gases are poor conductors.

Showing which material conducts best

Attach a pin to the end of equal-sized rods of different metals with wax, then heat the other ends equally. The pin falls first from the best conductor.

Supplement How conduction works

  • Lattice vibrations (all solids): particles at the hot end vibrate more strongly and pass this vibration on to their neighbours, one by one. This is slow.
  • Free (delocalised) electrons (metals only): electrons at the hot end gain kinetic energy and move quickly through the metal, colliding with particles further away and transferring energy. This is much faster, which is why metals are the best conductors.
  • Gases: the particles are far apart, so they collide with each other only rarely and energy is passed on very slowly.
  • Most liquids: the particles are close but not held in a lattice, and there are no free electrons, so passing on vibrations is inefficient. (Mercury, a liquid metal, is an exception.)
  • In between: many solids, such as glass, concrete and brick, conduct better than thermal insulators but much less well than metals.

Convection

Convection is the transfer of thermal energy by the movement of a liquid or gas (a fluid). It cannot happen in solids.

1
Fluid near the heater warms upIts particles move faster and spread further apart, so the fluid expands.
2
It becomes less dense and risesThe same mass now takes up more volume.
3
Cooler, denser fluid sinksIt moves in to replace the rising fluid, is heated in turn and rises.
4
A convection current formsA continuous loop of moving fluid carries energy around.

Showing convection: drop a crystal of potassium manganate(VII) at the bottom edge of a beaker of water and heat gently below it. The purple colour rises above the flame, moves across the top and sinks on the far side.

Common Mistake
“Heat rises” earns no marks. It is the warmer, less dense fluid that rises.

Thermal radiation

  • Thermal radiation is infrared electromagnetic radiation. All objects emit it, and hotter objects emit more.
  • It needs no medium, so it can travel through a vacuum. That is how energy reaches us from the Sun.

Measure the infrared from each surface with an infrared detector at the same distance, or record how fast each can cools. The dull black surface emits most and cools fastest.

Absorbers: place two identical metal plates, one dull black and one shiny, the same distance either side of a radiant heater. Fix a thermometer (or stick a coin with wax) on the back of each. The thermometer behind the dull black plate rises faster (or its coin falls first), so dull black is the better absorber.

Supplement Rate of radiation and the Earth’s temperature

  • The rate at which an object emits radiation increases with its surface temperature and its surface area.
  • An object stays at a constant temperature when it absorbs energy at the same rate as it emits it. If it absorbs faster, it warms up; if it emits faster, it cools down.
  • The Earth absorbs radiation from the Sun and emits infrared back into space. Its temperature depends on the balance. Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere absorb some of the outgoing infrared and re-emit some back towards the Earth. More greenhouse gas means less energy escapes, so the balance is reached at a higher temperature. On a cloudy night, clouds trap more infrared, so it stays warmer than on a clear night.

What a wave does

Waves transfer energy from one place to another without transferring matter. The particles of the medium vibrate about fixed positions; they don’t travel with the wave.

Wavelength (λ)
Distance between two neighbouring crests (or any two matching points). Unit: m
Amplitude
Maximum distance of a point from its rest position (centre line). Unit: m
Frequency (f)
Number of waves passing a point per second. Unit: hertz, Hz
Wave speed (v)
Distance travelled by the wave per second. Unit: m/s
Crest / trough
The highest / lowest points of a transverse wave
Wavefront
A line joining matching points on a wave, e.g. all the crests. Drawn as lines one wavelength apart
wave speed = frequency × wavelength    v = f λ
Worked example
A water wave has a frequency of 2.5 Hz and a wavelength of 0.40 m. Calculate its speed. Then find the wavelength of a radio wave of frequency 100 MHz (speed 3.0 × 108 m/s).
v = fλ = 2.5 × 0.40 = 1.0 m/s
λ = v ÷ f = 3.0 × 108 ÷ 100 × 106 = 3.0 m

Forgetting to convert prefixes: kHz × 103, MHz × 106, GHz × 109. Also, the source sets the frequency, so frequency does not change when a wave moves into a new medium; speed and wavelength do.

Reflection, refraction and diffraction

A ripple tank shows these effects with water waves. A light above it projects the wavefronts onto a screen below.

  • Reflection: waves bounce off a flat barrier. The angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence, and the wavelength stays the same.
  • Refraction: waves change speed when they move into a different medium (in a ripple tank, into shallower water where they slow down). The wavelength gets shorter and, if the waves arrive at an angle, they change direction.
  • Diffraction: waves spread out when they pass through a gap or round the edge of an obstacle. The wavelength stays the same.

Supplement How much do waves diffract?

  • Diffraction is greatest when the gap is about the same size as the wavelength. A gap much wider than the wavelength gives only a little spreading at the edges.
  • Longer wavelengths diffract more round obstacles. That is why you can hear someone round a corner (sound wavelengths are around a metre) but can’t see them (light wavelengths are less than a millionth of a metre).

Light: Reflection and Refraction

Key words

Normal
A line drawn at 90° to a surface where the ray hits it (draw it dashed)
Angle of incidence (i)
Angle between the incoming ray and the normal
Angle of reflection
Angle between the reflected ray and the normal
Angle of refraction (r)
Angle between the refracted ray and the normal
Common Mistake
Measuring angles from the surface instead of from the normal. Every angle in this topic is measured from the normal.

Reflection in a plane mirror

Law of reflection: angle of incidence = angle of reflection.

The image in a plane mirror is:

  • the same size as the object
  • the same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front
  • virtual (it can’t be shown on a screen, because the light only appears to come from it)
  • upright, and Background laterally inverted (left and right swapped)

Supplement Drawing the image in a plane mirror

1

2

3
Draw the incident raysJoin the object to each point where the rays meet the mirror. Add arrows pointing from object to mirror to eye.
Worked example
A ray of light strikes a plane mirror at 25° to the mirror surface. Find the angle of reflection and the angle between the incident and reflected rays.
Angles are measured from the normal: i = 90° − 25° = 65°
Angle of reflection = angle of incidence = 65°
Angle between the rays = 65° + 65° = 130°

Refraction

Light changes speed when it passes from one material into another. If it hits the boundary at an angle, this makes it change direction: this is refraction.

  • Air → glass or water: light slows down and bends towards the normal (r is smaller than i).
  • Glass or water → air: light speeds up and bends away from the normal.
  • A ray that hits the boundary along the normal (i = 0) changes speed but does not change direction.

Investigating refraction with a glass block

Draw round a rectangular block on paper. Shine a narrow ray from a ray box into one side, mark the ray going in and coming out with crosses, then remove the block and join the points. Draw the normal and measure i and r with a protractor. Repeat for several angles of incidence. The same method works for blocks of other shapes, such as a triangular prism or a semicircular block (aim the ray at the centre of the flat face).

Supplement Refractive index

The refractive index (n) is the ratio of the speeds of a wave in two different regions. For light going from air (or a vacuum) into a material: n = speed of light in air ÷ speed of light in the material. It has no units, and the larger it is, the more the light slows and bends. Glass is about 1.5 (light travels at 3.0 × 108 ÷ 1.5 = 2.0 × 108 m/s in it); water is about 1.33.

n = sin i ÷ sin r

Here i is the angle in air and r is the angle in the material.

Worked example
Light enters a plastic block with an angle of incidence of 50°. The angle of refraction is 31°. Calculate the refractive index of the plastic.
n = sin 50° ÷ sin 31° = 0.766 ÷ 0.515 = 1.49

Total internal reflection

  • When light travels from glass (or water) towards air, some is refracted out and some is reflected back.
  • As the angle of incidence increases, the refracted ray bends further from the normal. The critical angle (c) is the angle of incidence (in the glass or water) at which the angle of refraction is 90°: the refracted ray just travels along the boundary.
  • If the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle, all the light is reflected back inside: total internal reflection (TIR).
  • Seeing it in the lab: shine a ray box through the curved side of a semicircular glass block towards the centre of its flat face (it enters along a normal, so it does not bend). Increase the angle of incidence: the refracted ray bends further from the normal and gets dimmer while the reflected ray gets brighter, until beyond the critical angle all the light reflects.
  • Everyday examples: light trapped in optical fibres, the prisms in periscopes and binoculars, the sparkle of a cut diamond, and the silvery look of a water surface seen from underneath.
Examiner Tip
TIR needs two conditions: (1) light travelling from a denser to a less dense medium (e.g. glass to air), and (2) angle of incidence greater than the critical angle. Many answers lose a mark by giving only one.

Supplement Critical angle and optical fibres

n = 1 ÷ sin c
Worked example
Glass has a refractive index of 1.50. Calculate its critical angle.
sin c = 1 ÷ n = 1 ÷ 1.50 = 0.667
c = sin−1(0.667) = 42°

Optical fibres are thin, flexible strands of very clear glass. Light entering one end hits the inside surface at an angle greater than the critical angle, so it is totally internally reflected again and again and comes out of the far end with very little loss.

  • Communications: carrying telephone, cable TV and broadband signals as pulses of light or infrared.
  • Background Medicine: an endoscope uses bundles of fibres to carry light into the body and an image back out, so doctors can look inside without major surgery.

Supplement Analogue and digital signals

  • This gives an increased range (the signal can be regenerated accurately many times along the way) and an increased rate of transmission of data.

Sound

How sound is produced and travels, how pitch and loudness relate to the wave, how to measure the speed of sound, and how ultrasound is used to see inside things.

How sound is made and travels

  • Sound is produced by vibrating objects (a guitar string, a loudspeaker cone, your vocal cords).
  • Sound is a longitudinal wave: the particles of the medium vibrate back and forth parallel to the direction the wave travels.
  • Supplement It travels as a series of compressions (particles squashed together, higher pressure) and rarefactions (particles spread out, lower pressure).
  • Sound needs a medium (solid, liquid or gas) and cannot travel through a vacuum. If air is pumped out of a jar containing a ringing bell, the sound fades even though you can still see the bell vibrating.

Hearing, pitch and loudness

  • The range of human hearing is about 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz (20 kHz).
  • Higher frequency = higher pitch.
  • Larger amplitude = louder sound.
  • An echo is a reflected sound wave.
Examiner Tip
On an oscilloscope trace, more waves across the screen means higher frequency (higher pitch); taller waves mean larger amplitude (louder). Compare the traces carefully: questions often change only one of these.

The speed of sound

  • In air, the speed of sound is about 330–350 m/s.
  • Supplement In general, sound travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids and slowest in gases.

Measuring the speed of sound in air (echo method)

1
Set upStand a measured distance (e.g. 50 m, using a tape measure) from a large wall.
2
Clap and timeClap in time with the echoes so each clap coincides with the echo of the one before. Time 20 claps with a stopwatch.
3
CalculateTime for one clap = total ÷ 20. The sound travelled to the wall and back, so speed = 2 × distance ÷ time for one clap.

Another method: two people stand a measured distance apart. One makes a loud sound with a visible action (bangs two blocks together) and the other starts a stopwatch on seeing it and stops it on hearing it. Light arrives almost instantly, so speed = distance ÷ time.

Worked example
A student stands 85 m from a cliff and hears the echo of a shout 0.50 s later. Calculate the speed of sound.
Distance travelled = there and back = 2 × 85 = 170 m
speed = distance ÷ time = 170 ÷ 0.50 = 340 m/s
Common Mistake
Forgetting to double the distance in echo questions. The sound goes to the wall and back.

Ultrasound

Ultrasound is sound with a frequency higher than 20 kHz, too high for humans to hear.

Supplement Uses of ultrasound

Ultrasound partly reflects at boundaries between different materials, and the time taken for the echo to return shows how deep the boundary is.

  • Non-destructive testing: finding cracks or flaws inside metal parts (such as aircraft wings or rail tracks) without damaging them. A crack reflects some ultrasound back early.
  • Medical scanning of soft tissue: e.g. scanning an unborn baby. Unlike X-rays, ultrasound is not ionising, so it is safe.
  • Sonar: ships send pulses of ultrasound down and time the echo from the sea bed or a shoal of fish to find the depth.
Worked example
A ship sends an ultrasound pulse to the sea bed. The echo returns after 0.80 s. The speed of sound in sea water is 1500 m/s. Calculate the depth of the sea. [3]
Total distance = speed × time = 1500 × 0.80 = 1200 m
This is there and back, so depth = 1200 ÷ 2 = 600 m

Simple Phenomena of Magnetism

How magnets push and pull, which materials they affect, what a magnetic field is and how to map one. Everything later in the topic (relays, motors, generators, transformers) builds on this.

Topical Past Papers for this topic46 real exam questions on Electricity and Magnetism, with model answers.Practise →

Magnetic poles and forces

  • Every magnet has two ends where its magnetic effect is strongest: a north pole (N pole) and a south pole (S pole). A freely hanging magnet turns so that its N pole points (roughly) north.
  • Like poles repel: N and N push apart, S and S push apart.
  • Unlike poles attract: N and S pull together.
  • A magnet attracts an unmagnetised piece of magnetic material (such as iron) whichever pole you bring up. A magnet and an unmagnetised material never repel.
  • A magnetised object has its own N and S poles. An unmagnetised object has no poles of its own, even if it is made of a magnetic material.
Examiner Tip
Repulsion is the only sure test for a magnet. If a bar is attracted to a magnet, it could be a magnet or just unmagnetised iron. If one end of the bar is repelled by a known pole, the bar must be a magnet.

Induced magnetism

When a piece of magnetic material (for example an iron nail) is placed near or touching a magnet, it becomes a magnet itself. This is induced magnetism. The end of the nail nearest the magnet’s N pole becomes an S pole, so the nail is attracted. That is also why a chain of paper clips can hang from one magnet: each clip magnetises the next.

Magnetic and non-magnetic materials

  • Magnetic materials are attracted by a magnet and can be magnetised: iron, steel, nickel and cobalt.
  • Non-magnetic materials are not attracted by a magnet and cannot be magnetised: for example copper, aluminium, brass, wood, plastic and glass.
Common Mistake
Saying “all metals are magnetic”. Most are not. Aluminium cans and copper wires are not attracted by a magnet; that is how recycling plants separate steel cans from aluminium cans.

Magnetic fields

A magnetic field is a region in which a magnetic pole experiences a force.

The direction of a magnetic field at a point is the direction of the force on the N pole of a magnet at that point. So field lines always point away from N and towards S outside a magnet.

Rules for drawing field lines

  • Lines start on the N pole and end on the S pole, with arrows pointing from N to S.
  • Lines never cross or touch each other.
  • Draw them as smooth curves, symmetrical about the magnet, and closer together near the poles.

Supplement Field strength and why magnets exert forces

  • The relative strength of a magnetic field is shown by the spacing of the field lines: lines close together mean a strong field, lines far apart mean a weak field. Near the poles the lines are crowded, so the field is strongest there.
  • Magnetic forces are due to interactions between magnetic fields. When two magnets are brought close, their fields overlap and combine; it is this interaction that pushes or pulls the magnets.

Electric Charge

Static electricity, why rubbing things charges them, electric fields, and the electron model that explains why metals conduct and plastics don’t.

Topical Past Papers for this topic46 real exam questions on Electricity and Magnetism, with model answers.Practise →

Positive and negative charge

  • There are two kinds of charge: positive and negative.
  • Like charges repel: positive repels positive, negative repels negative.
  • Unlike charges attract: positive attracts negative.
  • An object with equal amounts of positive and negative charge is neutral (uncharged).

Supplement The unit of charge

Charge is measured in coulombs (C). Its symbol in equations is Q.

Charging by friction

  • Rubbing two different insulating materials together (for example a polythene rod with a dry cloth) can charge both of them.
  • Only negative charges (electrons) move. Electrons are rubbed off one material and onto the other. Positive charges stay where they are, because they are in the nuclei of the atoms.
  • The object that gains electrons becomes negatively charged. The object that loses electrons becomes positively charged, because it now has more positive charge than negative.
  • The two objects end up with equal and opposite charges.
Common Mistake
Writing “the rod gained positive charges” or “protons moved onto the cloth”. In charging by friction, only electrons are transferred. A positively charged object has lost electrons.

Experiments with static charge

Showing that charges are produced by friction

1
Charge two rodsRub two polythene rods with a dry cloth. Hang one rod in a paper stirrup from a thread so it can turn freely.
2
Like chargesBring the second polythene rod near the hanging one. The hanging rod turns away: like charges repel.
3
Unlike chargesNow rub an acetate rod with a cloth and bring it near the hanging polythene rod. The rod turns towards it: the acetate has the opposite charge, and unlike charges attract.

Detecting charge

  • Small pieces of paper: a charged rod picks up small bits of paper, but an uncharged rod does not.
  • Gold-leaf electroscope: touching the metal cap with a charged object spreads charge down to the metal stem and the thin gold leaf. Both get the same charge, so the leaf repels the stem and rises. The bigger the charge, the further the leaf rises.

Supplement Electric fields

An electric field is a region in which an electric charge experiences a force.

The direction of an electric field at a point is the direction of the force on a positive charge at that point. So field lines point away from positive charges and towards negative charges.

Examiner Tip
When you sketch the field between parallel plates, draw at least four straight, evenly spaced lines at right angles to the plates, with arrows from the positive plate to the negative plate. End effects (the curving at the edges) are not examined.

Conductors and insulators

  • Electrical conductors let charge flow through them easily. Examples: metals such as copper, aluminium, silver and gold, and carbon (graphite).
  • Electrical insulators do not let charge flow. Examples: plastic (PVC, polythene), rubber, glass, dry wood, ceramics and dry air.

The electron model

In a metal, some electrons are not tied to any one atom. These free electrons can drift through the whole metal, so charge can move through it: metals are good conductors. In an insulator, all the electrons are held tightly by their atoms. There are (almost) no free electrons, so charge cannot flow. This is also why an insulator can hold static charge in one place when rubbed, while a metal held in your hand loses it straight away.

Experiment: is it a conductor or an insulator?

1

2
Test the sampleClip the test material into the gap.
3
ConcludeIf the lamp lights (or the ammeter shows a current), the material is a conductor. If not, it is an insulator.
Model answer
A student rubs a polythene rod with a woollen cloth. The rod becomes negatively charged. Explain how the rod becomes charged and state the charge on the cloth. [3]
Friction transfers electrons from the cloth to the rod. The rod now has more electrons than protons, so it is negatively charged. The cloth has lost electrons, so it is left with an equal amount of positive charge.

Current, E.M.F., P.D. and Resistance

The three quantities in every circuit question: how much charge flows (current), how much energy each coulomb carries (e.m.f. and p.d.), and how hard a component makes it for current to flow (resistance).

Topical Past Papers for this topic46 real exam questions on Electricity and Magnetism, with model answers.Practise →

Electric current

  • An electric current is a flow of charge. The bigger the current, the more charge flows past a point each second.
  • In metals, the current is carried by free electrons drifting through the metal. A cell pushes them round the circuit.
  • Current is measured in amperes (A), often shortened to amps. Small currents are given in milliamperes: 1 mA = 0.001 A.

Supplement Defining current

Electric current is the charge passing a point per unit time.

I = Q ÷ t   or   Q = I t
I
Current, in A
Q

t

Worked example Supplement
A current of 0.40 A flows through a lamp for 2.5 minutes. Calculate the charge that passes through the lamp. [3]
t = 2.5 × 60 = 150 s
Q = I t = 0.40 × 150

Supplement Conventional current and electron flow

  • Conventional current flows from positive to negative (from the + terminal of the cell, round the circuit, to the − terminal). Circuit arrows always show conventional current.
  • The free electrons actually flow the other way, from negative to positive, because they are negatively charged.

Measuring current: ammeters

  • An ammeter is connected in series, so the current being measured passes through it.
  • An ideal ammeter has (almost) zero resistance, so it does not change the current it is measuring.
  • Analogue ammeters have a needle and a scale. Read the scale straight on (to avoid parallax error), and check the needle is on zero before you start.
  • Digital ammeters show a number. If the last digit flickers, record the value it settles on (or the middle of the values it flickers between).
  • Ammeters come with different ranges (for example 0–1 A, 0–5 A, 0–100 mA). Choose a range just above the largest current you expect: too small a range and the meter goes off the scale (and may be damaged); too large a range and the reading is less precise.

Electromotive force (e.m.f.) and potential difference (p.d.)

The electromotive force (e.m.f.) is the electrical work done by a source in moving a unit charge around a complete circuit.

The potential difference (p.d.) is the work done by a unit charge passing through a component.

  • Both e.m.f. and p.d. are measured in volts (V). People often call p.d. “voltage”.
  • Think of it in terms of energy: e.m.f. is energy given to each coulomb by the source; p.d. is energy given out by each coulomb in a component (as light, heat and so on).

Measuring p.d.: voltmeters

  • A voltmeter is connected in parallel (across) the component, because it compares the energy of the charge on each side of it.
  • An ideal voltmeter has a very high resistance, so almost no current flows through it.
  • As with ammeters, voltmeters can be analogue or digital and have different ranges (for example 0–3 V, 0–15 V). Pick the smallest range that the reading will not go beyond.
Common Mistake
Drawing the voltmeter in series with the component. A voltmeter always goes across the component, connected to the wires on either side of it. Only the ammeter goes in the main loop.

Resistance of a wire: length and thickness

  • A longer wire has a larger resistance: the electrons have further to travel through the metal.
  • A thicker wire (larger cross-sectional area) has a smaller resistance: there is more room for the electrons to flow, like a wider road carrying more traffic.

Supplement Resistance, length and area: proportionality

For a metallic electrical conductor (same material, same temperature):

  • resistance is inversely proportional to cross-sectional area: R ∝ 1 ÷ A (double the area, half the resistance)

Doubling the diameter does not double the area. Area depends on diameter squared, so doubling the diameter makes the area 4 times bigger and the resistance 4 times smaller.

Electrical Energy and Power

Circuits exist to move energy from a supply to where it is useful. This section gives the power and energy equations and shows how electricity bills are worked out in kilowatt-hours.

Energy transfer in circuits

  • An electric circuit transfers energy from a source of electrical energy, such as a cell, a battery or the mains supply, to the circuit components, and then into the surroundings.
  • For example, in a torch: chemical energy in the battery → energy carried by the current → the lamp → light and thermal energy spreading into the room.
  • In a kettle the energy goes to the heating element and then into the water (and some heats the kettle and the air). In a fan it ends up as kinetic energy of the air, and finally as thermal energy.

Power is the rate of energy transfer. An appliance with a higher power transfers more energy every second.

The kilowatt-hour (kW h)

The joule is a tiny unit for household energy, so electricity companies use the kilowatt-hour.

One kilowatt-hour is the energy transferred by a 1 kW appliance used for 1 hour.

energy (kW h) = power (kW) × time (h)     cost = energy (kW h) × cost per kW h

1 kW h = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3 600 000 J (3.6 MJ).

Energy = P t = 2.0 × 90 = 180 kW h
Cost = 180 × 25 = 4500 sen = RM 45

Series and parallel: the basic rules

  • You should be able to build and use both kinds of circuit: in series, components are joined end to end in one loop; in parallel, each component is on its own branch connected across the supply.
  • The current is the same at every point in a series circuit.
  • For a parallel circuit, the current from the source is larger than the current in each branch.

Combining cells and resistors in series

  • The combined e.m.f. of several sources in series is the sum of their e.m.f.s, as long as they all point the same way. A cell connected the wrong way round subtracts its e.m.f.
  • The combined resistance of resistors in series is the sum of the individual resistances:
R = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + …
Worked example
Three 1.5 V cells are connected in series, but one of them is the wrong way round. What is the combined e.m.f.? [1]
1.5 + 1.5 − 1.5 = 1.5 V

Resistors in parallel

The combined resistance of two resistors in parallel is less than that of either resistor by itself. Adding a branch gives the current an extra path, so more current flows from the same supply.

Supplement Calculating resistance in parallel

1 ÷ R = 1 ÷ R₁ + 1 ÷ R₂

For two resistors this is the same as R = (R₁ × R₂) ÷ (R₁ + R₂), “product over sum”.

Why do currents at a junction add up? Charge is conserved: it cannot be created or destroyed, and it cannot pile up at a junction. So every coulomb per second that flows in must flow out again along the branches.

A 4.0 Ω resistor and an 8.0 Ω resistor are connected in series to two 1.5 V cells. Calculate (a) the current and (b) the p.d. across each resistor. [4]

I = V ÷ R = 3.0 ÷ 12 = 0.25 A
(b) V₁ = I R₁ = 0.25 × 4.0 = 1.0 V; V₂ = 0.25 × 8.0 = 2.0 V
Check: 1.0 + 2.0 = 3.0 V, the total e.m.f.
Worked example Supplement
A 6.0 Ω resistor and a 3.0 Ω resistor are connected in parallel to a 6.0 V supply. Calculate (a) the combined resistance, (b) the current in each resistor and (c) the current from the supply. [5]
(a) 1/R = 1/6.0 + 1/3.0 = 0.5, so R = 2.0 Ω
(b) Each branch has the full 6.0 V across it.
I₁ = 6.0 ÷ 6.0 = 1.0 A; I₂ = 6.0 ÷ 3.0 = 2.0 A
(c) I = 1.0 + 2.0 = 3.0 A (check: 6.0 ÷ 2.0 = 3.0 A)
Common Mistake
Forgetting the last step in a parallel calculation: 1/R = 0.5 gives R = 2.0 Ω, not 0.5 Ω. Sense check: the combined resistance must be smaller than the smallest resistor in the arrangement.

Why lamps in a house are wired in parallel

  • Each lamp gets the full supply voltage, so it works at its normal brightness.
  • Each lamp can be switched on and off independently.
  • If one lamp breaks (or its filament blows), the others stay on, because their branches are still complete.

Potential Dividers and Sensor Circuits

How p.d. is shared between components, and how a potential divider with a thermistor or an LDR makes an automatic sensor that responds to temperature or light.

Topical Past Papers for this topic46 real exam questions on Electricity and Magnetism, with model answers.Practise →

P.d. and resistance

For a constant current, the p.d. across an electrical conductor increases as its resistance increases. This follows from V = I R: if I stays the same, a bigger R means a bigger V.

Worked example
A current of 0.20 A flows through a 10 Ω resistor and a 30 Ω resistor in series. Calculate the p.d. across each. [2]
V = I R: 0.20 × 10 = 2.0 V; 0.20 × 30 = 6.0 V
The resistor with three times the resistance has three times the p.d.

So in any series circuit, the supply p.d. is shared out between the components in the same ratio as their resistances. The biggest resistance gets the biggest share.

How thermistors and LDRs behave

NTC stands for negative temperature coefficient: as temperature goes up, resistance goes down.

Supplement The potential divider

A potential divider is two resistors (or a resistor with a sliding contact) in series across a supply. The supply p.d. is divided between them in the ratio of their resistances, so you can take off a smaller output p.d. across one of them.

R₁ ÷ R₂ = V₁ ÷ V₂
R₁, R₂
The two resistances in series, in Ω
V₁, V₂

Worked example Supplement
A 2.0 kΩ resistor and a 4.0 kΩ resistor are connected in series to a 12 V battery. Calculate the p.d. across each resistor. [3]
R₁ ÷ R₂ = 2.0 ÷ 4.0 = 1 : 2, so V₁ : V₂ = 1 : 2
The 12 V splits into 3 equal parts of 4.0 V
V₁ = 4.0 V across the 2.0 kΩ resistor; V₂ = 8.0 V across the 4.0 kΩ resistor

Supplement The variable potential divider

  • A variable potential divider (potentiometer) is a single resistor with a sliding contact. The output p.d. is taken between one end and the slider.
  • Moving the slider changes the resistance of the section you take the output from, so the output p.d. can be set to any value from zero up to the full supply p.d.
  • This is different from a variable resistor in series, which can reduce the p.d. across a component but never all the way to zero.
  • Replacing one of two fixed resistors with a thermistor or an LDR makes the output p.d. change automatically with temperature or light. This is how sensor circuits switch on a heater, a fan or a street light.
Worked example Supplement
In the temperature sensor above, the supply is 9.0 V and the fixed resistor is 10 kΩ. The thermistor’s resistance is 20 kΩ when cold and 5.0 kΩ when warm. Calculate the output p.d. across the thermistor in each case and describe the change. [4]
Cold: total R = 10 + 20 = 30 kΩ; Vout = 9.0 × 20 ÷ 30 = 6.0 V
Warm: total R = 10 + 5.0 = 15 kΩ; Vout = 9.0 × 5.0 ÷ 15 = 3.0 V
As the temperature rises the thermistor’s resistance falls, so it gets a smaller share of the supply p.d. and the output p.d. falls.
Examiner Tip
Explain sensor circuits as a chain: condition changes → resistance of sensor changes → its share of the p.d. changes → output p.d. changes. To make the output rise instead of fall, take the output across the fixed resistor, or swap the two components round.
Common Mistake
Saying “the thermistor takes more current”. In a potential divider the two parts are in series, so they always carry the same current. What changes is how the p.d. is shared.

Other components in action

  • Relay: a small current through the relay coil works a switch in a second circuit. This lets a low-current sensor circuit safely switch a high-current device such as a motor or a heater. (How it works: see Magnetic Effect & Motors.)
  • Fuse: melts if the current is too large, protecting the circuit (see Electrical Safety).
  • Supplement Diode and LED: conduct in the forward direction only. An LED in series with a resistor is often used as an indicator lamp; the resistor limits the current so the LED is not damaged.

Electrical Safety

Mains electricity can kill or start fires. This section covers the hazards, the three wires, and how fuses, trip switches, earthing and double insulation keep people safe.

Hazards of using the mains supply

HazardWhy it is dangerous
Damaged insulationBare live wires can be touched, giving an electric shock. Bare wires can also touch each other, causing a short circuit, a very large current and a fire.
Overheating cablesA cable carrying more current than it is designed for gets hot. The insulation can melt and the cable can start a fire.
Damp conditionsWater (with dissolved impurities) conducts electricity. Wet hands or wet appliances give a path for current through a person, causing a shock.
Excess current from overloading plugs, extension leads, single and multiple socketsPlugging too many appliances into one socket or extension lead makes the total current too large. The plug, lead and wiring overheat, which can cause a fire.

The three wires in a mains circuit

WireColour (UK/Malaysia standard)Job
Live wire (line wire)BrownCarries the alternating high voltage from the supply to the appliance.
Neutral wireBlueCompletes the circuit back to the supply; it stays at (close to) zero volts.
Earth wireGreen and yellow stripesA safety wire connected to the metal case. It normally carries no current.

Why the switch must be in the live wire

When the switch is open, it must cut the appliance off from the high voltage. If the switch were in the neutral wire, the appliance would stop working when switched off, but its parts would still be connected to the live wire at a high voltage. Anyone touching a faulty part, for example while changing a bulb, could get a shock. With the switch in the live wire, the appliance is safe once it is switched off.

Fuses and trip switches

  • A fuse is a short, thin piece of wire in the live wire. If the current goes above the fuse rating, the wire heats up and melts, breaking the circuit and cutting off the live supply. A blown fuse must be replaced.
  • A trip switch (circuit breaker) does the same job but uses an electromagnet: a current above its setting makes the electromagnet strong enough to pull a switch open. It acts faster than a fuse and can simply be reset once the fault is fixed.
  • Both protect the wiring and the appliance from too large a current, which would cause overheating and fire.

Choosing a fuse rating or trip setting

Work out the normal current with I = P ÷ V, then choose the fuse rating (or trip switch setting) slightly higher than this current. Common plug fuse ratings are 3 A, 5 A and 13 A.

Worked example
A 2000 W heater is connected to the 230 V mains. Fuses of rating 3 A, 5 A and 13 A are available. Which should be used? [3]
I = P ÷ V = 2000 ÷ 230 = 8.7 A
3 A and 5 A would melt during normal use. Choose the 13 A fuse, the smallest rating above 8.7 A.
Worked example
A 2300 W kettle and a 1200 W toaster are plugged into the same 13 A extension lead on a 230 V supply. Explain whether this is safe. [3]
Total power = 2300 + 1200 = 3500 W
I = P ÷ V = 3500 ÷ 230 = 15.2 A
This is more than 13 A, so the lead is overloaded: it could overheat and start a fire (or its fuse will blow). Not safe.
Common Mistake
Choosing a fuse rating far bigger than needed “to be safe”. A 13 A fuse in a lamp that needs 0.26 A would not blow until the current was huge, and the thin lamp flex could overheat first. Pick the smallest rating that is above the normal current.

Earthing and double insulation

The outer casing of an appliance must be either non-conducting (double-insulated) or earthed, so that a person touching the case cannot get a shock if something goes wrong inside.

  • Earthed metal case: if the live wire comes loose and touches the case, a large current flows from live, through the case and down the low-resistance earth wire. This large current blows the fuse (or trips the switch) and cuts off the live supply. The case never stays live, so anyone touching it is safe.
  • Double-insulated appliance: the case is made of an insulator such as plastic, and the live parts are insulated from it twice over. The case cannot become live, so no earth wire is needed. Such appliances have a two-core cable and show the symbol of a square inside a square.
  • A fuse without an earth wire still protects the circuit and the cabling for a double-insulated appliance: it melts if the current becomes too large, for example in a short circuit.
Examiner Tip
For “explain how the earth wire and fuse protect the user”, the full chain gets full marks: live touches case → large current flows to earth → fuse melts → circuit is broken / case is no longer live → no shock. Saying “the current goes to earth instead of through you” alone is usually not enough.

Electromagnetic Induction and the A.C. Generator

How moving a magnet or a wire produces a voltage without any battery. This is how almost all of the world’s electricity is generated.

Topical Past Papers for this topic46 real exam questions on Electricity and Magnetism, with model answers.Practise →

Inducing an e.m.f.

An e.m.f. can be induced in a conductor when:

  • the conductor moves across a magnetic field (cutting through the field lines), or
  • a changing magnetic field links with the conductor (for example a magnet moving in or out of a coil, or the field of an electromagnet being switched on or off).

If the conductor is part of a complete circuit, the induced e.m.f. drives an induced current. No relative movement (or no change in the field) means no induced e.m.f.

Experiment: demonstrating electromagnetic induction

1
Set upConnect a coil of insulated wire to a sensitive centre-zero meter (galvanometer), so the needle can move either way.
2
Push the magnet inPush the N pole of a bar magnet into the coil. The needle flicks one way, showing an induced current.
3
Hold it stillHold the magnet still inside the coil. The needle returns to zero: no movement, no induced current.
4
Pull it outPull the magnet out. The needle flicks the opposite way. Turning the magnet round (S pole first) also reverses the deflection.

A second version: move a straight wire, connected to the meter, quickly down between the poles of a U-shaped magnet. The needle deflects. Moving the wire up gives a deflection the other way; moving it along the field lines (from pole to pole) gives none.

Factors affecting the size of the induced e.m.f.

The induced e.m.f. is larger when:

  • the magnet or conductor moves faster (the field changes more quickly)
  • a stronger magnet is used
  • the coil has more turns
Examiner Tip
All three factors come down to one idea: how quickly field lines are cut by, or change through, the conductor. If a question asks for the effect of moving the magnet more slowly, the answer is a smaller deflection (but in the same direction).

Supplement Direction of the induced e.m.f.

The direction of an induced e.m.f. opposes the change causing it.

  • Push an N pole into a coil: the induced current makes the end of the coil nearest the magnet an N pole, which repels the incoming magnet.
  • Pull the N pole out: the induced current reverses, the near end becomes an S pole, which attracts the magnet and opposes its removal.
  • So you must always do work to move the magnet. That work is the source of the electrical energy: this is why induction does not break the principle of conservation of energy.

Supplement Relative directions: Fleming’s right-hand rule

For a straight wire moving across a magnetic field, the motion (force applied), the field and the induced current are all at right angles to each other. Hold your right hand with thumb, first finger and second finger all at right angles:

Thumb
Motion of the wire (thuMb = Motion)
First finger
Field, from N to S (First = Field)
Second finger
Induced conventional current (seCond = Current)

The left-hand version, for motors, is in the Magnetic Effect & Motors section, with a sketch of both rules.

Model answer Supplement
The N pole of a magnet is pushed into a coil connected to a meter. Explain why there is a current and state the polarity of the end of the coil nearest the magnet. [3]
As the magnet moves, the magnetic field linking the coil changes, so an e.m.f. is induced in the coil, and because the circuit is complete a current flows. The induced e.m.f. opposes the change causing it, so the near end of the coil becomes an N pole and repels the approaching magnet.

Supplement The a.c. generator

  • Rotating-coil generator: a coil of wire is turned between the poles of a magnet. As each side of the coil cuts through the field lines, an e.m.f. is induced. Each side moves up then down every turn, so the e.m.f. reverses every half turn: the output is a.c.
  • Slip rings and brushes: each end of the coil is joined to its own complete metal ring (slip ring) that turns with the coil. Carbon brushes press against the rings and make a sliding contact, so the coil can keep rotating without twisting the output wires.
  • Rotating-magnet generator: the magnet turns and the coil stays still (as in a bicycle dynamo or a power-station alternator). The output wires connect directly to the fixed coil, so no slip rings or brushes are needed.

Linking the graph to the coil position

Plane of coil perpendicular to the field (vertical, for a horizontal field)Sides move along the field lines, not cutting themZero
Plane of coil parallel to the field (horizontal)Sides move straight across the field lines, cutting them fastestMaximum (a peak or a trough)
Next parallel position, half a turn laterEach side now moves the opposite way through the fieldMaximum in the opposite direction
  • One complete turn of the coil gives one complete cycle of the a.c. graph.
  • Turning the coil faster gives higher peaks (bigger e.m.f.) and more cycles per second (higher frequency). A stronger magnet or more turns raises the peaks but does not change the frequency.
Common Mistake
Thinking the e.m.f. is biggest when the coil faces the poles “full on” (perpendicular to the field). At that instant the sides are moving parallel to the field lines and cut none, so the e.m.f. is zero. The e.m.f. peaks when the plane of the coil is parallel to the field.

Magnetic Effect of a Current and the Motor Effect

A current makes a magnetic field, and a current in a magnetic field feels a force. These two facts explain relays, loudspeakers and electric motors.

Topical Past Papers for this topic46 real exam questions on Electricity and Magnetism, with model answers.Practise →

Magnetic field of a straight wire and a solenoid

  • Straight wire: the field lines are concentric circles around the wire, in planes at right angles to it.
  • Direction (right-hand grip rule): grip the wire with your right hand with the thumb pointing along the conventional current. Your fingers curl the way the field lines go. Current out of the page (⊙) gives an anticlockwise field; current into the page (⊗) gives a clockwise field.
  • Solenoid (a long coil of wire): outside, the field has the same shape as a bar magnet’s; inside, the field lines are straight, parallel and evenly spaced.
  • Which end is N? Grip the solenoid with your right hand with the fingers curling the way the current goes round the turns. Your thumb points to the N pole. Or look at an end: current going anticlockwise means that end is N; clockwise means S.

Experiment: field pattern around a wire or solenoid

1
Set upPass a straight wire vertically through a hole in a horizontal piece of card (for a solenoid, thread the coil through the card so its axis lies along the card). Connect it to a d.c. supply that can give a large current, through a switch.
2
PatternSprinkle iron filings on the card, switch on and tap the card gently. The filings line up to show the pattern. Switch off soon afterwards so the wire does not overheat.
3
DirectionPlace small plotting compasses at several points on the card. The N end of each needle shows the field direction there. Reverse the current: every needle turns round.

Supplement Strength of the field and the effect of the current

  • Straight wire: the field is strongest close to the wire and gets weaker with distance from it; the circles are drawn further apart further out.
  • Solenoid: the field is strong and uniform inside (same strength everywhere), and much weaker outside.
  • Increasing the current makes the field stronger at every point (draw the lines closer together). Decreasing it makes the field weaker.
  • Reversing the current reverses the direction of the field (the pattern stays the same). For a solenoid, the N and S poles swap ends.
Examiner Tip
To make an electromagnet stronger, give three ways: more current, more turns on the coil, and a soft-iron core inside the coil. Soft iron is chosen because it is magnetised strongly while the current flows and loses its magnetism when the current stops.

Using the magnetic effect: relays and loudspeakers

  • Relay: a small current in the relay coil turns the soft-iron core into an electromagnet. It attracts an iron armature, which closes (or opens) a pair of contacts in a separate circuit. When the current stops, the core loses its magnetism and a spring pulls the armature back. Applications: a car’s ignition key uses a small current to switch the very large current to the starter motor; a sensor circuit can switch a mains heater or a pump; the circuit you touch is kept separate from the dangerous high-voltage one.
  • Loudspeaker: a coil is fixed to a paper or plastic cone and sits in the field of a permanent magnet. An alternating current from the amplifier flows in the coil. The force on the coil keeps reversing as the current reverses, so the coil and cone vibrate in and out at the frequency of the current and produce a sound wave. Applications: headphones, phones, radios, PA systems.

Force on a current-carrying conductor

A wire carrying a current in a magnetic field experiences a force (as long as the wire is not parallel to the field). This is the motor effect. It happens because the field of the current interacts with the field of the magnet.

Experiment: showing the force

1
Set upLay two horizontal copper rails between the poles of a U-shaped magnet and connect them to a low-voltage d.c. supply. Rest a light copper rod across the rails, at right angles to the field.
2
Switch onThe rod rolls along the rails: a force acts on it, at right angles to both the current and the field.
3
Reverse the currentSwap the supply connections. The rod rolls the other way: reversing the current reverses the force.
4
Reverse the fieldTurn the magnet over so the poles swap. The force reverses again: reversing the field reverses the force. Reversing both together leaves the force in the original direction.

Supplement Relative directions: Fleming’s left-hand rule

  • The force, the magnetic field and the current are all at right angles. On your left hand: thuMb = Motion (force), First finger = Field (N to S), seCond finger = Current (conventional, + to −).
  • If the current is parallel to the field, there is no force.

Supplement Beams of charged particles

  • A beam of charged particles moving through a magnetic field is a current, so it feels a force and its path bends (into a circular arc).
  • For a beam of positive particles (such as alpha particles), point the second finger the way the particles move.
  • For a beam of electrons (or beta particles), conventional current is in the opposite direction to their motion, so point the second finger backwards along the beam.
Worked example Supplement
A beam of electrons travels from left to right across the page. A magnetic field acts into the page. State the direction of the force on the electrons. [2]
Electrons move left to right, so conventional current is right to left.
Left hand: first finger into the page (field), second finger pointing left (current). The thumb points down.
The force on the electrons is towards the bottom of the page, so the beam curves downwards. (A beam of positive particles moving the same way would curve upwards.)
Common Mistake
Using the right hand for the force on a wire, or forgetting to reverse the current for an electron beam. Motor effect: left hand. Electrons: current is opposite to their motion.

The d.c. motor

A current-carrying coil in a magnetic field may experience a turning effect. The turning effect is increased by increasing:

  • the number of turns on the coil
  • the current
  • the strength of the magnetic field

Reversing either the current or the field makes the motor turn the other way.

Supplement How the d.c. motor works

1
Forces on the coilCurrent flows one way along one side of the coil and the opposite way along the other side. By the left-hand rule, one side is pushed up and the other down. This pair of forces turns the coil.
2
Vertical positionWhen the coil reaches the vertical position (perpendicular to the field), the forces pull straight up and down through the axle and have no turning effect. The coil’s momentum carries it past this point.
3
Split-ring commutatorAt that moment the gaps in the split ring pass the brushes, and each half-ring swaps to the other brush. This reverses the current in the coil every half turn, so the side that is now on the left is always pushed up and the coil keeps turning the same way.
4
BrushesCarbon brushes press against the rotating commutator and make a sliding contact, connecting the spinning coil to the fixed supply.
Examiner Tip
Do not mix up the two kinds of ring. A split ring (commutator) reverses the connections every half turn: used in a d.c. motor. Slip rings keep the same connections all the time: used in an a.c. generator.
Model answer

Increase the current in the coil (for example by increasing the supply voltage). Use a coil with more turns. Use stronger magnets, so the magnetic field is stronger.

The Transformer

Transformers change the size of an alternating voltage. They make the national grid possible and are inside every phone charger.

Topical Past Papers for this topic46 real exam questions on Electricity and Magnetism, with model answers.Practise →

Construction

  • A simple transformer has two coils of insulated wire wound on a soft-iron core.
  • The primary coil is connected to the a.c. input. The secondary coil gives the a.c. output.
  • The two coils are not connected electrically: energy is passed from one to the other by the changing magnetic field in the core.
  • A step-up transformer has more turns on the secondary than on the primary, so the output voltage is higher than the input. A step-down transformer has fewer turns on the secondary, so the output voltage is lower.

The turns-ratio equation

Vp ÷ Vs = Np ÷ Ns
Vp, Vs
Voltage across the primary / secondary coil, in V
Np, Ns
Number of turns on the primary / secondary coil
Worked example
(a) A transformer has 1150 turns on its primary coil and 60 turns on its secondary. The primary is connected to the 230 V mains. Calculate the output voltage. (b) A second transformer changes 230 V to 11.5 V and has 2000 turns on its primary. How many turns are on its secondary? [4]
(a) Vs = Vp × Ns ÷ Np = 230 × 60 ÷ 1150 = 12 V (step-down)
(b) Ns = Np × Vs ÷ Vp = 2000 × 11.5 ÷ 230 = 100 turns

Supplement How a transformer works

1
Changing fieldThe alternating current in the primary coil produces a magnetic field that keeps changing in size and direction.
2
Core links the coilsThe soft-iron core is easily magnetised and demagnetised, so it carries this changing magnetic field through the secondary coil.
3
InductionThe changing magnetic field linking the secondary coil induces an alternating e.m.f. in it, at the same frequency as the input.
Common Mistake
Saying a transformer works on d.c. A steady direct current makes a steady field, and a field that does not change induces no e.m.f. in the secondary. Also avoid “current flows through the core”: it is the magnetic field that passes through the core.

Supplement Power in an ideal transformer

For a transformer that is 100% efficient, the power out equals the power in:

Ip Vp = Is Vs
Ip, Is
Current in the primary / secondary coil, in A
Vp, Vs
Voltage across the primary / secondary coil, in V

So a step-up transformer that increases the voltage reduces the current by the same factor, and a step-down transformer increases the current.

Worked example Supplement
The 12 V output of the transformer in part (a) above supplies a current of 2.0 A to a lamp. Assuming the transformer is 100% efficient, calculate the current in the primary coil. [2]
Ip = Is Vs ÷ Vp = 2.0 × 12 ÷ 230 = 0.104 A
Ip = 0.10 A

High-voltage transmission of electricity

  • At the power station, a step-up transformer raises the voltage to a very high value (hundreds of thousands of volts) for the transmission cables.
  • Near towns and homes, step-down transformers lower it in stages to the safe mains value (230 V in Malaysia and the UK).

Advantages of high-voltage transmission

  • For the same power, a higher voltage means a smaller current in the cables.
  • A smaller current means less energy is lost as heat in the cables, so transmission is more efficient.
  • Thinner, lighter and cheaper cables can be used, and pylons can be further apart.

Supplement Why power losses are smaller at high voltage

P = I2 R
P
Power lost as heat in the cables, in W
I
Current in the cables, in A
R
Resistance of the cables, in Ω

Since P = IV, transmitting the same power at a higher voltage needs a smaller current. The power lost depends on the current squared, so making the current 10 times smaller makes the power loss 100 times smaller.

Worked example Supplement
500 kW of power is sent along cables with a total resistance of 4.0 Ω. Calculate the power lost in the cables if it is transmitted at (a) 25 kV and (b) 250 kV. [4]
(a) I = P ÷ V = 500 000 ÷ 25 000 = 20 A
Power lost = I2R = 202 × 4.0 = 1600 W
(b) I = 500 000 ÷ 250 000 = 2.0 A
Power lost = 2.02 × 4.0 = 16 W
Ten times the voltage gives one-tenth of the current and one-hundredth of the power loss.
Examiner Tip
In the cable-loss formula, use the current in the cable and the resistance of the cable. Do not use the transmission voltage in P = I2R or V2/R: the 25 kV is across the whole system, not across the cables.

The structure of the atom

  • An atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus at its centre, made of protons and neutrons.
  • Electrons (negatively charged) orbit the nucleus. Most of the atom is empty space.
  • An atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so it is neutral overall.
  • An atom that loses electrons becomes a positive ion; one that gains electrons becomes a negative ion.

Radioactivity

The three types of nuclear radiation, how they are detected, how nuclei change when they decay, how to work with half-life, and how to use radioactive sources safely.

Topical Past Papers for this topic18 real exam questions on Nuclear Physics, with model answers.Practise →

Detecting radiation and background radiation

  • Ionising nuclear radiation is measured with a detector such as a Geiger–Müller (GM) tube connected to a counter. The count rate is given in counts per second or counts per minute.
  • Background radiation is the low level of radiation around us all the time. Its sources include:
    • radon gas in the air (from rocks and soil)
    • rocks and buildings
    • food and drink
    • cosmic rays from space

Supplement Correcting for background

Measure the background count rate with no source present, then subtract it from every reading taken with the source. This gives the corrected count rate due to the source alone.

The three types of radiation

  • Radioactive decay is a change in an unstable nucleus. It can give out an alpha particle or a beta particle, and/or gamma radiation.
  • Decay is spontaneous (nothing triggers it) and random: you can’t predict which nucleus will decay next or when, and the radiation is given out in random directions.

To identify a source in an experiment: if the count drops when paper is added, it emits alpha. If it drops when aluminium is added (but not paper), beta. If neither makes much difference, gamma.

Supplement Deflection in electric and magnetic fields

  • Alpha and beta are charged, so they are deflected by electric and magnetic fields, in opposite directions.
  • Beta is deflected more than alpha because a beta particle has a much smaller mass.
  • Gamma is uncharged, so it is not deflected.
  • In an electric field, alpha bends towards the negative plate and beta towards the positive plate.
  • In a magnetic field, alpha and beta curve in opposite directions, at right angles to both the field and their direction of travel. Use Fleming’s left-hand rule; for beta, point the “current” finger opposite to its motion because it is negative.

Supplement Why their ionising effects differ

Radiation ionises an atom by knocking electrons off it, and each ionisation uses up some of the radiation’s kinetic energy. How strongly it ionises depends on its electric charge and its kinetic energy:

  • Alpha: large charge (+2) and large mass. It has a lot of kinetic energy but moves relatively slowly, so it pulls hard on the electrons of every atom it passes and spends longer near each one. It causes many ionisations per millimetre and uses up all its kinetic energy within a few cm of air.
  • Beta: smaller charge (−1) and a tiny mass, so it moves much faster. It pulls less strongly and spends less time near each atom, so it ionises less and travels further.
  • Gamma: no charge (it is an electromagnetic wave), so it rarely interacts with atoms. It ionises least and penetrates furthest.

Radioactive decay

  • In alpha and beta decay the number of protons changes, so the nucleus becomes a different element.
  • Gamma emission does not change the proton or nucleon number. The nucleus just loses energy.

Supplement Why isotopes decay and what happens in beta decay

  • Isotopes of an element can be radioactive because the nucleus has an excess of neutrons and/or because the nucleus is too heavy.
  • Every decay makes the nucleus more stable. Alpha decay makes a heavy nucleus lighter. Beta decay reduces the number of excess neutrons. Gamma emission just removes surplus energy.
  • In beta decay, a neutron changes into a proton and an electron. The proton stays in the nucleus and the electron is emitted as the beta particle. That is why Z goes up by 1 but A stays the same.

Half-life

The half-life of a radioactive isotope is the time taken for half the nuclei in a sample to decay. Equivalently, it is the time taken for the count rate (activity) to fall to half its value.

Worked example
A sample has a count rate of 800 counts/min. Its half-life is 3 hours. What is the count rate after 12 hours?
Number of half-lives = 12 ÷ 3 = 4
800 → 400 → 200 → 100 → 50 counts/min
Worked example Supplement
A detector reads 520 counts/min near a source. The background count rate is 40 counts/min. After 10 days the reading is 160 counts/min. Find the half-life.
Corrected count rates: 520 − 40 = 480, and 160 − 40 = 120
480 → 240 → 120 is 2 half-lives in 10 days
Half-life = 10 ÷ 2 = 5 days
Common Mistake
Thinking a sample is completely gone after two half-lives. After 1 half-life ½ is left, after 2 half-lives ¼, after 3 half-lives ⅛: it never quite reaches zero. In Supplement questions, also remember to subtract background before halving.

Safety

  • Ionising radiation can kill cells, cause mutations (damage to DNA) and lead to cancer.
  • Keep exposure as low as possible:
    • Reduce exposure time: use the source for as short a time as possible.
    • Increase distance: handle sources with long tongs and never point them at people.
    • Use shielding: store sources in lead-lined boxes and use lead or concrete barriers.

Supplement Explaining the safety precautions

  • Shorter time: fewer particles or rays reach the body, so less ionisation and lower risk.
  • Greater distance: radiation spreads out (and alpha and beta are absorbed by air), so less reaches the body.
  • Shielding: dense materials like lead absorb the radiation before it reaches people.
  • Alpha sources are most dangerous inside the body (if swallowed or breathed in), because alpha is so strongly ionising. Outside the body, skin stops it.

Seasons

The Earth’s axis is tilted (by about 23°) and always points the same way in space as the Earth orbits the Sun. When your hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun, it is summer: the Sun is higher in the sky, its energy is more concentrated on each square metre of ground and the days are longer. Six months later your hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun and it is winter.

Common Mistake
Saying summer happens because the Earth is closer to the Sun. The distance hardly changes. It is the tilt that matters, which is why the two hemispheres have opposite seasons at the same time.

Phases of the Moon

  • The Moon gives out no light of its own. We see it because it reflects sunlight.
  • The half of the Moon facing the Sun is always lit. As the Moon orbits the Earth, we see different amounts of that lit half.
  • New Moon: the Moon is between the Earth and the Sun, so its lit side faces away from us and we see almost nothing.
  • Full Moon: the Earth is between the Sun and the Moon, so we see the whole lit half.
  • The cycle from new Moon to full Moon and back takes about a month, showing that the Moon orbits the Earth once a month.

What is in the Solar System

  • One star: the Sun.
  • Eight planets orbiting the Sun. In order from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.
  • Minor planets orbiting the Sun, including dwarf planets such as Pluto, and asteroids in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter.
  • Moons (natural satellites), which orbit planets.
  • Smaller bodies, including comets: lumps of ice and dust with very stretched (elliptical) orbits.

How the Solar System formed (the accretion model)

1
A cloud of gas and dustThe Solar System began as a huge interstellar cloud of gas and dust containing many elements (mostly hydrogen and helium, plus heavier elements made in earlier stars). The cloud collapsed under its own gravity.
2
A spinning accretion disc formsThe cloud was rotating. Most of the material fell to the centre and formed the Sun. The rest flattened into a spinning disc around it, called an accretion disc.
3
Particles clump together (accretion)Gravity pulled particles in the disc together into larger and larger lumps, which became the planets.
4
Rocky inside, gas outsideNear the Sun it was too hot for gases and ices to collect, so only rock and metal could clump together, making small rocky planets. Further out it was cold enough for gases and ice to collect too, so the outer planets grew much larger and are mostly gas.

Light travelling across the Solar System

Distances in space are so large that even light takes time to cross them. Use speed = distance ÷ time with the speed of light, 3.0 × 108 m/s.

Worked example
The Earth is 1.5 × 1011 m from the Sun. How long does sunlight take to reach the Earth?
time = distance ÷ speed = 1.5 × 1011 ÷ 3.0 × 108
time = 500 s (just over 8 minutes)

Gravity and orbits

  • The gravitational attraction of the Sun is the force that keeps the planets, minor planets and comets in orbit.
  • The Sun contains most of the mass of the Solar System (over 99%). This is why the planets orbit the Sun.
  • The gravitational field strength at the surface of a planet depends on the planet’s mass.
  • The gravitational field around a planet gets weaker as the distance from the planet increases.

Supplement Elliptical orbits

  • Planets, minor planets and comets move in elliptical orbits (stretched circles). Most planets’ orbits are very nearly circular; comets’ are very stretched.
  • The Sun is not at the centre of an elliptical orbit: it sits off to one side, nearer one end. Only when the orbit is approximately circular is the Sun at the centre.

Supplement Using planetary data

Exam questions often give a table of data about the planets (distance from the Sun, orbital time, density, surface temperature, gravitational field strength) and ask you to spot patterns. Look for:

  • The further a planet is from the Sun, the longer its orbit takes and (generally) the colder its surface. Venus is the exception for temperature: its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps energy.
  • The inner rocky planets have higher densities than the outer gas giants.
  • Planets with more mass generally have a stronger gravitational field at their surface (Jupiter’s is the strongest). Background A planet’s size matters too, so the pattern is not perfect: Uranus has about 15 times the Earth’s mass but a slightly weaker surface field.
Examiner Tip
When describing a pattern from data, quote numbers from the table to back it up, and mention any planet that doesn’t fit the pattern.

Supplement The Sun’s field and orbital speeds

  • The strength of the Sun’s gravitational field decreases as the distance from the Sun increases.
  • So the orbital speeds of the planets decrease the further they are from the Sun. Outer planets also have further to travel, so their orbits take much longer (Mercury about 88 days, Neptune about 165 years).

v = 2πr ÷ T
v

2πr is the distance round a circular orbit, so this is just speed = distance ÷ time.

Worked example
The Earth orbits the Sun at an average radius of 1.5 × 1011 m once every 365 days. Calculate its average orbital speed. [3]
T = 365 × 24 × 60 × 60 = 3.15 × 107 s
v = 2πr ÷ T = 2 × π × 1.5 × 1011 ÷ 3.15 × 107

Common Mistake
Leaving T in days or hours, or r in km. Convert to seconds and metres first (km × 1000).

Supplement Speed in an elliptical orbit

  • An object in an elliptical orbit (such as a comet) moves fastest when it is closest to the Sun and slowest when it is furthest away.
  • Why (conservation of energy): as the comet moves towards the Sun, it loses gravitational potential energy, which is converted to kinetic energy, so it speeds up. As it moves away, kinetic energy is converted back to gravitational potential energy, so it slows down. The total energy stays the same.
  • In a circular orbit the distance from the Sun stays the same, so the speed is constant, but the velocity changes all the time because the direction of motion keeps changing.

Stars and the Universe

What the Sun is and what powers it, how stars are born and die, how big the Universe is, and the evidence that it began with the Big Bang.

Topical Past Papers for this topic12 real exam questions on Space Physics, with model answers.Practise →

The Sun

  • The Sun is a star of medium size.
  • It is made mostly of hydrogen and helium.
  • It radiates most of its energy as infrared, visible light and ultraviolet. Visible light is the peak; infrared has longer wavelengths and ultraviolet shorter.
  • Supplement Stars are powered by nuclear reactions that release energy. In stable stars like the Sun, the reaction is the fusion of hydrogen nuclei into helium.

Galaxies and the light-year

  • A galaxy is made up of many billions of stars.
  • The Sun is one of the stars in our galaxy, the Milky Way.
  • Other stars in the Milky Way are much further from the Earth than the Sun is. The nearest, Proxima Centauri, is about 4 light-years away.
  • The Milky Way is one of many billions of galaxies in the Universe. Its diameter is about 100 000 light-years.

Astronomical distances can be measured in light-years. A light-year is the distance travelled by light in (the vacuum of) space in one year.

Supplement One light-year = 9.5 × 1015 m. Learn this value.

Common Mistake
A light-year is a distance, not a time, even though it has “year” in the name.
Worked example Supplement
Show that one light-year is about 9.5 × 1015 m.
One year = 365 × 24 × 60 × 60 = 3.15 × 107 s
distance = speed × time = 3.0 × 108 × 3.15 × 107 = 9.5 × 1015 m

Supplement The life cycle of a star

1
NebulaA star forms from an interstellar cloud of gas and dust, mostly hydrogen, which collapses under gravity.
2
ProtostarAs the cloud collapses, its temperature rises. A protostar is unstable: it is still collapsing and heating up.
3
Stable starWhen the centre is hot enough, hydrogen fusion begins. The star is stable when the inward force of gravity is balanced by the outward force due to the high temperature in its centre. It stays like this for most of its life.
4
Running out of hydrogenAll stars eventually run out of hydrogen fuel. When most of the hydrogen in the centre has been converted to helium, the star expands. A star like the Sun becomes a red giant; a much more massive star becomes a red supergiant.
5
Smaller stars (like the Sun)The red giant throws off its outer layers as a planetary nebula, leaving a small, hot core called a white dwarf.
6
Massive starsThe red supergiant explodes as a supernova, leaving a neutron star or, for the most massive stars, a black hole.
7
RecyclingA supernova throws out a nebula containing hydrogen and new, heavier elements. This material may later form new stars with orbiting planets. The Solar System formed from material like this.
Common Mistake
Mixing up the two nebulas. A planetary nebula comes from a red giant (smaller stars) and has nothing to do with planets. A supernova comes only from massive stars (red supergiants).

The expanding Universe and redshift

  • Redshift is an increase in the observed wavelength of electromagnetic radiation from stars and galaxies that are moving away from us. The light is shifted towards the red end of the spectrum.
  • Light from distant galaxies is redshifted compared with light from the same elements on Earth. So the galaxies are moving away from us.
  • This shows the Universe is expanding and supports the Big Bang Theory: the Universe began from a single point a long time ago and has been expanding ever since.

Supplement Cosmic microwave background radiation (CMBR)

  • The CMBR is microwave radiation of a specific frequency that is detected coming from every direction in space.
  • It was produced shortly after the Universe formed, when the Universe was extremely hot. As the Universe expanded, the wavelength of this radiation was stretched until it reached the microwave region.
  • It is further evidence for the Big Bang.

Supplement The Hubble constant and the age of the Universe

  • The speed v at which a galaxy is moving away is found from the change in wavelength of its light (its redshift).
  • The distance d to a far galaxy can be found from the brightness of a supernova in it: supernovae of a particular type all have about the same true brightness, so a dimmer one is further away.
  • The further away a galaxy is, the faster it is moving away. The ratio is the Hubble constant, H0, currently estimated at about 2.2 × 10−18 per second.
H0 = v ÷ d

On a graph of speed (y-axis) against distance (x-axis) for galaxies, the gradient is H0.

age of the Universe ≈ d ÷ v = 1 ÷ H0

Why: if a galaxy has always moved away at speed v, then d ÷ v is the time it has taken to travel distance d from the point where everything started. Because d ÷ v is the same for every galaxy, everything was together at a single point at that time. This is evidence that the Universe expanded from a single point.

Worked example
A galaxy is moving away from the Earth at 6.6 × 106 m/s. Taking H0 = 2.2 × 10−18 /s, find (a) its distance and (b) the age of the Universe in years.
(a) d = v ÷ H0 = 6.6 × 106 ÷ 2.2 × 10−18 = 3.0 × 1024 m
(b) age = 1 ÷ H0 = 1 ÷ 2.2 × 10−18 = 4.5 × 1017 s
In years: 4.5 × 1017 ÷ 3.15 × 107 = 1.4 × 1010 years (about 14 billion years)

Example 1: Reading a speed–time graph

Practice question · [10 marks]

The graph shows the motion of an MRT train between two stations. (a) Describe the motion of the train between 8 s and 28 s. (b) (Supplement) Calculate the acceleration of the train in the first 8 s. (c) Calculate the distance between the two stations. (d) Calculate the average speed of the train for the whole journey. (e) (Supplement) Calculate the acceleration between 28 s and 34 s and say what the sign means.

Answer:

  1. (a) The train moves at a constant speed of 12 m/s; the line is horizontal, so it is not accelerating (1).
  2. (b) a = Δv ÷ Δt = (12 − 0) ÷ 8 (1) = 1.5 m/s2 (1). This is the gradient of the first section.
  3. (c) Distance = area under the graph. A (triangle) = ½ × 8 × 12 = 48 m; B (rectangle) = 20 × 12 = 240 m; C (triangle) = ½ × 6 × 12 = 36 m (2). Total = 48 + 240 + 36 = 324 m (1).
  4. (d) Average speed = total distance ÷ total time = 324 ÷ 34 (1) = 9.5 m/s (1).
  5. (e) a = (0 − 12) ÷ (34 − 28) = −2.0 m/s2 (1). The negative sign shows a deceleration: the train is slowing down at 2.0 m/s2 (1).
Common Mistake
Finding the average speed by averaging the speeds, (0 + 12) ÷ 2 = 6 m/s. Average speed is always total distance ÷ total time. Also check which graph you have: the area gives distance only on a speed–time graph, and on a distance–time graph the gradient gives the speed.

Example 2: Balancing a see-saw

Practice question · [8 marks]

Aina and Ben sit on a see-saw in a playground. The plank is uniform and is pivoted at its centre. (a) Calculate the distance d from the pivot at which Ben must sit so that the see-saw balances. (b) (Supplement) Aina puts her school bag, weight 200 N, on the plank 0.9 m from the pivot on her side. Calculate the new distance at which Ben must sit. (c) State the two conditions for the see-saw to be in equilibrium. (d) Explain why the weight of the plank does not appear in your calculations.

Answer:

  1. (a) Principle of moments: clockwise moment = anticlockwise moment (1). 600 × d = 450 × 1.6 = 720 N m (1), so d = 720 ÷ 600 = 1.2 m (1).
  2. (b) Total anticlockwise moment = (450 × 1.6) + (200 × 0.9) = 720 + 180 = 900 N m (1). 600 × d = 900, so d = 1.5 m (1).
  3. (c) There is no resultant force (1) and no resultant moment (1) on the see-saw.
  4. (d) The plank is uniform, so its weight acts at its centre, which is at the pivot. The perpendicular distance is zero, so its moment about the pivot is zero (1).
Examiner Tip
Always state the principle first (“sum of clockwise moments = sum of anticlockwise moments”) and work out every moment about the same point, the pivot. Keep the units consistent: if one distance is in cm, put them all in cm, and the moment is then in N cm.

Supplement Example 3: A collision and impulse

Practice question · [9 marks]

Trolley A, mass 2.0 kg, moves at 3.0 m/s towards trolley B, mass 1.0 kg, which is at rest. Velcro pads make the trolleys stick together when they collide. (a) Calculate the momentum of A before the collision. (b) Calculate the velocity of the trolleys just after the collision. (c) Calculate the impulse on trolley B. (d) The collision lasts 0.040 s. Calculate the average force on B. (e) Show that kinetic energy is not conserved in this collision, and suggest where the missing energy goes.

Answer:

  1. (a) p = mv = 2.0 × 3.0 = 6.0 kg m/s to the right (1).
  2. (b) Momentum is conserved: total momentum after = 6.0 kg m/s (1). (2.0 + 1.0) × v = 6.0, so v = 2.0 m/s to the right (1).
  3. (c) Impulse = change in momentum of B = 1.0 × 2.0 − 1.0 × 0 = 2.0 N s to the right (1).
  4. (d) Impulse = FΔt, so F = 2.0 ÷ 0.040 (1) = 50 N (1).
  5. (e) Kinetic energy before = ½ × 2.0 × 3.02 = 9.0 J; after = ½ × 3.0 × 2.02 = 6.0 J (1). 3.0 J of kinetic energy is transferred (1), mostly to internal (thermal) energy of the trolleys and pads, and some to sound (1).
Why this scores
Momentum is always conserved in a collision when no outside force acts; kinetic energy usually is not. By Newton’s third law, A gets an equal and opposite impulse of 2.0 N s to the left: its momentum falls from 6.0 to 4.0 kg m/s. Momentum and impulse are vectors, so give a direction.

Supplement Example 4: Kettle efficiency and a Sankey diagram

Practice question · [8 marks]

A 2.0 kW electric kettle is switched on for 210 s. It heats 1.0 kg of water from 25 °C to 100 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/(kg °C). (a) Calculate the electrical energy supplied to the kettle. (b) Calculate the energy needed to heat the water. (c) Calculate the efficiency of the kettle. (d) The Sankey diagram is drawn to scale. Use it to find the energy that heats the air. (e) Suggest one way of making the kettle more efficient.

Answer:

  1. (a) P = ΔE ÷ t, so ΔE = Pt = 2000 × 210 (1) = 420 000 J (420 kJ) (1). Convert 2.0 kW to 2000 W first.
  2. (b) ΔE = mcΔθ = 1.0 × 4200 × (100 − 25) (1) = 315 000 J (315 kJ) (1).
  3. (c) Efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input × 100% = 315 000 ÷ 420 000 × 100% (1) = 75% (1).
  4. (d) Wasted energy = 420 − 315 = 105 kJ. Energy that heats the air = 105 − 63 = 42 kJ (1). Check on the diagram: that arrow is two-thirds as wide as the 63 kJ arrow.
  5. (e) Any one: insulate the body of the kettle; keep the lid closed; boil only the amount of water you need (1).
Common Mistake
Getting the efficiency upside down and writing 133%. An efficiency can never be more than 100%, so the useful output always goes on top. Also note that the energy is not “lost” or “used up”: it is transferred to the thermal stores of the kettle and the surroundings, where it is no longer useful.

Example 5: Ray diagram for a converging lens

Practice question · [9 marks]

In a simple projector, an object 3.0 cm tall stands 15 cm from a thin converging lens of focal length 10 cm. (a) Draw a ray diagram to scale to find the position of the image. (b) Determine the distance of the image from the lens and the height of the image. (c) Describe the image using three terms. (d) (Supplement) The object is moved to 6.0 cm from the lens. Describe the image now and state a use of the lens arranged like this.

Answer:

  1. (a) Draw the lens and principal axis, and mark F at 10 cm on each side. From the top of the object draw: a ray parallel to the axis that passes through F on the far side after the lens (1); a ray straight through the centre of the lens without bending (1); (optional third ray) a ray through F on the object side that leaves the lens parallel to the axis. The image is where the rays cross (1).
  2. (b) Measuring from the drawing: image distance = 30 cm from the lens (1); image height = 6.0 cm (1).
  3. (c) Real (the rays really meet, so it can be caught on a screen), inverted and enlarged (2 for all three, 1 for two).
  4. (d) The object is now closer to the lens than F, so the rays spread out after the lens. The image is virtual, upright and enlarged, on the same side of the lens as the object (1). The lens is being used as a magnifying glass (1).
Examiner Tip
Use a sharp pencil and a ruler, put an arrow on every ray, and draw rays all the way to the image. The object here is between F and 2F, which always gives a real, inverted, enlarged image beyond 2F on the other side. That is why a projector works this way round.

Example 6: Critical angle in a semicircular block

Practice question · [9 marks]

A ray of light is directed through the curved surface of a semicircular glass block towards the centre O of its flat face. The refractive index of the glass is 1.50. (a) Explain why the ray does not change direction as it enters the curved surface. (b) State what is meant by the critical angle. (c) (Supplement) Calculate the critical angle of the glass. (d) (Supplement) The ray meets the flat face at an angle of incidence of 30°. Calculate the angle of refraction r in the air. (e) The angle of incidence is increased to 50°. Describe what happens to the ray and explain why.

Answer:

  1. (a) The ray travels along a radius, so it meets the curved surface along the normal. Its angle of incidence is 0°, so it does not bend (1).
  2. (b) The critical angle is the angle of incidence in the glass for which the angle of refraction in the air is 90° (1).
  3. (c) n = 1 ÷ sin c, so sin c = 1 ÷ 1.50 = 0.667 (1), c = 42° (41.8°) (1).
  4. (d) The light goes from glass into air, so the larger angle is in the air: n = sin r ÷ sin 30° (1). sin r = 1.50 × 0.500 = 0.750, so r = 49° (48.6°) (1).
  5. (e) 50° is greater than the critical angle of 42° (1), so no light refracts into the air: there is total internal reflection (1). The ray reflects back into the glass with an angle of reflection of 50° (1).
Common Mistake
Using n = sin i ÷ sin r with the glass angle on top, which gives sin r = 0.333 and r = 19°. Light leaving glass bends away from the normal, so the angle in air must be the larger one. Total internal reflection only happens when light travels from the denser material towards the less dense one, and the angle of incidence is bigger than the critical angle.

Supplement Example 7: Currents and p.d.s in a series–parallel circuit

Practice question · [9 marks]

In the circuit shown, the battery has an e.m.f. of 12 V. Ignore the resistance of the battery, the ammeters and the wires. (a) Calculate the combined resistance of R2 and R3 in parallel. (b) Calculate the total resistance of the circuit. (c) Calculate the reading on ammeter A1. (d) Calculate the reading on the voltmeter and the p.d. across R2. (e) Calculate the readings on ammeters A2 and A3, and show that they agree with your answer to (c).

Answer:

  1. (a) 1 ÷ R = 1 ÷ 6.0 + 1 ÷ 12 = 3 ÷ 12 (1), so R = 4.0 Ω (1). It is less than either resistor alone, as it should be.
  2. (b) Series: Rtotal = 4.0 + 4.0 = 8.0 Ω (1).
  3. (c) I = V ÷ R = 12 ÷ 8.0 = 1.5 A (1).
  4. (d) Voltmeter: V = IR = 1.5 × 4.0 = 6.0 V (1). The p.d.s in series add up to 12 V, so the p.d. across the parallel pair, and therefore across R2, is 12 − 6.0 = 6.0 V (1).
  5. (e) A2: I = 6.0 ÷ 6.0 = 1.0 A (1). A3: I = 6.0 ÷ 12 = 0.50 A (1). Check: 1.0 + 0.50 = 1.5 A, the current into the junction equals the current out (1).
Examiner Tip
Work from the outside in: combine the parallel pair, add the series resistor, find the main current, then split the p.d. and the current. Each branch of a parallel arrangement has the same p.d.; the branch with the smaller resistance takes the larger current.

Example 8: A transformer in a phone charger

Practice question · [8 marks]

A phone charger contains a transformer that changes the 240 V a.c. mains supply to 6.0 V a.c. The primary coil has 2000 turns. (a) State whether this is a step-up or a step-down transformer, and why the core is made of soft iron. (b) Calculate the number of turns on the secondary coil. (c) (Supplement) The output current is 2.0 A. Assuming the transformer is 100% efficient, calculate the current in the primary coil. (d) (Supplement) Explain why the transformer would not work from a 240 V d.c. supply.

Answer:

  1. (a) Step-down, because the output voltage is lower than the input voltage (1). Soft iron is easily magnetised and demagnetised, so it carries the changing magnetic field from the primary coil to the secondary coil (1).
  2. (b) Vp ÷ Vs = Np ÷ Ns, so 240 ÷ 6.0 = 2000 ÷ Ns (1). Ns = 2000 × 6.0 ÷ 240 = 50 turns (1).
  3. (c) IpVp = IsVs, so Ip × 240 = 2.0 × 6.0 = 12 W (1). Ip = 12 ÷ 240 = 0.050 A (1).
  4. (d) An alternating current in the primary coil produces a magnetic field in the core that keeps changing; this changing field induces an alternating e.m.f. in the secondary coil (1). A direct current produces a steady field, which does not change, so no e.m.f. is induced (1).
Examiner Tip
A step-down transformer steps the voltage down but steps the current up by the same factor (here 40 times, from 0.050 A to 2.0 A), so the power in and out stays the same. Check your answer to (c) this way: if the voltage went down, the current must go up.

Example 9: Half-life from a decay curve

Practice question · [9 marks]

A hospital uses a gamma-emitting tracer to diagnose a patient. The graph shows the count rate from a sample of the tracer, corrected for background radiation. (a) Use the graph to determine the half-life of the tracer. (b) Predict the count rate after 30 hours. (c) Calculate how long it takes for the count rate to fall to one-sixteenth of its starting value. (d) (Supplement) A second radioactive sample is measured without subtracting the background count of 20 counts/min. The measured count rate is 340 counts/min at first and 100 counts/min 16 hours later. Calculate the half-life of this sample. (e) (Supplement) Explain why a gamma emitter with a half-life of a few hours is suitable for diagnosis inside the body.

Answer:

  1. (a) The count rate falls from 800 to 400 counts/min in 6.0 hours, and from 400 to 200 in a further 6.0 hours (1). Half-life = 6.0 hours (1).
  2. (b) 30 hours = 5 half-lives: 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 → 50 → 25 counts/min (1).
  3. (c) 1 → ½ → ¼ → ⅛ → 1⁄16 is 4 half-lives (1), so the time is 4 × 6.0 = 24 hours (1).
  4. (d) Subtract the background first: 340 − 20 = 320 counts/min, and 100 − 20 = 80 counts/min (1). 320 → 160 → 80 is 2 half-lives in 16 hours, so half-life = 8.0 hours (1).
  5. (e) Gamma radiation is weakly ionising and very penetrating, so it passes out of the body to be detected (1). The short half-life means the activity drops quickly, so the patient receives a small dose, but it lasts long enough for the scan to be done (1).
Common Mistake
In part (d), halving the raw readings: 340 → 170 → 85 does not fit the data and gives the wrong half-life. Background radiation is always there and does not decay, so subtract it from every reading before you look for halving.

Example 10: Orbital speeds of Earth and Mars

Practice question · [9 marks]

The Earth orbits the Sun at an average radius of 1.5 × 1011 m, taking 365 days. Mars orbits at an average radius of 2.28 × 1011 m, taking 687 days. (a) (Supplement) Calculate the average orbital speed of the Earth. (b) (Supplement) Calculate the average orbital speed of Mars. (c) Name the force that keeps the planets in orbit and state its direction. (d) (Supplement) Use your answers to describe how orbital speed changes with distance from the Sun, and give a reason. (e) Light travels at 3.0 × 108 m/s. Calculate the time it takes light from the Sun to reach the Earth.

Answer:

  1. (a) T = 365 × 24 × 3600 = 3.15 × 107 s (1). v = 2πr ÷ T = 2π × 1.5 × 1011 ÷ 3.15 × 107 (1) = 3.0 × 104 m/s (about 30 km/s) (1).
  2. (b) T = 687 × 24 × 3600 = 5.94 × 107 s. v = 2π × 2.28 × 1011 ÷ 5.94 × 107 = 2.4 × 104 m/s (about 24 km/s) (1).
  3. (c) The gravitational attraction of the Sun (1), acting towards the centre of the Sun (1).
  4. (d) Mars is further from the Sun and has a lower orbital speed, so orbital speed decreases as distance from the Sun increases (1). The strength of the Sun’s gravitational field decreases with distance (1).
  5. (e) time = distance ÷ speed = 1.5 × 1011 ÷ 3.0 × 108 = 500 s (about 8 minutes) (1).
Examiner Tip
The period must be in seconds to get a speed in m/s: days × 24 × 3600. The radius in v = 2πr ÷ T is the distance from the centre of the Sun, and 2πr is the distance travelled in one complete orbit.
