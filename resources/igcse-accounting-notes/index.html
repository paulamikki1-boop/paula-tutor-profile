Exam Technique: How to Score Full Marks

Knowing the accounting is only half the job. This section shows how the two papers work, what each command word is asking for, and the presentation habits that examiners reward with marks.

How the qualification is assessed

For Cambridge IGCSE Accounting 0452 (examinations 2027–2029) every candidate sits the same two papers. There is no Core/Extended split, so every topic in these notes can be tested.

PaperFormatWhat it rewardsHow to approach it
Paper 1Multiple-choice questions, each with four options (A–D), one mark eachBreadth of knowledge across all seven topics; accurate definitions; quick calculationsAbout a minute per question; do quick workings on the question paper; never leave a blank
Paper 2Structured written questions, each broken into parts that build on each otherPreparing accounts and statements, calculations, explanations and reasoned adviceRead the whole question first; use the marks shown as a guide to length and detail

Command words: what each one needs

The command word tells you the type of answer and how far to take it. Matching the command word is the fastest way to stop losing marks you actually know.

Command wordWhat the examiner wantsHow to write it
State / Name / IdentifyA short, precise fact or termOne word, phrase or sentence. No explanation needed; extra writing earns nothing.
DefineThe exact meaning of a termA complete, textbook-style sentence, e.g. “Capital is the amount the owner has invested in the business.”
ExplainA point plus the reason or effect behind itMake the point, then develop it with “because…”, “this means that…”. One point developed well usually beats two bare points.

Model answer
Explain why a business owner might want to know the profit for the year. [3]
The profit shows whether the business is earning more revenue than the expenses it incurs (1). If profit is high, the owner can decide whether to withdraw more drawings or reinvest to expand (1). If profit is falling, the owner can investigate which costs have risen and take action before the business makes a loss (1).
Model answer
Advise Priya whether she should accept a bank loan of $20 000 to buy a delivery vehicle. Justify your answer. [5]
For: the vehicle would allow Priya to deliver to customers further away, which could increase revenue and profit. A loan means she does not need to use her own savings or reduce working capital.
Against: interest will be charged, which reduces profit for the year, and the loan must be repaid even if sales do not rise. Her current ratio is only 1.1 : 1, so repayments may cause cash flow problems.
Decision: Priya should accept the loan only if forecast extra profit is greater than the interest cost; based on her low liquidity, she should first consider a smaller loan or leasing the vehicle.

Presentation rules examiners reward

1
Full headingsEvery statement needs the business name, the statement title and the period or date, e.g. “Lee Traders – Income statement for the year ended 31 December 2026” or “Statement of financial position at 31 December 2026”. An income statement covers a period (“for the year ended”); a statement of financial position is at a single date (“at”).
2
Dates in ledgers and journalsEvery entry needs a date. Balances carried down use the last day of the period; balances brought down use the first day of the next period.
3
Details column names the other accountIn a ledger account the details column shows the name of the account where the double entry is completed (e.g. “Bank”, “Sales”, “R Lee”), never a vague description like “money in”.
4
Balance c/d and Balance b/dBalance carried down goes on the smaller side to make totals equal; the same figure is brought down on the opposite side below the totals. Totals must be level and ruled.
5
Label every sub-totalWrite “Gross profit”, “Profit for the year”, “Net current assets”, “Total assets”. An unlabelled figure often scores nothing even if it is correct.
6
Show workingsPut workings next to the answer or below the statement, clearly labelled (W1, W2). For ratios write the formula, the figures, then the answer to the decimal places asked.
7
Journal narrativesEach general journal entry needs a short narrative explaining the transaction, unless the question says narratives are not required.

The own-figure rule

Paper 2 questions often use an earlier answer in a later part (for example, gross profit from part (a) is used in a ratio in part (c)). If your earlier figure was wrong but you use it correctly later, examiners award the later marks on your “own figure”.

  • This only works if the examiner can see where your figure came from — so show workings and label sub-totals.
  • If you cannot calculate an earlier figure, make a sensible estimate, state it (“assume gross profit is $30 000”) and carry on.
  • Never leave a later part blank because an earlier part went wrong.
Examiner Tip
Look at the marks in brackets. A [2] “explain” usually needs one developed point; a [4] needs two developed points or one point developed fully with an example from the question. In statements, roughly one mark is available for each correctly placed and labelled line plus the heading.

Common errors that cost marks

  • Writing “Net profit” or “Debtors” instead of the current terms profit for the year and trade receivables. Use the syllabus terminology.
  • Reversing the double entry (debiting where you should credit) — always check with DEAD CLIC.
  • Missing the heading or using “at” for an income statement.
  • Putting balance b/d on the same side as balance c/d.
  • Treating drawings as an expense in the income statement; drawings reduce capital.
  • Adding instead of deducting: e.g. adding returns inwards to revenue, or adding closing inventory in cost of sales.
  • Rounding ratios incorrectly or forgetting units (%, : 1, times, days).
  • “Advise” answers with no final recommendation.
  • Generic answers that ignore the business in the question. Use its name and figures.
Common Mistake
Copying the question’s figures into a statement without arithmetic checks. Before moving on, check that the statement of financial position balances or that a ledger’s totals agree. If it does not balance, do not spend ten minutes hunting; note it, move on and return if time allows.

Timing

  • Paper 1: work steadily at about one minute per question. Mark difficult questions, come back to them at the end, and guess sensibly rather than leave a blank — there is no penalty for a wrong answer.
  • Paper 2: divide your time in proportion to marks (roughly one mark per minute, leaving 5–10 minutes to check). Long statement questions can eat time; set yourself a limit.
  • Use reading time well: underline dates, the business name, “on credit”, “by cheque”, “in cash”, and any adjustments.
  • Do the parts you are confident about first if a question allows it, but keep answers in their labelled spaces.

Multiple-choice strategies

  • Work the answer out yourself before looking at the options — distractors are designed from common errors.
  • Watch for small words: “NOT”, “increase”/“decrease”, “debit”/“credit”.
  • For accounting equation questions, write a quick A = L + C check on the paper.

Quick Checklist

  • I can describe the format of Paper 1 and Paper 2 and how to pace myself in each.
  • I can match my answer to State, Explain, Calculate, Prepare and Advise/Evaluate command words.
  • I always write a full heading with business name, title and period or date.
  • I date every ledger entry and place balance c/d and b/d on the correct sides.
  • I show labelled workings so I can earn own-figure marks.
  • I finish every Advise or Evaluate answer with a justified decision.
  • I use current syllabus terminology such as trade receivables and profit for the year.
Topic 1 · Fundamentals

The Fundamentals of Accounting

This topic explains why businesses keep accounting records, who uses them, and the accounting equation that sits underneath every double entry and every statement of financial position. Equation questions appear regularly in Paper 1 and underpin the whole of Paper 2.

The purpose of accounting

Accounting is the process of recording, classifying, summarising, analysing and communicating financial information about a business so that users can make decisions.

  • Record financial transactions accurately so there is a permanent, reliable history of what happened.
  • Calculate profit or loss for a period, so the owner knows whether the business is successful.
  • Show the financial position — what the business owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities) and the owner’s investment (capital) at a given date.
  • Control the business: keep track of amounts owed by customers and to suppliers, monitor cash and inventory, and detect errors or fraud.
  • Support decision-making and planning: pricing, expansion, borrowing, cutting costs.
  • Meet obligations to outside parties such as tax authorities and lenders.

The role of the accountant and ethics

People who prepare accounts are trusted with confidential and important information. Accountants are expected to act with:

  • Integrity — being honest and straightforward; not manipulating figures.
  • Objectivity — not letting bias, conflicts of interest or pressure from others affect judgement.
  • Professional competence — keeping knowledge and skills up to date.
  • Confidentiality — not disclosing business information without proper authority.
  • Professional behaviour — complying with laws and regulations and avoiding actions that discredit the profession.

Unethical behaviour, such as overstating profit to obtain a loan, misleads users and can lead to poor decisions, legal penalties and loss of trust.

Key definitions

Asset
A resource owned or controlled by the business, e.g. premises, inventory, trade receivables, bank
Liability
An amount owed by the business to others, e.g. trade payables, bank loan, bank overdraft
Capital
The amount invested in the business by the owner; what the business owes the owner
Drawings
Money, goods or other assets taken from the business by the owner for personal use
Revenue (income)
Amounts earned by the business, mainly from sales of goods or services
Expense
Costs of running the business in a period, e.g. wages, rent, electricity

Classifying assets and liabilities

  • Non-current assets: held for use in the business for more than one year, not for resale — land and buildings, machinery, fixtures and fittings, motor vehicles.
  • Current assets: expected to be turned into cash within one year — inventory, trade receivables, other receivables, bank, cash.
  • Non-current liabilities: due for repayment after more than one year — long-term bank loans.
  • Current liabilities: due within one year — trade payables, other payables, bank overdraft.

The accounting equation

The equation
Assets = Liabilities + Capital
Rearranged: Capital = Assets − Liabilities   and   Liabilities = Assets − Capital

Everything a business owns has been financed either by the owner (capital) or by outsiders (liabilities). Because every transaction has two effects, the equation always stays in balance. This is the foundation of double-entry book-keeping and is why the statement of financial position balances.

Finding a missing figure

  • A business has assets of $45 000 and liabilities of $12 500. Capital = $45 000 − $12 500 = $32 500.
  • Capital is $32 500 and assets are $45 000. Liabilities = $45 000 − $32 500 = $12 500.

Common Mistake
Saying a credit sale increases assets by the full selling price. The inventory sold leaves the business at cost, so assets only rise by the profit on the sale (selling price minus cost), and that profit is added to capital.

Profit, loss and capital

  • Profit = revenue − expenses (when revenue is greater than expenses).
  • Loss occurs when expenses are greater than revenue.
  • Profit increases capital; a loss reduces it. Drawings reduce capital. Extra capital introduced increases it.
Formula
Closing capital = Opening capital + Capital introduced + Profit for the year − Drawings

Model answer
Explain why the accounting equation always balances. [3]
Every asset a business has must have been financed either by the owner’s capital or by a liability owed to someone outside the business (1). Each transaction has a two-fold effect, so whenever one figure in the equation changes another changes by the same amount (1). For example, buying inventory on credit increases assets and increases trade payables by the same amount, so the two sides remain equal (1).

The path that every transaction follows from the moment it happens to the final statements:

Multiple-choice questions often ask which transaction would leave total assets unchanged, or what the effect of drawings is. Sketch three columns (Assets | Liabilities | Capital) and mark + or − for each transaction before choosing.

Topic 2 · Sources & Recording

Sources and Recording of Data

This topic covers how transactions are captured: the double-entry rules, the business documents that provide evidence, the books of prime entry, and how ledger accounts are written up and balanced. Almost every Paper 2 question expects you to make correct double entries, so this is the core skill of the whole course.

The double-entry system

Double-entry book-keeping records every transaction twice: once on the debit side of one account and once on the credit side of another, with equal amounts. This reflects the two-fold effect of each transaction and keeps the accounting equation in balance.

  • Debit (Dr) = left-hand side of an account. Credit (Cr) = right-hand side.
  • The account that receives value is debited; the account that gives value is credited.
  • Total debits must always equal total credits.
Debit: Drawings Expenses Assets  |  Credit: Liabilities Income Capital

DEAD CLIC: drawings, expenses and assets increase on the debit side; liabilities, income and capital increase on the credit side. A decrease goes on the opposite side.

Trade discount and cash discount

  • Trade discount: a reduction in list price given to other traders, often for bulk buying. It is shown on the invoice and never recorded in the books — only the net amount is recorded.
  • Cash discount: a reduction given for paying within a stated time. It is recorded in the cash book discount columns as discount allowed (an expense for the seller) or discount received (income for the buyer).

Balancing an account

1
Add both sidesFind the total of the debit side and the credit side separately (in rough).
2
Find the differenceSubtract the smaller total from the larger.
3
Enter Balance c/dWrite the difference on the smaller side, dated the last day of the period, as “Balance c/d”.
4
Total and rule offBoth sides now have the same total; write the totals level with each other.
5
Bring the balance downEnter the same figure on the opposite side, below the totals, dated the first day of the next period, as “Balance b/d”.
6
Interpret itA debit balance b/d is usually an asset, expense or drawings; a credit balance b/d is usually a liability, income or capital.

If an account has only one entry, or both sides are already equal, no balancing is needed — simply total both sides if they are equal.

The cash book

  • A two-column cash book has a cash column and a bank column on each side. Receipts go on the debit side; payments on the credit side.
  • A three-column cash book adds a discount column on each side: discount allowed on the debit side (given to customers who pay promptly) and discount received on the credit side (from suppliers we pay promptly).
  • The discount columns are memorandum columns only — they are not balanced. Their totals are posted: Dr Discount allowed account; Cr Discount received account. Individual discounts are posted to each customer’s or supplier’s account.
  • A contra entry is a transfer between cash and bank within the cash book, marked “C”. Both sides of the double entry are in the cash book itself.
  • A credit balance on the bank column means a bank overdraft (a current liability). The cash column can never have a credit balance.

The petty cash book and the imprest system

Small, frequent expenses (postage, cleaning, travel, refreshments) are paid by a petty cashier from a separate float so the main cashier is not distracted by minor items.

1
Set the imprestThe main cashier gives the petty cashier a fixed float (the imprest), e.g. $200.
2
Pay with vouchersEach payment is supported by a numbered, authorised petty cash voucher and entered in the petty cash book with an analysis column.
3
Total the periodAt the end of the period total the payments and each analysis column.
4
Restore the imprestThe main cashier reimburses exactly the amount spent, bringing the float back to $200.
5
Post to the ledgerEach analysis column total is debited to the relevant expense account; the cash book (credit) records the reimbursement.

Topic 3 · Verification

Verification of Accounting Records

How a business checks that its books are accurate: the trial balance, finding and correcting errors, bank reconciliation and control accounts. These topics come up in almost every Paper 2, often as a long question with journal entries and a corrected profit.

The trial balance

A trial balance is a list of the balances on all the ledger accounts at a certain date. Debit balances go in one column and credit balances in the other, and the two totals should agree.

Purpose of a trial balance

  • It checks the arithmetical accuracy of the double entry: for every debit there should be an equal credit.
  • It helps to find errors. If the totals disagree, there must be at least one mistake.
  • It gives a summary of all ledger balances in one place, which makes it a starting point for preparing the financial statements.

Limitations of a trial balance

  • When the totals agree, that does not prove the books are free of errors. Some errors leave the totals equal.
  • It does not show where an error is, only that one exists.
  • It is only correct for the date on which it is drawn up.

Common Mistake
Students often confuse commission with principle. In an error of commission the account is the right type but belongs to the wrong person, e.g. one customer instead of another. In an error of principle the account is the wrong class altogether, e.g. an expense instead of a non-current asset.

Errors that are revealed by a trial balance

  • Single entry: only one side of the transaction is recorded.
  • Two debits or two credits: both entries are made on the same side.
  • Different amounts: the debit and the credit are for different figures, for example because of a transposition such as $540 entered as $450 on one side only.
  • Casting errors: a book of prime entry or ledger account is overcast or undercast, i.e. added up wrongly.
  • Balance omitted from the trial balance, or entered in the wrong column.

Correction of errors

Errors are corrected through the journal. Each entry gives the date, the account to debit, the account to credit, the amounts and a short narrative explaining the correction.

The suspense account

  • When the trial balance totals do not agree, the difference is entered in a suspense account for the time being, so that draft financial statements can still be prepared.
  • If the debit total is larger, the suspense account gets a credit balance. If the credit total is larger, it gets a debit balance.
  • Only errors that affect the trial balance totals pass through the suspense account. Once all of these are corrected, the suspense account balance falls to zero (the account clears).

Bank reconciliation

  • Items on the bank statement that are not yet in the cash book, so the cash book must be updated:

    • direct debits and standing orders

    • outstanding lodgements (amounts not yet credited): money paid in and entered in the cash book but not yet shown on the bank statement
  • Errors, made either in the cash book or by the bank.

Accounting Procedures

The year-end adjustments that turn raw ledger balances into a fair profit figure: capital and revenue items, depreciation and disposals, accruals and prepayments, irrecoverable debts and provisions, and inventory valuation. Capital expenditure
Buying or improving non-current assets, including costs to get them ready for use

Day-to-day running costs, including repairs and maintenance
Capital receipts

  • Capital expenditure is money spent on buying, adding to or improving non-current assets. It includes delivery, legal fees, installation and testing. It is shown in the statement of financial position.
  • Revenue expenditure is money spent on running the business day to day, or on keeping non-current assets in working order. It is charged to the income statement.

Depreciation

Depreciation is the estimated loss in value of a non-current asset over its useful life. It is an application of the matching (accruals) principle: the cost of the asset is spread over the years that benefit from using it.

Causes of depreciation

  • Wear and tear from use
  • Obsolescence: the asset is replaced by newer technology
  • Passage of time: for example, a lease runs out
  • Depletion: natural resources such as a mine are used up

Depreciation is a non-cash expense. It reduces profit, but no money leaves the business.

Straight-line method

The same amount is charged every year.

Worked calculation

Depreciation per year = (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life in years

A machine costs $24 000, has an estimated residual value of $4 000 and a useful life of 5 years. Depreciation = ($24 000 − $4 000) ÷ 5 = $4 000 a year. The rate may instead be given as a percentage of cost.

Reducing balance method

A fixed percentage is applied each year to the net book value (cost less accumulated depreciation), so the charge gets smaller each year.

Valuation of inventory

  • Inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value. This applies the prudence concept: profit is not overstated, and losses are recognised as soon as they are expected.
  • Cost is the purchase price plus any costs of bringing the goods to their present location and condition, such as carriage inwards.
  • Net realisable value (NRV) is the expected selling price minus any costs still to be paid to complete and sell the goods, such as repairs or selling costs.
  • The rule is applied to each item or group of items separately, not to the inventory as a whole.

Explain why inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value. [3]

This applies the prudence concept. If goods can only be sold for less than they cost, for example because they are damaged or out of date, the expected loss must be recognised now rather than when they are sold. Valuing them at cost would overstate closing inventory, understate cost of sales and therefore overstate profit and current assets.

Preparation of Financial Statements

How to turn a trial balance (or an incomplete set of records) into an income statement and a statement of financial position for sole traders, partnerships, limited companies, clubs and manufacturers. The two main financial statements

  • Income statement — a summary of revenue and expenses for a period (usually one year), showing gross profit and profit for the year (or loss). It is headed “for the year ended …”.

2. Partnerships

A partnership is a business owned by two or more people (usually 2 to 20) who share the profits and losses. Advantages over a sole trader: more capital, shared workload and skills, shared losses. Disadvantages: profits shared, possible disagreements, and (in an ordinary partnership) unlimited liability.

The partnership agreement

A written agreement normally sets out:

  • the capital each partner contributes;
  • the ratio in which residual profits and losses are shared;
  • interest on capital (a reward for investing money) and its rate;
  • partners’ salaries (a reward for extra work or responsibility);
  • interest on drawings (a charge to discourage partners taking out too much cash);
  • limits on drawings and rules for admitting or retiring partners.

If there is no agreement, the usual legal default is that profits and losses are shared equally, with no interest on capital, no salaries and no interest on drawings.

Appropriation account

The appropriation account follows the income statement and shows how the profit for the year is shared. Interest on drawings is added (it is charged to partners); interest on capital and salaries are deducted; the remainder (residual profit) is shared in the profit-sharing ratio.

  • Separate legal entity: the company can own assets, sue and be sued in its own name.

Explain two differences between a debenture holder and an ordinary shareholder. (4 marks)

A debenture holder is a lender (creditor) of the company, whereas an ordinary shareholder is an owner of the company. Therefore the debenture holder has no vote at general meetings, while the shareholder can vote and influence who the directors are.

A debenture holder receives a fixed rate of interest that must be paid even if the company makes a loss, so it is an expense in the income statement. Topic 6 · Analysis & Interpretation

Analysis and Interpretation

This topic is about turning the figures in the income statement and statement of financial position into ratios that measure profitability, liquidity and efficiency. Exam questions reward accurate calculation, but most of the marks go to interpreting the results, comparing them and advising the owner.

Why we calculate ratios

  • A single figure (e.g. profit for the year of $24 000) tells you little on its own. A ratio relates one figure to another so that performance can be judged and compared.
  • Ratios fall into three groups:
    • Profitability — how well the business turns revenue and capital into profit.
    • Liquidity — whether the business can pay its short-term debts as they fall due.
    • Efficiency (activity) — how well the business manages inventory, credit customers and credit suppliers.
  • Comparisons can be made with previous years (inter-period), with other businesses (inter-firm), or with a target or industry average.

What each one tells you

  • Gross margin: the gross profit earned on each $100 of sales. A fall usually means the business is paying more for goods, cutting selling prices, or suffering inventory losses (theft, damage).
  • Mark-up: the percentage added to cost to arrive at selling price. It always gives a higher figure than gross margin for the same goods.
  • Profit margin: the profit left from each $100 of sales after all expenses. If gross margin is steady but profit margin falls, expenses have risen relative to revenue.
  • ROCE: the return the owner earns on the money invested. Compare it with the return available elsewhere (e.g. interest on a savings account) to judge whether staying in the business is worthwhile.
  • Expense-to-revenue ratios (e.g. wages, rent, advertising, delivery ÷ revenue): show which expense is driving a change in profit margin.
Examiner Tip
Gross margin and mark-up are linked. If mark-up is 50%, gross margin is 50 ÷ 150 = 33.3%. If gross margin is 25%, mark-up is 25 ÷ 75 = 33.3%. Questions on incomplete records often expect you to convert between them.

Liquidity ratios and working capital

Working capital
Current assets − Current liabilities
Current ratio
Current assets : Current liabilities (expressed as x : 1)
Liquid (acid test) ratio
(Current assets − Inventory) : Current liabilities
  • Working capital is the amount available to finance day-to-day trading. A business with too little working capital may be unable to pay suppliers, lose cash discounts, or be refused further credit.
  • The current ratio compares all current assets with current liabilities. A figure of around 1.5 : 1 to 2 : 1 is often regarded as comfortable, but the right level depends on the type of business.
  • The liquid ratio leaves out inventory because inventory must first be sold (and the money perhaps collected from customers) before it becomes cash. Around 1 : 1 is often treated as satisfactory.
  • A ratio that is too high is also a problem: it suggests money tied up in idle inventory, slow-paying customers or surplus cash that could be invested to earn a return.
  • A large gap between the current and liquid ratios shows the business relies heavily on inventory.

  • Inventory turnover: how many times in the year the average inventory is sold and replaced, or how many days goods stay in the warehouse. A faster rate (more times, fewer days) usually means higher sales and less risk of goods becoming damaged or out of date. Perishable-goods businesses expect a very fast rate.
  • Trade receivables turnover: the average time credit customers take to pay. Compare it with the credit terms allowed (e.g. 30 days). Longer collection means cash is tied up and the risk of irrecoverable debts grows.
  • Trade payables turnover: the average time taken to pay credit suppliers. Taking longer keeps cash in the business, but going beyond agreed terms risks losing cash discounts, goodwill and future credit.
  • Ideally the business collects from customers faster than it pays suppliers.
Common Mistake
Using total revenue or purchases when the question gives credit sales or credit purchases, or using closing inventory instead of average inventory for inventory turnover. Also, students often state a ratio has “improved” when a liquid ratio has risen to 3 : 1 — a very high ratio can signal poor use of resources.

1

2

3

4

Inter-period and inter-firm comparison

  • Inter-period comparison: comparing the same business over two or more years to identify trends (is the business improving or getting worse?).
  • Inter-firm comparison: comparing a business with another business, ideally in the same trade and of similar size, to judge relative performance.

General limitations of ratio analysis

  • Ratios are based on historic figures, so they may not predict the future.
  • The statement of financial position shows the position on one day, which may not be typical (and can be window-dressed).
  • Ratios ignore non-financial factors: staff quality and morale, customer loyalty, reputation, environmental impact, location.
  • Ratios show symptoms, not causes — further investigation is needed.
  • Historic cost means assets may be shown at values well below their current worth, distorting ROCE.

Topic 7 · Concepts & Modern Practice

Accounting Concepts and Modern Practice

Accounting concepts are the agreed rules that explain why transactions are recorded the way they are, and modern practice covers ethics, technology and sustainability. Exam questions ask you to name the concept behind a treatment, explain it in context, and judge ethical or practical situations.

Why accounting concepts matter

  • Concepts (also called principles or conventions) give a common framework so that financial statements are prepared in the same way by everyone.
  • They make statements reliable and comparable between years and between businesses.
  • Almost every adjustment you meet in other topics — depreciation, accruals, prepayments, provisions, inventory valuation — is justified by one or more concepts.

The accounting concepts

ConceptDefinitionExample of how it is applied
Business entityThe business is treated as separate from its owner. Only transactions that affect the business are recorded in its books.The owner pays her family’s holiday from the business bank account: this is recorded as drawings, not as a business expense. Her private car is not shown as a business asset.
ConsistencyOnce a method of treating an item is chosen, it should be used in every period unless there is a good reason to change.Delivery vans are depreciated by the reducing balance method every year; the business does not switch to straight line just to report a higher profit.
DualityEvery transaction has two aspects: one account is debited and another credited with the same amount. Assets = Capital + Liabilities always holds.Buying equipment for $3 000 on credit: debit Equipment, credit the supplier’s account; assets and liabilities both rise by $3 000.
Going concernIt is assumed that the business will continue to operate for the foreseeable future and has no intention of closing down.Non-current assets are shown at cost less depreciation rather than at the (lower) amount they would raise in a forced sale.
Historic costAssets are recorded at their original cost price, not at their current value.Premises bought for $120 000 ten years ago are still shown at $120 000 even though they might now sell for $200 000.
Matching (accruals)Revenue earned in a period is matched with the expenses incurred in earning it in that same period, regardless of when cash is received or paid.Electricity used in December but billed in January is included as an expense for the year to 31 December and shown as an other payable (accrual). MaterialityItems of small value that would not affect a user’s view of the statements need not be treated strictly; they may be recorded in the simplest way.A stapler costing $8 that will last several years is written off as an expense rather than capitalised and depreciated as a non-current asset.

Model answer
Ravi, a trader, has made the following decisions. For each, name the accounting concept applied and explain how it applies. (a) He has reduced the value of 20 damaged jackets from cost of $40 each to an expected selling price of $25 each. Common Mistake
Naming the concept but not linking it to the situation. “Prudence means not overstating profit” earns at most one mark; you must explain what in the scenario would be overstated and how the treatment prevents it. Another frequent error is confusing realisation (when revenue is recognised) with matching (pairing expenses with that revenue).

Accounting policies and the qualities of useful information

  • Accounting policies are the specific methods a business chooses to apply the concepts, e.g. which depreciation method and rate to use, how inventory is valued, what percentage provision is made for doubtful debts.
  • Policies should be chosen so that the financial statements give information with the following qualities, and should be applied consistently.
QualityMeaningExample
RelevanceInformation can influence users’ decisions; it is timely and helps them assess past or future performance.Producing the statements soon after the year-end, so the bank can decide on a loan application.
Reliability (faithful representation)Information is free from material error and bias, complete, and shows what it claims to show; it can be depended upon.Inventory is counted and valued carefully; figures are supported by source documents.
ComparabilityUsers can compare the information with other periods and other businesses because it is prepared on the same basis.Using the same depreciation policy each year; disclosing any change in policy and its effect.
UnderstandabilityInformation is presented clearly so that users with reasonable knowledge can understand it.Using standard headings and layouts; grouping items logically; avoiding unnecessary detail.

There can be a conflict between qualities: waiting to obtain fully accurate figures increases reliability but reduces relevance because the information arrives late.

Ethical considerations in accounting

Accountants are trusted by owners, lenders, tax authorities and the public. Professional bodies expect them to follow these fundamental principles:

1
IntegrityBeing honest and straightforward in all work; not being associated with information that is false or misleading.
2
ObjectivityNot allowing bias, conflicts of interest or pressure from others to override professional judgement.
3
Professional competence and due careKeeping knowledge up to date and working carefully and thoroughly.
4
ConfidentialityNot disclosing information about the business or its clients without proper authority, unless there is a legal or professional duty to do so, and not using it for personal gain.
5
Professional behaviourComplying with laws and regulations and avoiding actions that discredit the profession.

Examples of unethical practice

  • Window dressing: making the statement of financial position look better than it really is at the year-end, e.g. delaying payments to suppliers so the bank balance looks healthy, or recording next year’s sales early.
  • Overstating profit by not providing for depreciation or doubtful debts, or by valuing inventory above cost.
  • Understating profit to reduce tax (tax evasion).
  • Recording fictitious sales or assets, or omitting liabilities.
  • Changing accounting policies only to manipulate reported profit.
  • Passing on confidential customer or employee data, or using inside information for personal benefit.

Why ethical behaviour matters

  • Users rely on the statements to make decisions; misleading figures can cause lenders, suppliers and investors to lose money.
  • Unethical practice can lead to fines, prosecution and loss of professional membership.
  • It damages the reputation of the business and the trust of customers and suppliers.
Model answer
Mei is the bookkeeper for a small business. Just before the year-end the owner asks her to leave out an invoice for $6 000 of repairs received in December, so that the business’s profit looks higher when it applies for a bank loan. Explain why Mei should refuse, referring to ethical principles and accounting concepts. [4]

Leaving out the invoice would breach the principle of integrity, because Mei would knowingly be producing misleading figures. It would also breach objectivity, since she would be letting pressure from the owner override her professional judgement.

The repairs relate to December, so under the matching (accruals) concept the $6 000 must be included as an expense and an other payable for this year; omitting it would also break prudence, as profit would be overstated and liabilities understated.

The bank could lend money based on false information, and Mei and the business could face legal action and loss of reputation. She should explain this to the owner and record the invoice correctly.

Examiner Tip
In ethics scenarios, (1) name the principle, (2) say exactly what in the scenario breaches it, (3) state the consequence for users or the business, and (4) suggest the correct action. Linking ethics to a concept (such as prudence or matching) often earns an extra mark.

Technology in accounting

  • Accounting software: once a transaction is entered, the software posts it to the ledgers, updates balances and produces the trial balance, financial statements and reports automatically.
  • Automation: bank feeds import transactions directly; invoices can be scanned and read automatically; recurring entries (rent, loan repayments) and reminders to customers are generated without manual input.
  • Cloud accounting: records are stored online and can be accessed from anywhere by the owner, staff and the accountant at the same time, with automatic backups and updates.
  • Data security: financial and personal data must be protected using passwords, multi-factor authentication, access levels for different staff, encryption, regular backups, anti-virus software and staff training against fraud such as phishing.

Claiming that accounting software “eliminates all errors”. Software prevents arithmetical errors and one-sided entries, but errors of commission, principle, omission and original entry can still happen because a person still enters the data.

Sustainability and the role of accounting

  • Sustainability means meeting present needs without damaging the ability of future generations to meet theirs. Businesses are increasingly expected to consider their impact on the environment and society, not just their profit.
  • Environmental costs include energy and fuel use, waste disposal, pollution clean-up, recycling schemes, carbon taxes or fines, and investment in cleaner equipment (e.g. solar panels, electric delivery vans).
  • Role of accounting:
    • Identifying and recording environmental costs separately so managers can see and control them.
    • Measuring savings from sustainable choices (e.g. lower energy bills after installing insulation) to support investment decisions.
    • Sustainability reporting: providing information on carbon emissions, energy use, waste and social impact alongside the financial statements, so that stakeholders can judge the business’s wider performance.
    • Providing evidence to lenders, customers and investors who prefer to deal with responsible businesses.
  • Limitations: many environmental effects are hard to measure in money (the money measurement concept), so they are not shown in the financial statements and must be reported in non-financial terms.
  • Paperless accounting (digital invoices, cloud records) is itself a sustainable practice, linking technology and sustainability.
Examiner Tip
When asked to discuss a sustainable investment, give both sides: short-term costs reduce profit (and may reduce liquidity), but long-term savings, improved reputation and access to customers or finance may increase profit later. Finish with a justified conclusion.

Question 7: Double entry and errors not revealed by a trial balance

Practice question · [7 marks]

(a) Explain the principle of double entry, using an example. [3]
(b) A trial balance agrees. State two types of error that may still exist and give an example of each. [4]

Answer (a): every transaction has two aspects (the duality concept), so it is recorded twice: a debit entry in one account and a credit entry of the same amount in another account. For example, when a business buys office equipment for $600 by bank transfer, it debits the office equipment account $600 (an asset increases) and credits the bank account $600 (an asset decreases). Because every debit has an equal credit, total debit balances should equal total credit balances.

Answer (b): any two of the following.

  • Error of omission: a transaction is left out completely, e.g. a sales invoice for $200 is never recorded.
  • Error of commission: the correct amount is entered on the correct side but in the wrong account of the same class, e.g. a payment from L Diaz is credited to L Dias’s account.
  • Error of principle: an entry is made in the wrong class of account, e.g. the purchase of a delivery van is debited to the motor expenses account.
  • Error of original entry: the wrong amount is used for both entries, e.g. a $540 invoice is recorded as $450 in both accounts.
  • Complete reversal of entries: the debit and credit are swapped, e.g. cash sales are debited to sales and credited to cash.
  • Compensating error: two errors of equal value cancel each other out.
Why this scores
In (a), marks are given for “two aspects”, “equal debit and credit” and a correct example that names both accounts. In (b), name the type of error and give a specific, accurate example. A vague example such as “wrong account” does not show which error it is.

Question 8: Advise on using accounting software

Practice question · [6 marks]

Sara Lim runs a florist’s shop with two staff. She writes up her books of prime entry and ledger by hand. A friend suggests she should switch to accounting software. Advise Sara whether she should switch. Justify your answer by discussing the advantages and disadvantages.

Answer:

Advantages

  • Once a transaction is entered, the software posts the double entry automatically, so balancing and arithmetic errors are far less likely and the trial balance always agrees.

Question 8: Advise on choosing a depreciation method

Practice question · [6 marks]

Lucia Moreno is starting a delivery business. She will buy three delivery vans, which she expects to replace after four years, and some office furniture, which she expects to keep for ten years. Vans lose much of their value in the first two years, and their repair costs rise as they get older. Advise Lucia which depreciation method she should use for the vans. Justify your answer by discussing the straight-line and reducing balance methods.

Answer:

Straight-line method

  • Simple to calculate and understand, and the same charge each year makes profits easy to compare from year to year.
  • However, it does not reflect the fast early loss in a van’s value, so the NBV in the early years would be higher than the van could be sold for.
  • When combined with rising repair costs, the total cost of using the van (depreciation plus repairs) would rise each year, so later years would bear more of the cost.

Reducing balance method

  • Charges more depreciation in the early years, which matches the way vans actually lose value, so the NBV is closer to market value.
  • High depreciation and low repairs early on, and low depreciation and high repairs later, give a more even total cost each year, which matches costs fairly against revenue.
  • However, it is slightly more complex to calculate, and profits in the first years will be lower, which may concern a new business.

Recommendation

Lucia should use the reducing balance method for the vans, because it reflects their rapid early fall in value and evens out the combined cost of depreciation and repairs over their four-year life. The straight-line method would suit the office furniture, which loses value evenly over a long life. Once a method is chosen, she should apply it every year (the consistency concept).

Why this scores
Marks are given for points on both methods, developed and applied to vans (early loss in value, rising repairs), followed by a clear recommendation with a reason. Mentioning the consistency concept or a different method for the furniture shows extra judgement.

Issuing ordinary shares. Against: the existing shareholders' control is diluted, and future profits are shared among 300 000 shares rather than 200 000, which may reduce the dividend per share.

Issuing debentures. In favour: shareholders keep control, and the interest ($8 000 a year) is a known amount. If the branch earns more than 8%, the extra profit belongs to the shareholders. Against: interest must be paid even if the branch makes a loss, and $100 000 must be repaid in the future, which increases the company's financial risk.

Recommendation: Zenith should issue ordinary shares. Profit from operations was $68 000 on equity of $308 000, so the business is profitable. However, it already has $50 000 of debentures, and a new branch's profits are uncertain at first. Adding $8 000 of compulsory interest on top of the existing $3 000 would increase risk at the time when cash flow is most uncertain. If the directors were confident that the branch would quickly earn well above 8%, debentures would be better for existing shareholders.

Why this scores
Discuss both options with points for and against, use figures from the question, and finish with a clear decision linked to the reasons. A recommendation with no justification earns no marks.

Question 7: Accounting concepts and ethics

Practice question · [4 marks]

Ruth keeps the accounts for a small building company. Near the year end the owner asks her to (i) leave out a $3 000 invoice for repairs received on 28 December until January, and (ii) value unsold timber at its expected selling price of $9 000 instead of its cost of $6 500, "so the profit looks better for the bank loan". Explain which accounting principles each request breaks and why Ruth should refuse.

Answer:

  • Request (i) breaks the accruals (matching) principle. The repairs were received in this year, so the $3 000 expense must be recorded in this year as an other payable, whenever it is paid. Leaving it out would overstate profit by $3 000.
  • Request (ii) breaks the prudence principle and the rule that inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value. Using $9 000 would include a profit of $2 500 that has not yet been earned (realised).
  • Ethics: together, the requests would overstate profit by $5 500 to mislead the bank. That would break Ruth's integrity (honesty) and objectivity (not being influenced by pressure from others). The financial statements would no longer give a true and fair view, and the bank could lend on false information. Ruth should explain the rules to the owner, record the correct figures and keep a record of the conversation.
Why this scores
Name the principle, apply it to the figures in the question (the effect on profit), and link the ethical point to who could be harmed. A general phrase like "it is wrong" without a named principle scores nothing.

Quick Checklist

  • In my answer I write a narrative under every journal entry and leave errors that do not affect the totals out of the suspense account.
  • In my answer I decide whether each correction affects income or expenses before I change profit.
  • In my answer I update the cash book before I write the bank reconciliation, and the statement ends on the updated balance.
  • In my answer I enter control account items on the same side as they would appear in an individual customer or supplier account.
  • In my answer I show the formula, the working and the unit for every ratio, using credit figures for the days ratios.
  • In my answer I end every "Advise" question with a clear recommendation, justified with figures from both sides.
