Topic 1 · Characteristics & Classification

Characteristics and Classification of Living Organisms

What makes something alive, and how biologists sort the huge variety of life into groups. Expect definition marks, dichotomous key questions and "identify the group from these features" questions.

The seven characteristics of living organisms

Every living organism shows all seven of these. A car moves and "respires" fuel, but it does not grow, reproduce or show sensitivity in the biological sense, so it is not alive.

Movement Respiration Sensitivity Growth Reproduction Excretion Nutrition
CharacteristicDefinition (mark-scheme wording)
MovementAn action by an organism or part of an organism causing a change of position or place
RespirationThe chemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules and release energy for metabolism
SensitivityThe ability to detect and respond to changes in the internal or external environment
GrowthA permanent increase in size and dry mass
ReproductionThe processes that make more of the same kind of organism
ExcretionThe removal of waste products of metabolism and substances in excess of requirements
NutritionThe taking in of materials for energy, growth and development
Common Mistake
Writing "growth is getting bigger". You must say permanent increase in size and dry mass. A plant that swells with water has not necessarily grown. Similarly, respiration is not "breathing": it is a chemical process inside cells.

Why classify, and how

  • Organisms are sorted into groups using shared features. This makes the diversity of life manageable and helps scientists identify and communicate about organisms.
  • A species is a group of organisms that can reproduce to produce fertile offspring.
  • The hierarchy runs from large groups to small: kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species.
  • Supplement Classification systems aim to reflect evolutionary relationships: organisms in the same group share a more recent common ancestor.
  • Supplement Classification is traditionally based on studies of morphology and anatomy, but the sequences of bases in DNA and of amino acids in proteins are now used. The more similar the base sequences, the more closely related the two species.

The binomial system

The binomial system is an internationally agreed system in which the scientific name of an organism is made up of two parts showing the genus and the species.

Example
Panthera leo (lion)
Genus
First word, capital letter
Species
Second word, lower case
Format
Italics when typed, underlined when handwritten

Common names vary between languages and regions; the binomial name is the same worldwide, so there is no confusion. Panthera leo and Panthera tigris share a genus, so they are closely related.

Dichotomous keys

A dichotomous key identifies an organism through a series of paired statements, each giving two choices. You follow the choice that fits until you reach a name.

1
Pick one visible featureChoose something that splits the group into two, such as "has wings / has no wings".
2
Write it as a yes/no pairEach statement must be observable and not a matter of opinion (avoid "large" or "pretty").
3
Keep splittingRepeat with new features for each sub-group until every organism is on its own.
4
CheckFor n organisms you need n − 1 pairs of statements. Test the key with each organism.
Examiner Tip
When constructing a key, use features you can actually see in the drawing (number of legs, presence of antennae, shape of wings). Features such as "lives in water" or "eats meat" usually cannot be checked from a picture and will not be credited.

All cells contain cytoplasm, a cell membrane, DNA as genetic material, ribosomes for protein synthesis and enzymes involved in respiration.

Putting units on magnification ("×600 mm") or dividing the wrong way round. Remember: the image is bigger, so magnification = image over actual gives a number greater than 1.
Model answer
Explain how a red blood cell is adapted to its function. [3]
It contains haemoglobin, which combines with oxygen (1). It has no nucleus, so there is more space for haemoglobin and more oxygen can be carried (1). Its biconcave shape gives a large surface area to volume ratio for faster diffusion of oxygen (1). It is small and flexible so it can squeeze through capillaries (1).

Topic 3 · Cells & Organisation

Movement into and out of Cells

How substances cross cell membranes by diffusion, osmosis and active transport. Precise definitions and water potential explanations are heavily tested, often through potato or dialysis tubing practicals.

Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration (i.e. down a concentration gradient), as a result of their random movement.

  • The energy for diffusion comes from the kinetic energy of random movement of molecules and ions. No energy from respiration is needed (it is a passive process).
  • Some substances move into and out of cells by diffusion through the cell membrane.
  • Importance to organisms: oxygen and carbon dioxide exchange in alveoli and leaves; absorption of digested food in the small intestine; movement of urea from cells into blood.

Factors that increase the rate of diffusion

Surface area
Larger area = faster
Temperature
Higher = more kinetic energy = faster
Concentration gradient
Steeper = faster
Distance
Shorter = faster
Common Mistake
Leaving out "net" or "random movement" from the definition. Particles move in both directions; diffusion describes the overall (net) movement. Also avoid saying particles move "along" the gradient; say "down" the gradient.

Water as a solvent

Water dissolves many substances, which matters for:

  • Digestion: enzymes and food molecules are in solution so reactions can happen.
  • Excretion: urea and excess salts are removed dissolved in urine.
  • Transport: glucose, ions and hormones dissolve in blood plasma; mineral ions and sucrose dissolve in xylem and phloem sap.

Osmosis

  • Water diffuses through partially permeable membranes by osmosis. The cell membrane is partially permeable.
  • Supplement Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential (dilute solution) to a region of lower water potential (concentrated solution), through a partially permeable membrane.
  • Pure water has the highest water potential. Adding solute lowers the water potential.

  • Supplement Turgor pressure: the pressure of the cell contents pushing outwards against the inelastic cell wall. It keeps plant cells firm and supports non-woody stems and leaves. Loss of turgor causes wilting.
  • Supplement Plants absorb water because the cell sap in root hair cells has a lower water potential than the soil water, so water moves into root hairs by osmosis.
  • Supplement Animal tissues must be bathed in fluid of the right water potential (the blood plasma and tissue fluid), otherwise cells are damaged.

Required practical: osmosis in potato (or dialysis tubing)

1
Prepare tissueCut equal-sized potato cylinders with a cork borer; blot dry and record the starting mass of each.
2
ImmersePlace one cylinder in each of a range of sucrose solutions (e.g. 0.0 to 1.0 mol/dm3) at the same temperature for the same time (e.g. 30 minutes).
3
RemeasureRemove, blot gently to remove surface liquid, and reweigh.
4
CalculatePercentage change in mass = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100. Using percentages allows fair comparison if starting masses differ.
5
AnalysePlot % change against concentration. Where the line crosses zero, the solution has the same water potential as the potato cells.

With dialysis (Visking) tubing: fill with sugar solution, tie it, place in water and watch the tubing swell or a liquid level rise in an attached capillary tube as water enters by osmosis.

Examiner Tip
In osmosis explanations always name water (not "solution" or "sugar") as the thing that moves, state the direction in terms of water potential (Supplement) and mention the partially permeable membrane. These are three separate marking points.

Active transport

Active transport is the movement of particles through a cell membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration (i.e. against a concentration gradient), using energy from respiration.

  • Example: root hair cells take up mineral ions (e.g. nitrate) from the soil, where they are at a lower concentration than inside the cell.
  • Other examples: absorption of glucose in the small intestine and reabsorption of glucose in the kidney.
  • Supplement Protein carriers in the membrane carry out active transport: the particle binds to the carrier; energy from respiration changes the carrier's shape; the particle is released on the other side.
  • Cells that do a lot of active transport have many mitochondria. Anything that stops respiration (no oxygen, cyanide, low temperature) stops active transport.

Model answer
A strip of potato was placed in concentrated salt solution. After an hour it was soft and bendy. Explain why. [4]
The salt solution had a lower water potential than the potato cells (1). Water left the cells by osmosis (1) through the partially permeable cell membranes (1). The vacuoles shrank and the cells lost turgor / became flaccid (or plasmolysed), so the tissue was no longer firm (1).

  • I can describe and analyse the potato osmosis practical.

Topic 5 · Molecules & Enzymes

Enzymes

How enzymes speed up reactions, why they are specific, and how temperature and pH affect them. Graph interpretation and the "explain the shape of this curve" question are classic high-mark items.

Key definitions

  • A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction and is not changed by the reaction.
  • Enzymes are proteins that function as biological catalysts and are involved in all metabolic reactions.
  • Enzymes are important because they keep reaction rates fast enough, at body temperature, to sustain life. Without them, reactions such as respiration and digestion would be far too slow.

Protein

How an enzyme works

1
CollisionThe substrate and enzyme move randomly and collide.
2

3
Enzyme-substrate complexA temporary enzyme-substrate complex forms, and the reaction takes place.
4
ReleaseThe product(s) leave the active site. The enzyme is unchanged and can be used again.
  • The same model works for breaking molecules down (e.g. amylase: starch → maltose) and for building them up (e.g. joining glucose to make glycogen).
  • Supplement Specificity: each enzyme only catalyses one reaction (or one type of reaction) because the shape of its active site is complementary to the shape of only one substrate. Amylase cannot digest protein because protein molecules do not fit its active site.
Common Mistake
Saying the active site and substrate have "the same shape". They are complementary (they fit together like a key into a lock). Also, enzymes are not living and cannot be "killed"; they are denatured.

Effect of temperature

  • As temperature rises towards the optimum, the rate increases.
  • Above the optimum, the rate falls quickly and eventually stops.
  • Most human enzymes have an optimum of about 37°C.
  • Supplement Rising to the optimum: molecules gain kinetic energy and move faster, so there are more frequent effective collisions between enzyme and substrate, and more enzyme-substrate complexes form.
  • Supplement Above the optimum: the enzyme vibrates so much that bonds holding its shape break. The active site changes shape, so the substrate no longer fits and fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form. The enzyme is denatured; this is permanent.
  • At low temperatures enzymes are inactive but not denatured; activity returns on warming.

Effect of pH

  • Each enzyme has an optimum pH at which its activity is greatest.
  • Pepsin in the stomach works best at around pH 2; most enzymes in the small intestine, such as trypsin and lipase, work best at around pH 7 to 8.
  • Supplement Moving away from the optimum pH (either more acidic or more alkaline) changes the shape of the active site, so the substrate fits less well and fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form. Extreme pH denatures the enzyme.

Required practical: investigating temperature or pH

A typical method uses amylase and starch, testing for the disappearance of starch with iodine.

1
Set up spotting tilePut a drop of iodine solution in each well.
2
EquilibratePlace separate tubes of starch solution and amylase in a water bath at the chosen temperature for 5 minutes so they reach that temperature. For pH, add a buffer solution of known pH instead and keep temperature constant.
3
Mix and timeMix the two and start a stopwatch.
4
SampleEvery 30 seconds, transfer a drop of the mixture to a fresh iodine well.
5
End pointRecord the time when the iodine no longer turns blue-black (stays orange-brown): all starch has been digested. Rate = 1 ÷ time.
6
RepeatRepeat at several temperatures (e.g. 20, 30, 40, 50, 60°C) and repeat each to calculate a mean.

Control variables: volume and concentration of starch and amylase; pH (when varying temperature); temperature (when varying pH); sampling interval. Other suitable investigations include catalase breaking down hydrogen peroxide (measure the volume of oxygen collected) or protease acting on milk or egg white.

Examiner Tip
When describing a graph, quote data: "the rate increased from 20°C to a peak at 40°C and then fell to zero at 60°C." When explaining, use the key phrases kinetic energy, collisions, enzyme-substrate complexes, active site changes shape and denatured.
Model answer
Explain why the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction falls when the temperature rises above the optimum. [4]
At high temperature the enzyme molecule vibrates and bonds holding its shape break (1). The shape of the active site changes (1), so it is no longer complementary to the substrate / the substrate cannot fit (1). Fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form (1). The enzyme is denatured, which is irreversible (1).

  • I can describe and explain the effect of temperature, including kinetic energy and denaturation (Supplement).
  • I can describe and explain the effect of pH on enzyme activity.
  • I can plan an enzyme investigation with suitable control variables.
  • I can interpret and describe enzyme rate graphs using data.
Topic 6 · Nutrition

Plant Nutrition

How green plants make their own food by photosynthesis, what limits the rate, and how the leaf is built to do the job. Practical-based questions on starch testing and rate experiments appear on almost every paper.

Photosynthesis: the definition

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants synthesise carbohydrates from raw materials using energy from light.

  • Raw materials: carbon dioxide (from the air, through stomata) and water (from the soil, through roots and xylem).
  • Chlorophyll is a green pigment found in chloroplasts. It absorbs light energy and transfers light energy into chemical energy in molecules, for the synthesis of carbohydrates.
  • Oxygen is released as a by-product.

Equations

Word equation
carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (in the presence of light and chlorophyll)
Supplement Balanced symbol equation
6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2
Common Mistake
Writing that chlorophyll “makes” or “produces” energy. Energy cannot be created. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy and transfers it into chemical energy.

  • Thick walls strengthened with lignin: support the plant and stop vessels collapsing under tension.

  • Cells joined end to end with no cross walls: form a continuous hollow tube.

  • Root hair cells are the site of water and mineral ion uptake. Water enters by osmosis.
  • Their long, thin extension gives a large surface area, increasing the rate of uptake of water by osmosis and mineral ions by active transport.

Transpiration

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from plant leaves by evaporation of water at the surfaces of the mesophyll cells followed by diffusion of water vapour through the stomata.

  • Supplement The mesophyll cells have a large surface area and the leaf interior has air spaces, so a lot of water evaporates; the air spaces become saturated with water vapour.

3

4

Practical: investigating transpiration

  • Use a potometer: a leafy shoot sealed into a water-filled tube with a capillary and air bubble.
  • Cut the shoot under water and assemble under water so no air enters the xylem; seal joints with petroleum jelly.
  • Measure the distance the air bubble moves in a set time. A potometer actually measures water uptake, which is close to the rate of water loss.
  • Vary temperature (e.g. in rooms of different temperatures) or wind speed (a fan at different distances or settings), keeping other factors constant.
  • Alternative: weigh a potted plant over time with the soil surface covered by a plastic bag.

Supplement Wilting

  • If a plant loses water faster than it is taken up, cells lose water by osmosis and become flaccid.
  • The cells no longer press against each other, so the leaves and stem lose support and droop.
Common Mistake
Saying “water evaporates out of the stomata”. Evaporation happens at the mesophyll cell surfaces inside the leaf; the water vapour then diffuses out through the stomata.

Supplement Translocation

Translocation is the movement of sucrose and amino acids in phloem from sources to sinks.

Source
Part of a plant that releases sucrose or amino acids (e.g. photosynthesising leaves, a storage organ in spring)
Sink
Part of a plant that uses or stores sucrose or amino acids (e.g. roots, growing buds, fruits, developing seeds)
  • Some parts act as a source at one time and a sink at another. A potato tuber is a sink in summer (storing starch) and a source in spring (supplying sugar to new shoots before leaves can photosynthesise).
Examiner Tip
Use precise names: sucrose (not glucose or “food”) moves in phloem, and mineral ions (not “minerals” or “nutrients”) move in xylem.
Model answer

At a higher temperature water molecules have more kinetic energy (1), so water evaporates faster from the surfaces of the mesophyll cells (1). Wind removes water vapour from around the leaf (1), which maintains a steep concentration gradient so water vapour diffuses out of the stomata faster (1).

Transport in Animals

The circulatory system: how the heart pumps blood, how blood vessels are built for their jobs, and what blood is made of. Heart diagrams, vessel comparisons and coronary heart disease questions are regulars.

Circulatory systems

The circulatory system is a system of blood vessels with a pump and valves to ensure one-way flow of blood.

  • Fish have a single circulation: blood passes through the heart once per circuit (heart → gills → body → heart).
  • Supplement Mammals have a double circulation: blood passes through the heart twice per circuit. The pulmonary circuit goes to the lungs; the systemic circuit goes to the rest of the body.
  • Supplement Advantages of double circulation: blood returns to the heart after the lungs so it can be pumped at high pressure to the body, delivering oxygen and glucose quickly; lower pressure in the lungs avoids damaging delicate capillaries.

Structure of the heart

  • Muscular wall of cardiac muscle that contracts to pump blood.
  • Septum separates the left and right sides, so oxygenated and deoxygenated blood do not mix.
  • Right and left atria (upper chambers) receive blood; right and left ventricles (lower chambers) pump blood out.
  • One-way valves prevent backflow.
  • Coronary arteries on the surface supply the heart muscle with oxygen and glucose.
  • Supplement Ventricles have thicker walls than atria because they pump blood out of the heart, not just into the ventricles below.
  • Supplement The left ventricle wall is thicker than the right because it pumps blood at high pressure all around the body; the right only pumps to the nearby lungs.
  • Supplement Atrioventricular valves lie between atria and ventricles; semilunar valves lie at the bases of the aorta and pulmonary artery.

1

2

3

4

Supplement The cardiac cycle

  • The atria contract, pushing blood through the open atrioventricular valves into the ventricles.
  • The ventricles contract: pressure rises, closing the atrioventricular valves (preventing backflow into the atria) and forcing open the semilunar valves so blood enters the arteries.
  • The ventricles relax: pressure falls, and the semilunar valves close, preventing backflow from the arteries into the ventricles.

Heart activity and exercise

  • Heart activity can be monitored by an ECG, by measuring pulse rate, and by listening to the sounds of valves closing with a stethoscope.
  • Physical activity increases heart rate.
  • Supplement Explanation: muscles respire faster during exercise and need more oxygen and glucose, and more carbon dioxide must be removed. A faster heart rate delivers blood to the muscles more quickly. If oxygen supply is insufficient, anaerobic respiration produces lactic acid, and heart rate stays high after exercise to remove it.

Coronary heart disease (CHD)

  • CHD is caused by blockage of the coronary arteries (fatty deposits build up in the artery walls), so heart muscle gets less oxygen and glucose. Heart muscle cells cannot respire and may die, causing a heart attack.
  • Risk factors: diet high in saturated fat and salt, lack of exercise, stress, smoking, genetic predisposition, increasing age, and sex (males are at higher risk).
  • Supplement Reducing risk: a diet low in saturated fat and salt reduces fatty deposits and blood pressure; regular exercise strengthens heart muscle, reduces blood pressure and helps maintain a healthy body mass.

  • Not all diseases are transmissible. Some are caused by the immune system attacking the body’s own cells, e.g. type 1 diabetes, where the insulin-secreting cells of the pancreas are destroyed.

Body defences

Mechanical barriers

  • Skin: a tough outer layer that pathogens cannot easily cross.
  • Hairs in the nose: trap larger particles in inhaled air.

Chemical barriers

  • Mucus in the airways traps pathogens; cilia sweep it away from the lungs.
  • Stomach acid (hydrochloric acid) kills pathogens in food and drink.

Cells

  • White blood cells: phagocytes carry out phagocytosis; lymphocytes produce antibodies.
  • Vaccination also protects the body by giving immunity in advance.

Controlling the spread of disease

  • A clean water supply.
  • Hygienic food preparation: washing hands, cooking thoroughly, keeping raw and cooked foods apart, refrigeration.
  • Good personal hygiene: handwashing, covering coughs and sneezes.
  • Waste disposal: collecting rubbish so it does not attract flies and rats.
  • Sewage treatment: prevents human waste contaminating drinking water.

Supplement Antibodies and antigens

  • Antigens are molecules (often proteins) on the surface of pathogens. Each pathogen has its own antigens with a specific shape.
  • Antibodies are proteins made by lymphocytes that bind to antigens.
  • Each antibody has a specific shape that is complementary to one particular antigen, so it fits only that antigen.
  • Antibodies work by directly destroying pathogens (or clumping them together) or by marking pathogens for destruction by phagocytes.

Supplement Active immunity

Active immunity is defence against a pathogen by antibody production in the body. It is gained after an infection by a pathogen or by vaccination, and is long-term because memory cells are produced.

  • Memory cells are not produced, so the protection fades as the antibodies are broken down.
  • Breast-feeding is important because it passes antibodies to the infant, protecting it while its own immune system is still developing.

Model answer
Explain how contraction of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles causes air to enter the lungs. [4]
The external intercostal muscles contract, moving the ribs upwards and outwards. The diaphragm contracts and flattens. This increases the volume of the thorax, which decreases the pressure inside the lungs below atmospheric pressure. Air therefore moves into the lungs down the pressure gradient.
Examiner Tip
Always link the chain in order: muscle contracts → volume increases → pressure decreases → air moves in. Marks are often lost by mentioning volume but not pressure, or pressure but not which way the air moves.
Common Mistake
Saying “the lungs expand, which sucks air in”. The lungs do not pull air in by themselves; the muscles change the thorax volume and pressure. Also, expired air still contains a lot of oxygen (about 16%), so never write that expired air has “no oxygen”.

Topic 12 · Gas Exchange & Respiration

Respiration

How every living cell releases energy from glucose, with or without oxygen. Equations, definitions and the oxygen debt appear in almost every paper, so learn them word for word.

Key definitions

  • Respiration: the chemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules and release energy for metabolism.
  • Aerobic respiration: the chemical reactions in cells that use oxygen to break down nutrient molecules to release energy.
  • Anaerobic respiration: the chemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules to release energy without using oxygen.

Respiration is controlled by enzymes, so its rate is affected by temperature. It happens in all living cells, all the time, including plant cells.

Uses of energy in the body

  • Muscle contraction
  • Protein synthesis
  • Cell division
  • Active transport
  • Growth
  • Passage of nerve impulses
  • Maintaining a constant body temperature

Supplement Lactic acid and the oxygen debt

During vigorous exercise the heart and lungs cannot supply oxygen to muscles fast enough, so muscles also respire anaerobically. Lactic acid builds up in the muscles and blood, causing an oxygen debt: the oxygen needed afterwards to break the lactic acid down.

Practical: temperature and yeast respiration

  • Mix yeast with glucose solution and place tubes in water baths at different temperatures (e.g. 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 °C).
  • Measure the rate by counting CO2 bubbles per minute, measuring gas volume in a syringe, or timing how long limewater (or hydrogencarbonate indicator) takes to change colour.
  • Control variables: mass of yeast, concentration and volume of glucose, time allowed to acclimatise.
  • Expected result: rate rises to an optimum (around 35–40 °C) and then falls as the enzymes are denatured.

Respiration in germinating seeds or small invertebrates can also be shown with a respirometer; a soda-lime absorbs CO2 so that movement of a liquid bead shows oxygen uptake.

Model answer
Explain why a sprinter continues to breathe deeply and quickly for several minutes after a race. [4]
During the race the muscles respired anaerobically because not enough oxygen reached them. This produced lactic acid, which built up in the muscles and blood, creating an oxygen debt. After the race, deep and fast breathing supplies extra oxygen, and a high heart rate carries lactic acid to the liver, where it is broken down by aerobic respiration.
Examiner Tip
Use the word release when talking about energy. Energy is released by respiration, never “made” or “produced”. Examiners regularly refuse answers that say respiration “creates energy”.
Common Mistake
Confusing respiration with breathing. Breathing (ventilation) is the movement of air in and out of the lungs; respiration is a chemical process inside every cell. Also remember that anaerobic respiration in human muscle does not produce carbon dioxide.

Supplement The liver and deamination

Coordination and Response

How animals and plants detect changes and respond: nerves, the eye, hormones, homeostasis and plant tropisms. This is one of the largest topics on the syllabus and a rich source of long-answer questions.

The nervous system

  • The mammalian nervous system is made of the central nervous system (CNS), the brain and spinal cord, and the peripheral nervous system (PNS), the nerves outside the CNS.
  • It coordinates and regulates body functions using electrical impulses that travel along neurones.
  • Sensory neurones carry impulses from receptors to the CNS; relay neurones (inside the CNS) connect sensory and motor neurones; motor neurones carry impulses from the CNS to effectors (muscles or glands).
  • Reflex action: a means of automatically and rapidly integrating and coordinating stimuli with the responses of effectors (muscles and glands).

Supplement Synapses

  • A synapse is a junction between two neurones, with a narrow gap called the synaptic cleft.
  • An impulse arriving at the end of the first neurone causes vesicles to release neurotransmitter molecules into the cleft.
  • The neurotransmitter diffuses across the gap and binds to receptor proteins on the next neurone, stimulating a new impulse.
  • Synapses ensure impulses travel in one direction only, because only the first neurone releases neurotransmitter and only the second has receptors.

Homeostasis

  • Supplement It works by negative feedback: when a condition moves away from the set point, changes are made to return it to the set point.

Drugs

What a drug is, how antibiotics treat bacterial infections, and how antibiotic resistance develops. A short topic, but the resistance explanation links to natural selection and is a common 4-mark question.

What is a drug?

  • Drug: any substance taken into the body that modifies or affects chemical reactions in the body.
  • Drugs can be medicinal (e.g. antibiotics, painkillers) or recreational. For this syllabus the focus is on antibiotics.

Antibiotics

Example

  • Antibiotics target structures and processes found in bacterial cells, such as the cell wall or bacterial enzymes and ribosomes.
  • Because these targets are not found in human cells, the antibiotic can harm the bacteria without damaging the patient’s cells.

Supplement Why antibiotics do not affect viruses

  • Viruses are not cells: they have no cell wall, no ribosomes of their own and no metabolism.
  • Viruses reproduce inside host cells, using the host’s own machinery.
  • So there is nothing for an antibiotic to act on, and killing the virus would mean damaging the host cell.
  • This is why antibiotics are not prescribed for colds or influenza.

Supplement Antibiotic resistance

Some bacteria are resistant to antibiotics. A well-known example is MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus), which is resistant to many common antibiotics and is hard to treat, especially in hospitals.

2

3

4

5

Reducing the development of resistant strains

  • Use antibiotics only when essential, e.g. not for viral infections or minor infections the body can fight off.
  • Complete the full course as prescribed, so that all the bacteria are killed and none are left to develop or spread resistance.
  • Other helpful measures: reduce use of antibiotics in farming, good hygiene in hospitals to stop resistant strains spreading.

Reproduction

Asexual and sexual reproduction in plants and humans, from flowers and pollination to gametes, pregnancy, hormones and sexually transmitted infections. Diagrams of flowers and reproductive systems come up often, as do comparison questions.

Asexual and sexual reproduction

  • Asexual reproduction: a process resulting in the production of genetically identical offspring from one parent. Examples: binary fission in bacteria, potato tubers, runners in strawberry plants, budding in yeast.
  • Sexual reproduction: a process involving the fusion of the nuclei of two gametes to form a zygote and the production of offspring that are genetically different from each other.
  • Fertilisation: the fusion of the nuclei of gametes.
  • Gametes are haploid (one set of chromosomes); the zygote is diploid (two sets).

Topic 17 · Genetics

Inheritance

How genetic information is stored, copied by mitosis and meiosis, and passed from parents to offspring. Genetic diagrams appear on almost every paper, so precise vocabulary and neat working earn easy marks.

Chromosomes, genes and alleles

  • Chromosome: a thread-like structure of DNA, carrying genetic information in the form of genes.
  • Gene: a length of DNA that codes for a protein.
  • Allele: an alternative form of a gene.
  • Sex is determined by one pair of chromosomes: females are XX, males are XY. Every egg carries an X; half of sperm carry X and half carry Y, so there is a 50% chance of either sex at each fertilisation.
  • Supplement Haploid nucleus: a nucleus containing a single set of chromosomes (e.g. gametes). Diploid nucleus: a nucleus containing two sets of chromosomes (e.g. body cells). In humans the diploid number is 46 (23 pairs); the haploid number is 23.

Supplement From gene to protein

  • The sequence of bases in a gene determines the sequence of amino acids in a protein.
  • Different sequences of amino acids give different shapes of protein, so different functions (e.g. enzyme active sites, antibodies, receptors).
  • All body cells contain the same genes, but most cells express only some of them. A cell only makes the proteins it needs, which is why a muscle cell differs from a liver cell.

Mitosis

Mitosis: nuclear division giving rise to genetically identical cells.

  • Used for growth, repair of damaged tissues, replacement of cells and asexual reproduction.
  • Supplement Before mitosis, each chromosome is replicated (copied) so it consists of two identical strands. During mitosis the copies separate and move to opposite poles, so each daughter cell gets a full, identical set of chromosomes.
  • Supplement Stem cells are unspecialised cells that divide by mitosis to produce daughter cells that can become specialised for specific functions.
  • Supplement Uncontrolled cell division by mitosis can result in cancer.

Meiosis

Meiosis: a reduction division in which the chromosome number is halved from diploid to haploid, resulting in genetically different cells.

  • Meiosis produces gametes (sperm, eggs, pollen nuclei, ovules).
  • Halving the number means fertilisation restores the diploid number.
  • The gametes are genetically different from each other, which is a source of variation.

Supplement Test crosses, codominance and sex linkage

  • Test cross: cross the individual with the dominant phenotype (unknown genotype) with a homozygous recessive individual. All dominant offspring suggests homozygous dominant; any recessive offspring (roughly 1 : 1) shows it is heterozygous.

Explain why red-green colour blindness is more common in males than in females. [3]

Females have two X chromosomes, so they must inherit two recessive alleles; with one they are unaffected carriers.

Organisms and their Environment

How energy flows and nutrients cycle through ecosystems, and what controls the size of populations. Expect food web interpretation, pyramid sketches, carbon and nitrogen cycle sequences and population curve analysis.

Energy flow

  • The Sun is the principal source of energy input to biological systems.
  • Energy flows through living organisms: light energy is converted to chemical energy by photosynthesis, passed along food chains, and is eventually lost to the environment as heat. Energy is not recycled, unlike nutrients.
Food chain
Shows the transfer of energy from one organism to the next, beginning with a producer

A network of interconnected food chains
Producer
An organism that makes its own organic nutrients, usually using energy from sunlight, through photosynthesis

An organism that gets its energy by feeding on other organisms
Herbivore
An animal that gets its energy by eating plants
Carnivore
An animal that gets its energy by eating other animals
Decomposer
An organism that gets its energy from dead or waste organic material
Trophic level
The position of an organism in a food chain or food web
  • Consumers are classed as primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary according to their position. Arrows point in the direction of energy flow (from the eaten to the eater).
  • Removing or adding a species changes a food web: e.g. if a predator is removed, its prey may increase, then overgraze the producers.

Supplement Efficiency of energy transfer

  • Transfer between trophic levels is inefficient: only about 10% of energy is passed on.
  • Energy is lost through respiration (released as heat), movement, excretion (urine, faeces) and parts that are not eaten (bones, roots, fur).
  • This limits most food chains to fewer than five trophic levels.
  • It is more energy-efficient for humans to eat crop plants than to eat livestock fed on crops, because a trophic level (and its energy losses) is removed, so more people can be fed per unit of land.

2

3

Supplement The nitrogen cycle

  • Nitrogen fixation: nitrogen-fixing bacteria (free-living in soil, or in root nodules of legumes such as peas and clover) convert N2 gas into ammonium/nitrogen compounds. Lightning also fixes nitrogen.
  • Absorption: plants take up nitrate ions by active transport through root hairs and use them to make amino acids and then proteins.
  • Feeding: animals obtain proteins by eating, digest them to amino acids and build their own proteins.
  • Deamination: excess amino acids are broken down in the liver to urea, which is excreted.
  • Decomposition: decomposers break down proteins in dead organisms and urea into ammonium ions.
  • Nitrification: nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium ions to nitrite and then to nitrate ions (needs oxygen, e.g. well-drained soil).
  • Denitrification: denitrifying bacteria convert nitrate back to N2 gas (in waterlogged, anaerobic soil).

Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down the proteins in the dead animal and release ammonium ions. Nitrifying bacteria oxidise the ammonium ions to nitrite ions and then to nitrate ions. The plant absorbs the nitrate ions through its root hair cells by active transport and uses them to make amino acids and proteins.

Food supply

Modern technology has greatly increased food production through:

  • Agricultural machinery to farm larger areas more efficiently.
  • Chemical fertilisers to improve yields by supplying mineral ions (nitrate, phosphate, potassium).

Habitat destruction

Reasons

  • Increased area needed for food crops, livestock and housing.
  • Extraction of natural resources (e.g. mining, logging).
  • Freshwater and marine pollution.

Humans alter food webs by removing species or introducing new ones, which can cause populations of other species to rise or fall.

Undesirable effects of deforestation

Extinction Loss of soil Flooding Carbon dioxide increase
  • Extinction and loss of biodiversity as habitats are destroyed.
  • Loss of soil: without roots to hold it, soil is eroded by rain; nutrients are leached out.
  • Flooding: less water is absorbed and transpired, so runoff increases.
  • Increase of CO2 in the atmosphere: less photosynthesis, and burning or decomposing trees release CO2.

Pollution

  • Plastics are non-biodegradable, so they persist. In water they entangle or are swallowed by animals (e.g. turtles, seabirds), and break into microplastics that enter food chains. On land they litter habitats and release toxic chemicals when burned.
  • Untreated sewage and excess fertiliser entering water cause eutrophication.
  • Supplement Female contraceptive hormones in sewage enter rivers and can cause feminisation of male fish (reduced fertility) and may contribute to reduced sperm counts in men.

Source material: Fig. 3.1 is a diagram of a section through a human heart.

Why this scores

People with cholera can become very dehydrated. Explain how cholera causes dehydration.

The cholera bacterium produces a toxin in the small intestine.

The toxin causes chloride ions to be secreted from the cells lining the intestine into the lumen.

The water is lost from the body as severe, watery diarrhoea, together with salts, so the person becomes dehydrated.

Vaccination can help to prevent the spread of diseases such as cholera. Outline the process of vaccination and explain how it can prevent the spread of diseases.

0610/42/M/J/24 Q2(b)(ii) · May/June 2024 · [3 marks]

Describe how the events that occur at a synapse generate an impulse in the next neurone.

When the impulse reaches the end of the first neurone, vesicles release neurotransmitter molecules into the synaptic gap.

The neurotransmitter diffuses across the gap.

Why this scores

Describe and explain how impulses are only passed in one direction from one neurone to the next.

The junction between two neurones is a synapse, which has a narrow synaptic gap.

Vesicles containing neurotransmitter are found only at the end of the first neurone, so only the first neurone can release neurotransmitter into the gap.

The neurotransmitter molecules diffuse across the gap.

Only the second neurone has receptor proteins whose shape is complementary to the neurotransmitter.

The neurotransmitter binds to these receptors and an impulse is stimulated in the second neurone.

The first neurone has no receptors and the second releases no neurotransmitter into this gap, so the impulse cannot travel backwards.

Why this scores

Source material: The eye is adapted for focusing on near and distant objects.

The ciliary muscles contract.

This reduces the tension in the suspensory ligaments, so they become slack.

The lens is no longer pulled thin and becomes more convex (fatter).

A fatter lens refracts light more, so light from the near object is focused on the retina.

Why this scores

0610/42/M/J/24 Q2(c) · May/June 2024 · [4 marks]

Describe the distribution and function of rods and cones in the eye.

Distribution: rods and cones are both found in the retina. Cones are concentrated in the fovea, where there are no rods; rods are spread over the rest of the retina. There are no rods or cones at the blind spot.

Function: both are light receptors that convert light into nerve impulses.

Cones work in bright light and give colour vision; there are three types of cone, each sensitive to a different wavelength (colour) of light.

Why this scores

Describe how nervous communication differs from hormonal communication.

Nervous communication uses electrical impulses carried along neurones, whereas hormonal communication uses chemicals carried in the blood.

Nervous communication is faster than hormonal communication.

The arterioles supplying the skin surface capillaries narrow (vasoconstriction).

Less blood flows near the skin surface, so less heat is lost from the blood to the surroundings and the core temperature is kept up.

The pancreas secretes glucagon into the blood.

The blood glucose concentration rises back to normal, and then less glucagon is secreted.

Why this scores
Marks for low blood glucose, glucagon secreted by the pancreas, glycogen broken down to glucose in the liver and glucose returning to normal. The examiner report notes some candidates swapped insulin and glucagon; a person who has not eaten needs glucagon.

0610/42/O/N/24 Q3(b) · October/November 2024 · [6 marks]

Outline how blood glucose concentration is controlled.

Blood glucose is controlled by negative feedback, as part of homeostasis.

A change in blood glucose concentration is detected by the pancreas.

If the concentration rises (for example after a meal), the pancreas secretes insulin.

Insulin stimulates the liver and muscles to convert glucose to glycogen, which is stored, and increases the uptake of glucose by cells for respiration.

This lowers the blood glucose concentration back to the set point.

If the concentration falls, the pancreas secretes glucagon.

Glucagon stimulates the liver to convert glycogen to glucose, which is released into the blood, so the concentration rises back to normal.

Why this scores

Antibiotics work by targeting structures and processes found only in bacterial cells, such as the cell wall, bacterial ribosomes or enzymes.

0610/43/M/J/20 Q5(c) · May/June 2020 · [3 marks]

Use antibiotics only when essential, and never for viral infections, so bacteria are exposed to antibiotics less often and there is less selection for resistance.

Patients should complete the full course, so all the bacteria are killed and none of the less-susceptible ones survive to reproduce.

Reduce the use of antibiotics in farming, for the same reason.

0610/42/M/J/23 Q3(a)(iv) · May/June 2023 · [5 marks]

Explain how bacteria become resistant to antibiotics.

A random mutation in the DNA of a few bacteria gives them an allele for resistance, so there is variation in the population.

When the antibiotic is used, the non-resistant bacteria are killed, but the resistant bacteria survive.

The survivors have less competition and reproduce rapidly by binary fission.

They pass on the resistance allele to their offspring; it can also be passed to other bacteria on plasmids.

Over many generations the frequency of the resistance allele increases, so most of the population becomes resistant.

This is an example of natural selection, with the antibiotic acting as the selection pressure.

Why this scores
Five marks from random mutation, variation, resistant bacteria surviving and reproducing, passing on the allele, the allele increasing in frequency and natural selection. Six points give a margin; the mutation happens by chance, not because of the antibiotic.

16.1 Asexual and Sexual Reproduction in Plants

These questions test whether you can sequence the events from pollination to seed, compare self- and cross-pollination and weigh up asexual against sexual reproduction; examiners reward precise terms such as pollen tube, male nucleus, zygote and genetic variation.

What examiners keep seeing
Mixing up pollination and fertilisation, and stopping at “fertilisation forms a zygote” without saying that a male nucleus fuses with a female nucleus and the zygote divides by mitosis to form the embryo — the examiner report notes very few candidates gave these steps.
0610/42/M/J/23 Q6(b)(ii) · May/June 2023 · [2 marks]

Source material: Seeds require oxygen and water to germinate.

Suggest why oxygen and water are required for germination.

Oxygen is needed for aerobic respiration, which releases the energy the embryo needs for cell division and growth.

Water activates the enzymes that break down the food stores, and acts as a solvent so the soluble products (e.g. glucose, amino acids) can be transported to the growing embryo; it also makes the seed swell so the testa splits.

Why this scores
One mark for linking oxygen to aerobic respiration and one for a specific use of water (solvent, enzyme activation, a named cell process or turgor). Vague answers such as “water is needed for the seed to grow” do not score.
0610/43/M/J/24 Q2(c) · May/June 2024 · [2 marks]

The hazel tree uses cross-pollination rather than self-pollination. Describe cross-pollination.

Cross-pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther of one flower to the stigma of a flower on a different plant of the same species.

Why this scores
One mark for anther → stigma, one for “different plant of the same species”. “Another flower” is not enough, because that could be a flower on the same plant (self-pollination).
0610/42/O/N/24 Q4(b) · October/November 2024 · [3 marks]

Explain why self-pollination that results in production of offspring is a form of sexual reproduction and not asexual reproduction.

Self-pollination still involves gametes: the pollen grain’s male nucleus and the female nucleus in the ovule.

These gametes are haploid because they were made by meiosis.

Fertilisation takes place — the two haploid nuclei fuse to form a diploid zygote.

Because meiosis shuffles the alleles, the offspring are genetically different from the parent and from each other, whereas asexual reproduction gives genetically identical offspring.

Why this scores
Any three of: gametes, haploid, meiosis, fertilisation/fusion of nuclei to form a zygote, genetic variation in the offspring. Having only one parent does not make it asexual — the presence of gametes and fertilisation is what counts.
0610/43/O/N/23 Q1(b)(ii) · October/November 2023 · [4 marks]

Outline the advantages and disadvantages of self-pollination compared with cross-pollination.

Advantages of self-pollination

  • Pollination is more likely to succeed, because the pollen only has to reach a stigma on the same plant, so less pollen is wasted.
  • The plant does not depend on pollinators such as insects, so it can still reproduce if it is isolated or pollinators are scarce.
  • Useful features of a variety are kept from one generation to the next.

Disadvantages of self-pollination

  • There is less genetic variation in the offspring, so the population is less able to adapt (evolve) if the environment changes.
  • A new disease could affect all the plants, as few would have resistance.
  • Harmful recessive alleles are more likely to be inherited in the homozygous form, so inherited disorders are more common.
Why this scores
Four marks are available from a list that includes both sides; a strong answer gives at least one advantage and one disadvantage, each phrased as a comparison with cross-pollination. Writing “more variation” without saying which type of pollination you mean loses the mark.
0610/43/M/J/24 Q2(a)(ii) · May/June 2024 · [4 marks]

Source material: Spider plants can reproduce by producing an identical plantlet, which grows away from the single parent plant.

Describe the advantages and disadvantages of asexual reproduction in a population of spider plants in the wild.

Advantages: asexual reproduction is fast, so the spider plants can quickly colonise an area; only one parent is needed, so no pollinators, gametes or fertilisation are required; the plantlets are genetically identical to a parent that is already well adapted to that habitat.

Disadvantages: there is little or no genetic variation, so if the environment changes (e.g. drought) or a new disease arrives, the whole population may be affected; the plantlets grow close to the parent, so they compete with it for light, water and mineral ions.

Why this scores
One mark per valid point up to four, with credit for both advantages and disadvantages. Linking “no variation” to vulnerability to environmental change or disease is the key mark many answers miss.
0610/41/O/N/20 Q4(b)(i) · October/November 2020 · [5 marks]

Describe how the pollen that is carried by an insect to another flower results in the formation of a plant embryo.

The insect brushes against the stigma, so pollen is transferred to the stigma of the second flower.

The pollen grain germinates and a pollen tube grows down through the style towards the ovary.

The pollen tube enters an ovule through the micropyle and the male nucleus travels down the tube.

The male nucleus fuses with the female nucleus in the ovule — this is fertilisation — producing a diploid zygote.

The zygote divides repeatedly by mitosis to form the plant embryo, and the ovule develops into the seed.

Why this scores
One mark each for pollen on the stigma, pollen tube growing down the style, entering the ovule in the ovary, fusion of male and female nuclei (fertilisation), zygote and mitosis forming an embryo — five needed. The examiner report notes few candidates mentioned the fusion of nuclei or mitosis, so include both.

0610/43/O/N/25 Q6(c) · October/November 2025 · [3 marks]

Sperm have adaptive features to carry out their function. Explain three adaptive features of sperm.

  1. A flagellum lashes from side to side so the sperm can swim towards the egg.
  2. Many mitochondria in the mid-piece carry out aerobic respiration, releasing the energy for the flagellum to move.
  3. An acrosome contains enzymes that digest the jelly coat so the sperm can penetrate the egg.

Why this scores

0610/42/O/N/21 Q3(d) · October/November 2021 · [3 marks]

Oral contraceptives are a method of birth control taken by women. Outline how the hormones in contraceptives act as a method of birth control.

The pill contains progesterone (often with oestrogen), keeping the levels of these hormones high.

This inhibits the secretion of FSH and LH by the pituitary gland, so no follicles or eggs mature.

Without an LH surge, ovulation does not happen, so there is no egg to be fertilised. Progesterone also thickens the cervical mucus, making it harder for sperm to enter the uterus.

Why this scores
Marks for progesterone in the pill, inhibition of FSH/LH, no follicle/egg development and no ovulation. The examiner report notes common errors: saying ovulation is only “delayed”, that the pill contains FSH and LH, or that it kills sperm.
0610/42/O/N/21 Q3(b) · October/November 2021 · [4 marks]

Describe the roles of FSH and LH in the menstrual cycle.

FSH (from the pituitary gland) stimulates a follicle in the ovary to develop, so the egg inside it matures.

FSH also stimulates the ovary to secrete oestrogen.

A surge of LH around day 14 triggers ovulation — the release of the mature egg from the follicle.

LH then stimulates the empty follicle (corpus luteum) to secrete progesterone; as LH falls, progesterone secretion also falls.

Why this scores
One mark each for FSH → follicle/egg development, FSH → oestrogen secretion, LH → ovulation, LH → progesterone from the corpus luteum. The examiner report warns that “FSH produces eggs” is wrong — eggs already exist and FSH makes them develop.
0610/42/M/J/24 Q5(d)(i) · May/June 2024 · [4 marks]

Source material: Pregnant human females have a placenta.

Describe the function of the placenta in humans.

The placenta is where oxygen passes from the mother’s blood to the fetal blood and carbon dioxide passes back from the fetus to the mother.

Dissolved nutrients such as glucose and amino acids move from mother to fetus, and excretory products such as urea move from fetus to mother.

These substances cross by diffusion, while the maternal and fetal blood are kept separate, protecting the fetus from the mother’s higher blood pressure and from many pathogens.

Antibodies cross to the fetus, giving it passive immunity, and the placenta secretes the hormone progesterone to maintain the pregnancy.

Why this scores
Any four functions score: gas exchange, nutrients in, excretory products out, by diffusion, keeping the blood supplies separate, antibodies, hormone production. Do not write that the mother’s blood flows into the fetus — the two circulations never mix.
0610/41/O/N/24 Q2(a)(ii) · October/November 2024 · [4 marks]

Source material: Chlamydia is a sexually transmitted infection (STI) that is caused by a bacterium.

Describe how the spread of STIs, such as chlamydia, can be controlled.

  • Using condoms (or femidoms) during sex, as a barrier to the bacterium.
  • Reducing the number of sexual partners, or abstaining from sexual contact.
  • Testing / screening people, especially in high-risk age groups, so that infections without symptoms are found.
  • Contact tracing so partners of an infected person can also be tested and treated.
  • Treating infected people with antibiotics, since chlamydia is caused by a bacterium.
  • Education about how STIs are transmitted and how to prevent them.
Why this scores
One mark per method, up to four. Antibiotics are creditworthy here only because chlamydia is bacterial — they would not be accepted for HIV. Give specific methods; “be careful” or “safe sex” alone is too vague.
0610/43/O/N/20 Q5(b) · October/November 2020 · [6 marks]

Some people use fertility drugs and in vitro fertilisation (IVF) to have children. Outline the use of fertility drugs and the process of IVF.

Fertility drugs: the woman is given (injected with) FSH and LH early in the menstrual cycle. FSH stimulates several follicles in the ovaries to develop and mature, and the ovaries secrete oestrogen; LH then stimulates ovulation, so more eggs are released than normal.

IVF: after the drugs, mature eggs are collected from the ovaries and sperm is collected from the partner (or a donor). Why this scores

0610/41/M/J/23 Q5(b) · May/June 2023 · [2 marks]

Explain why body cells can have different specialised functions even though they contain the same genes.

0610/42/F/M/20 Q5(b) · February/March 2020 · [3 marks]

Meiosis is another type of nuclear division. Describe how the nuclei in cells produced by meiosis differ from the nuclei in cells produced by mitosis.

Meiosis is a reduction division: the nuclei it produces have half the chromosome number of the parent cell, i.e. they are haploid, while mitosis gives diploid nuclei.

The nuclei from meiosis are genetically different from each other and from the parent cell, whereas the nuclei from mitosis are genetically identical.

Why this scores
Three marks: haploid, chromosome number halved (reduction division), genetically different. The question asks about the nuclei, so write about the nuclei and their chromosomes, not just “the cells”.
0610/41/M/J/25 Q5(c) · May/June 2025 · [3 marks]

Outline how the base sequences in DNA control cell function.

The sequence of bases in a gene codes for the sequence of amino acids in a protein (via mRNA, which carries a copy of the gene to the ribosome).

The amino acid sequence determines the shape of the protein, and therefore its function.

These proteins, such as enzymes (which control the cell’s reactions), receptors and antibodies, determine what the cell does.

Why this scores
One mark each for: base sequence → amino acid sequence; amino acids join to make proteins; a named protein (e.g. enzyme); amino acid sequence → protein shape. The examiner report says only the strongest candidates linked amino acid sequence to shape.
0610/41/O/N/22 Q4(a) · October/November 2022 · [4 marks]

Source material: Chromosomes are made of DNA.

Describe the structure of a DNA molecule.

DNA is made of two strands twisted together to form a double helix.

Each strand carries a sequence of four bases: A, T, C and G.

The bases on the two strands pair up by complementary base pairing: A always pairs with T and C always pairs with G.

The base pairs are held together by bonds (cross-links) between the bases, joining the two strands.

Why this scores
Four marks from: two strands, double helix, four bases named, A–T and C–G pairing, bonds between bases. The examiner report notes some candidates described DNA as a protein or a chain of amino acids — it is neither.
0610/41/M/J/25 Q3(d) · May/June 2025 · [4 marks]

Describe mitosis and its roles in organisms.

Mitosis is a nuclear division: each chromosome is first copied, then the copies separate to opposite ends of the cell, so each new nucleus gets the same chromosomes.

It produces two genetically identical cells with the same chromosome number as the parent cell.

Roles: growth of an organism; repair of damaged tissues; replacement of worn-out cells (e.g. skin, red blood cells); and asexual reproduction.

Why this scores

Source material: The cat had more toes than usual. This inherited condition is called polydactyly. The allele for polydactyly is dominant.

0610/43/M/J/22 Q2(b) · May/June 2022 · [2 marks]

Source material: Red blood cells contain the protein haemoglobin.

Fig. 2.1 shows a photomicrograph of some red blood cells from a person with sickle cell anaemia. Abnormal red blood cells occur because of a mutation in the gene for haemoglobin. Suggest how the shape of the abnormal red blood cell shown in Fig. 2.1 will affect blood flow.

0610/43/M/J/22 Q2(e) · May/June 2022 · [4 marks]

Mutations are always inherited in single-celled organisms that reproduce asexually but are not always inherited in organisms that reproduce sexually. Explain why.

A mutation is a change in the DNA, so it is only inherited if that DNA reaches the offspring.

In asexual reproduction there is one parent and the offspring are genetically identical to it (clones), so any mutation in the parent’s DNA is always copied into the offspring.

In sexual reproduction there are two parents, and the mutation may be in only one of them.

Gametes are made by meiosis, so they are not genetically identical: the mutation is in only some of the gametes, and it is inherited only if one of those gametes is fertilised.

A mutation in a body cell, rather than in a cell that makes gametes, is not passed on at all.

Why this scores
Marks for: mutations are in the DNA; asexual offspring are clones, so mutations are always inherited; sexual reproduction has two parents with the mutation in only one; the mutation is in only some gametes because meiosis does not give identical cells. The examiner report says many candidates wrongly used dominant and recessive alleles as the reason instead of the mutation not being in the gamete.

Source material: Fig. 4.1 is a photograph of a water lily, Nymphaea alba, in a lake.

Water lilies have a very thin cuticle compared to a plant growing on land. Explain how this is an adaptation for water lilies.

0610/43/M/J/20 Q4(a)(ii) · May/June 2020 · [3 marks]

Explain how xerophytes are adapted to reduce water loss to the atmosphere.

0610/43/M/J/20 Q4(a)(i) · May/June 2020 · [4 marks]

Explain how xerophytes, such as Y. treculeana, are adapted to absorb sufficient water in the conditions in which they live.

Why this scores
One mark for making its own organic nutrients and one for using light energy or photosynthesis. The examiner report says vague phrases such as “makes its own food” or “is at the bottom of the food chain” were ignored.
0610/41/M/J/25 Q1(a)(iv) · May/June 2025 · [2 marks]

Source material: Fig. 1.1 shows a pyramid of energy for an ecosystem.

Describe the advantages of representing food chains using a pyramid of energy compared with a pyramid of biomass.

A pyramid of energy is a more accurate picture of the energy actually transferred between trophic levels, and it takes time into account (energy per unit area per year), so seasonal changes do not distort it.

It also allows for organisms with different sizes, life spans and rates of reproduction: fast-breeding plankton have a small biomass at any moment but pass on a lot of energy over a year.

Why this scores
Any two of: more accurate representation of energy transfer; accounts for time or season, size, life span or rate of reproduction; allows better comparison of ecosystems. The examiner report says few candidates mentioned time, life span or reproduction rate.
0610/42/F/M/24 Q6(b)(ii) · February/March 2024 · [3 marks]

Source material: Fig. 6.2 shows the pyramid of energy for a food chain in an ocean ecosystem.

Explain why pyramids of energy usually have fewer than five trophic levels.

Energy is lost at each transfer between trophic levels; only about 10% is passed on.

For example, energy is released by respiration and lost as heat, and some is lost in faeces, urine and parts that are not eaten.

After four transfers so little energy remains that there is not enough to support another trophic level.

Why this scores
One mark each for energy lost between levels, a named example of the loss, and too little energy left for a fifth level. The examiner report says the best answers explained the loss with examples rather than just stating it.
0610/42/F/M/21 Q6(b) · February/March 2021 · [3 marks]

The total biomass of the snakes is much less than the total biomass of the mice in the food web shown in Fig. 6.1. Explain why the total biomass of the snakes is less than the total biomass of the mice. Use the term energy in your answer.

Snakes feed on mice, so they are at a higher trophic level.

Most of the energy in the mice is lost before it reaches the snakes: mice release energy in respiration, which is lost as heat, and energy is lost in their faeces and urine and in bones and fur that snakes do not digest.

Only about 10% of the energy passes on, so there is not enough energy to support a large biomass of snakes.

Why this scores
Marks for a higher trophic level, energy lost between levels, two examples of loss, and too little energy to sustain a large biomass. The examiner report says weaker answers only said snakes eat many mice, without the energy link.
0610/42/M/J/23 Q4(c) · May/June 2023 · [4 marks]

Animals such as salmon can be farmed for meat. Explain why it is more energy efficient for humans to eat crop plants than to eat livestock that have been fed on crop plants.

Energy transfer between trophic levels is inefficient: only about 10% of the energy in one level reaches the next.

Livestock lose most of the energy in the crops they eat: it is released by respiration and lost as heat, much of it during movement, and energy is lost in faeces (undigested material) and urine.

When humans eat the crop plants directly, the food chain has one fewer trophic level, so these losses are avoided.

More of the energy captured by the crop therefore reaches humans, and the same area of land can feed more people.

Why this scores
Any four of: fewer trophic levels; energy transfer is inefficient; losses in respiration, heat or movement; losses in faeces or inedible parts; losses in urine. The examiner report warns against saying energy is lost to growth (that energy becomes biomass) or that eating plants gives humans 100% of their energy.

These questions test whether you can name each process in the carbon and nitrogen cycles, say which organism carries it out and give the exact substance before and after; examiners reward the named bacteria and named ions in the right order.

What examiners keep seeing
Writing “nitrogen” for every compound (e.g. “nitrogen is turned into nitrate by denitrification”) and mixing up nitrogen fixation, nitrification and denitrification; examiners also see respiration wrongly linked to phytoplankton removing carbon dioxide.
0610/42/F/M/24 Q6(a)(i) · February/March 2024 · [2 marks]

Source material: Fig. 6.1 shows part of the carbon cycle in the ocean.

Phytoplankton contain chlorophyll. Explain how phytoplankton lower carbon dioxide concentrations in the atmosphere.

Phytoplankton are producers: their chlorophyll absorbs light energy for photosynthesis.

In photosynthesis they take in carbon dioxide (dissolved from the air) and use it to make carbohydrates such as glucose, so the carbon is locked up in their biomass instead of staying in the atmosphere.

Why this scores
One mark for linking chlorophyll to photosynthesis and one for carbon dioxide being used to make carbohydrates. The examiner report notes a few candidates wrongly said the carbon dioxide was used in respiration.
0610/41/O/N/25 Q3(a)(ii) · October/November 2025 · [2 marks]

Source material: Fig. 3.1 is a diagram of the nitrogen cycle.

State two ways that nitrogen fixation can occur.

1 By nitrogen-fixing bacteria (free-living in the soil or in the root nodules of legumes).

2 By lightning, whose energy makes nitrogen gas react with oxygen.

Why this scores
One mark each for bacteria and lightning; the Haber process (making fertiliser) is also accepted. Do not write “nitrifying bacteria” here: they make nitrate from ammonium, they do not fix nitrogen gas.
0610/42/O/N/24 Q6(b) · October/November 2024 · [2 marks]

State the names of two processes that occur in both the carbon and nitrogen cycles.

1 Decomposition (decay) by decomposers.

2 Feeding (passing carbon and nitrogen compounds along a food chain when animals eat).

Why this scores
Any two of decomposition, feeding and excretion score. Avoid photosynthesis or nitrification: each belongs to only one of the two cycles.
0610/41/M/J/20 Q5(d)(iv) · May/June 2020 · [3 marks]

Nitrifying bacteria are found in sewage works. Explain the importance of nitrifying bacteria in the nitrogen cycle.

Nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium ions into nitrite ions and then into nitrate ions (nitrification).

Nitrate ions are the main form of nitrogen that plants can absorb, so the bacteria make nitrogen available to plants.

Plants take in the nitrate ions through their root hair cells and use them to make amino acids and proteins, which then pass to animals in food chains.

Why this scores
Three marks for the ammonium → nitrite → nitrate conversion, nitrate being made available to and absorbed by plants, and its use to make amino acids/proteins. Naming the ions precisely is what earns the first mark.
0610/43/O/N/21 Q2(a)(v) · October/November 2021 · [3 marks]

Describe how nitrate ions are formed in the soil.

Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down proteins, amino acids and urea in dead organisms and waste, releasing ammonium ions.

Nitrifying bacteria then convert the ammonium ions to nitrite ions and then to nitrate ions (nitrification).

Nitrogen gas from the air can also be converted to ammonium ions by nitrogen-fixing bacteria (e.g. in root nodules) or by lightning, and these are nitrified to nitrate in the same way.

Why this scores
One mark each for decomposition of proteins/urea, ammonium → nitrate, and naming nitrifying (or nitrogen-fixing) bacteria; the fixation route is a spare point. The examiner report praised answers that began with fixation by lightning or bacteria and then nitrification.
0610/42/O/N/21 Q5(b) · October/November 2021 · [5 marks]

Nitrogen is an important element for organisms. In a livestock farm, waste from animals contains protein. This waste is often spread on farmland as a fertiliser. Describe how the nitrogen in protein is recycled in the soil into a form that plants can absorb and use.

Decomposers in the soil, such as bacteria and fungi, secrete protease enzymes that break the protein in the waste down into amino acids.

The decomposers then break down the amino acids, removing the nitrogen-containing part and releasing ammonium ions (NH4+); this is ammonification.

Nitrifying bacteria oxidise the ammonium ions to nitrite ions (NO2−) and then to nitrate ions (NO3−). This is nitrification and needs oxygen, so it happens in well-aerated soil.

The nitrate ions dissolve in soil water and are absorbed by root hair cells by active transport.

The plant uses the nitrate (and some ammonium) to make amino acids and then proteins.

Why this scores
One mark each for protein → amino acids, amino acids → ammonium, done by decomposers, ammonium → nitrate, nitrifying bacteria, and plants absorbing nitrate: six points for five marks. The examiner report says ammonification was missing even from strong answers, and many lost marks by describing nitrogen fixation, which this question does not ask about.

Source material: This species is part of many communities that are adapted to the different habitats on the island.

Define the term community.

All of the populations of different species living in an ecosystem.

0610/41/M/J/24 Q7(b)(i) · May/June 2024 · [1 mark]

Source material: A scientist obtained a sample of the bacterial decomposers and grew them in a flask. The resources available for bacterial growth in the flask became limiting.

Sigmoid (S-shaped) curve.

0610/41/M/J/24 Q7(b)(iii) · May/June 2024 · [1 mark]

State one factor, other than a lack of resources, that would cause bacteria to die during the death phase.

A build-up of toxic waste products, such as carbon dioxide, which lowers the pH.

Why this scores

0610/42/M/J/22 Q6(a) · May/June 2022 · [2 marks]

Source material: Coral reefs are important ecosystems.

Complete the sentence about ecosystems. An ecosystem can be defined as a unit containing the … of organisms and their …, interacting together.

An ecosystem can be defined as a unit containing the community of organisms and their environment, interacting together.

Why this scores
One mark each for community and environment. The examiner report notes candidates often confused population, species and habitat here, so learn the definitions word for word.
0610/41/M/J/21 Q3(c)(iii) · May/June 2021 · [3 marks]

Source material: Genetically engineered bacteria that are used to make insulin were grown in a fermenter for five days.

Explain, with reference to Fig. 3.1, why the bacteria did not grow in the fermenter for longer than five days.

By the end of the five days the population had reached the death phase because limiting factors were acting.

The nutrients (e.g. glucose and amino acids) and the oxygen in the fermenter were being used up, so there was increased competition between the bacteria for them.

Toxic waste products from respiration, such as carbon dioxide, built up and lowered the pH, and heat from respiration raised the temperature, so more bacteria died than were produced.

Why this scores
Three marks from limiting factors, competition, named nutrients or oxygen running out, waste build-up, pH change and temperature rise. The examiner report says “resources” was not accepted in place of named nutrients or oxygen.
0610/43/O/N/23 Q5(c)(iv) · October/November 2023 · [3 marks]

Source material: Genetically modified bacteria were grown in a fermenter.

Describe and explain the change in bacterial population size from 24 hours to 50 hours shown in Fig. 5.1.

From 24 hours the number of bacteria decreases: this is the death phase, where the death rate is greater than the birth rate.

Nutrients such as glucose and amino acids have been used up, so there is more competition for the remaining food and space.

Toxic waste such as carbon dioxide has built up in the fermenter, which kills more bacteria.

Respiration also releases heat, so the temperature may rise above the optimum.

Why this scores

0610/41/O/N/24 Q6(c)(i) · October/November 2024 · [2 marks]

Source material: Fig. 6.1 shows the change in biomass of the organism that produces penicillin, when grown in a fermenter under controlled conditions.

Explain why air is supplied to the fermenter shown in Fig. 6.2.

Air supplies oxygen to the fungus (Penicillium).

The fungus needs oxygen for aerobic respiration, which releases the energy it needs to grow and make penicillin.

Why this scores

0610/43/M/J/20 Q2(b)(i) · May/June 2020 · [2 marks]

Source material: Some countries have invested in biofuels such as ethanol, biomass and biodiesel.

Describe how ethanol can be made by microorganisms.

Yeast is given sugar (e.g. glucose from maize or sugar cane).

0610/43/O/N/20 Q4(c) · October/November 2020 · [2 marks]

Source material: Yeast is an example of a single-celled fungus.

Explain why it is important that there is a tube in the fermenter that allows gases to escape.

The yeast produces carbon dioxide by respiration.

If the gas could not escape, the pressure inside the fermenter would build up and could burst it.

Why this scores
One mark for carbon dioxide from respiration and one for preventing a build-up of pressure. The examiner report says few candidates mentioned pressure, and some wrongly said the tube lets oxygen in.
0610/41/M/J/21 Q3(a) · May/June 2021 · [3 marks]

Source material: Bacteria are used in many biotechnological processes.

Explain why bacteria are useful in biotechnology.

They reproduce rapidly, so a large population can be grown quickly.

They can make complex molecules, such as proteins and antibiotics.

They contain plasmids, which can be used as vectors to carry new genes into the bacteria.

The genetic code is the same in all organisms, so bacteria can make a human protein from a human gene; and there are few ethical concerns about growing them.

Why this scores
Any three from rapid reproduction, complex molecules, plasmids as vectors, universal genetic code, few ethical concerns and simple/cheap growth requirements. 0610/41/O/N/21 Q3(a)(i) · October/November 2021 · [3 marks]

Source material: Biological washing powder removes stains made from oil, egg, juice, grass and many more.

Eggs contain protein. Describe how the biological washing powder removes egg stains.

The washing powder contains protease enzymes.

The proteases digest (break down) the protein in the egg stain into amino acids.

The protein is large and insoluble, but the amino acids are small and soluble, so they dissolve in the water and are washed out of the fabric.

Why this scores

0610/43/M/J/25 Q5(c)(iii) · May/June 2025 · [5 marks]

Source material: Penicillin can be made in a fermenter. Gas X in Fig. 5.2 is needed for the production of penicillin.

The concentration of gas X is one of the conditions that must be controlled to ensure that penicillin is produced. State the other conditions required to produce penicillin and describe how they are controlled in the fermenter.

Temperature: monitored by a temperature probe and kept at the optimum for the fungus’s enzymes by cold water flowing through a water jacket, which removes the heat released by respiration.

pH: monitored by a pH probe; carbon dioxide from respiration would lower the pH, so acid, alkali or a buffer is added to keep it at the optimum for enzyme activity.

Nutrients: a supply of glucose (sugar) and amino acids (a nitrogen source) is added through an inlet.

Waste: carbon dioxide and other waste gases are removed through a vent, which also stops pressure building up.

Mixing and sterility: paddles (a stirrer) spread the nutrients, oxygen and heat evenly, and the fermenter is sterilised and sealed so other microorganisms cannot contaminate it.

Why this scores
Five marks from: monitoring, temperature with a water jacket, pH with a buffer, keeping both at the optimum for enzymes, nutrient supply, waste/CO2 removal through a vent, stirring for even distribution and sealing against contamination; this answer gives more than five. Source material: Some people are concerned that genetically modified plants might cross-pollinate with wild varieties of plants.

Suggest how farmers could prevent cross-pollination between genetically modified plants and wild varieties of plants.

0610/41/O/N/20 Q4(c)(ii) · October/November 2020 · [2 marks]

0610/42/M/J/21 Q5(c)(i) · May/June 2021 · [2 marks]

Source material: Bacteria are useful in genetic engineering because they contain plasmids.

Describe how a plasmid is cut so that a new gene can be inserted into the plasmid.

The plasmid is cut with the same restriction enzyme that was used to cut out the new gene.

This leaves sticky ends on the plasmid that are complementary to the sticky ends on the gene.

Why this scores

0610/42/F/M/23 Q6(c) · February/March 2023 · [3 marks]

Source material: Golden rice is a type of rice that has been genetically modified to contain a nutrient called beta-carotene.

Discuss the disadvantages of genetically modifying rice plants to produce beta-carotene.

GM rice seed may be expensive, and farmers may not be able to save seed and must buy new seed every year, which poor farmers may not afford.

The beta-carotene gene could spread to wild rice by cross-pollination.

Growing one GM variety reduces the genetic diversity of the crop, so it is less able to adapt to new diseases.

Some consumers have ethical concerns or worry about unknown health effects, so they may refuse to buy it.

Why this scores
Three marks from seed cost, not being able to save seed, cross-contamination of wild rice, reduced genetic diversity, health concerns and consumer refusal. 0610/43/O/N/24 Q6(c) · October/November 2024 · [4 marks]

Crop plants such as soya can also be genetically modified. Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of genetic modification of crop plants.

Advantages: crops can be made resistant to insect pests, so less insecticide is sprayed; crops can be made resistant to herbicides, so weeds can be killed without harming the crop; both give a higher yield. Crops can also be given extra nutrients.

Disadvantages: GM seeds cost more and may need to be bought for every new crop; the inserted genes may transfer to wild plants by cross-pollination (e.g. producing herbicide-resistant weeds); the crop has less genetic variation, so it is less able to adapt to environmental change.

Why this scores
Four marks in total, at most three from either side, so a balanced answer with both advantages and disadvantages is essential. Specific points (seed bought every year, genes spreading by cross-pollination) score where “expensive” or “bad for the environment” do not.
0610/42/M/J/25 Q3(b)(iii) · May/June 2025 · [6 marks]

Source material: To develop GM maize, a gene for producing a natural insecticide was taken from a bacterium and transferred into maize plant cells.

Human proteins can also be made using bacteria that have been genetically modified. Describe how a gene can be transferred from one organism to another using genetic modification.

The required gene is cut out of the DNA of the first organism using a restriction enzyme, which cuts at a specific base sequence and leaves single-stranded sticky ends.

A bacterial plasmid is cut open with the same restriction enzyme, so its sticky ends are complementary to those on the gene.

The gene is inserted into the plasmid; the complementary bases pair up and DNA ligase joins the sugar–phosphate backbones.

This forms a recombinant plasmid, which is inserted into a bacterium (or other host cell).

The genetically modified bacteria multiply in a fermenter, each copying the plasmid, and they express the gene to make the protein, e.g. a human protein.

Why this scores
Six marks from: gene cut out, restriction enzyme, same enzyme for plasmid, sticky ends, complementary ends, gene inserted into plasmid, ligase, recombinant plasmid, inserted into bacteria, bacteria multiply and express the gene. The examiner report says the common errors were mixing up the two enzymes and describing the gene being cut into pieces rather than cut out whole.

0610/43/M/J/23 Q4(d) · May/June 2023 · [2 marks]

A farmer applied fertiliser to a field next to a lake. Suggest two precautions the farmer should take when applying fertiliser to reduce the risk of eutrophication occurring in the lake.

1. Apply only the amount of fertiliser the crop needs, and only when the crop is actively growing.

2. Do not apply it when heavy rain is forecast, and leave an unfertilised strip of land next to the lake, so less fertiliser is washed into the water.

Why this scores
One mark per precaution: a smaller / suitable amount and avoiding rain or the lake edge. The examiner report says the most common credited answers were using a minimal mass of fertiliser and spreading only in dry weather.
0610/43/M/J/25 Q6(c)(i) · May/June 2025 · [3 marks]

Source material: Deforestation is linked to an increase in global temperatures.

Explain how deforestation can contribute to an increase in global temperatures.

With fewer trees there is less photosynthesis, so less carbon dioxide is removed from the atmosphere. Felled trees are also burned (combustion) or decomposed by microorganisms, which releases more carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide absorbs and re-emits infrared radiation given off by the Earth’s surface, so the enhanced greenhouse effect increases and global temperatures rise.

Why this scores
Marks for less carbon dioxide removed by photosynthesis, more released by combustion / decomposition, carbon dioxide trapping infrared radiation and the enhanced greenhouse effect. The examiner report notes very few candidates scored fully: many just said “more carbon dioxide” without linking it to less photosynthesis, or wrote “traps heat” instead of infrared radiation.
0610/41/M/J/21 Q2(b)(ii) · May/June 2021 · [4 marks]

Source material: The green turtle, Chelonia mydas, is a species of marine animal that is harmed by plastic waste.

Outline the dangers of non-biodegradable plastic waste to marine animals, such as green turtles.

  • Non-biodegradable plastic is not broken down by decomposers, so it stays in the ocean for a very long time and builds up.
  • Animals become entangled in plastic, so they cannot move or swim to the surface to breathe and may drown.
  • Turtles mistake plastic bags for jellyfish and swallow them; the plastic blocks the gut, so they cannot feed and starve.
  • Sharp pieces cause injury and infection, and plastics can release toxic chemicals.
  • Floating plastic blocks light, so producers photosynthesise less and less energy enters the food web; microplastics also accumulate along food chains.
Why this scores
Covers direct effects (persistence, breathing and movement, feeding, injury / toxins) and an indirect effect (less light for producers, accumulation in food chains), giving more than the four marking points needed.
0610/41/O/N/25 Q3(c) · October/November 2025 · [6 marks]

Explain the process of eutrophication in aquatic ecosystems.

Fertiliser or untreated sewage runs off the land into a lake or river, increasing the concentration of nitrate and phosphate ions in the water.

These ions cause rapid growth and reproduction of algae, forming an algal bloom on the surface. The algae stop sunlight reaching the plants below, so these plants cannot photosynthesise and they die.

Bacteria (decomposers) feed on the dead plants and algae, so the bacterial population increases. The bacteria respire aerobically and use up the dissolved oxygen in the water. Fish and other aerobic organisms die (or leave) because of the lack of oxygen.

Why this scores
Covers the full chain for six marks with a margin: nutrients added, more nitrate, algal bloom, light blocked, plants die, bacteria decompose, bacterial population rises, aerobic respiration, oxygen used up, fish die. The mark scheme accepts “decomposers” for bacteria; saying the algae use up the oxygen loses the key marks.

21.4 Conservation

Conservation questions reward a named method together with how it works (for example, larger net mesh lets young fish survive to breed), and Supplement ideas such as genetic variation, inbreeding and the value of species for drugs, genes and crops.

What examiners keep seeing
Listing methods without saying how they conserve the species, and misreading the question: examiner reports note many candidates described what seed banks are for when asked why seeds come from many populations, or gave vague definitions such as “environmentally friendly” for a sustainable resource.
0610/42/F/M/24 Q6(c)(i) · February/March 2024 · [2 marks]

Source material: The oceans contain fish stocks that can be managed as a sustainable resource to provide food for humans.

State what is meant by the term sustainable resource.

A resource that is produced as rapidly as it is removed from the environment, so that it does not run out.

Why this scores
One mark for “produced as rapidly as it is used / removed” and one for “does not run out”. The examiner report says many candidates gave only the first half.
0610/42/M/J/21 Q4(b)(i) · May/June 2021 · [3 marks]

Source material: Seed banks are used to conserve endangered plant species.

Explain why seeds from one species are collected from many populations to store in a seed bank.

Seeds from many populations contain a wider range of alleles, so the stored seeds keep as much genetic variation as possible. Different populations are adapted to different environments, so some plants grown from them are more likely to survive if conditions change, for example a new disease or climate change. Collecting from many populations also means more seeds can be taken without removing too many from any one population.

Why this scores
Credits genetic variation, representing different environments / survival in a changing environment, and not taking too many seeds from one population. The examiner report notes many candidates described the purpose of seed banks instead of answering why many populations are sampled.
0610/41/O/N/23 Q5(b) · October/November 2023 · [3 marks]

Overfishing is one reason why populations of fish decline. Explain the risks to a species if its population size decreases.

A small population has a smaller gene pool, so there is less genetic variation. Related individuals are more likely to breed (inbreeding), so harmful recessive alleles are more likely to be inherited together, causing genetic disease or low fertility. With little variation the species is less able to adapt to change such as a new disease, and individuals may struggle to find mates, so the risk of extinction increases.

Why this scores
Gives four marking points for three marks: reduced genetic variation, inbreeding, increased frequency of recessive alleles with a consequence, and risk of extinction / difficulty finding mates.
0610/43/M/J/24 Q6(d)(ii) · May/June 2024 · [4 marks]

Source material: There are conservation programmes to reintroduce boreal toads into their natural habitat.

Conservation programmes for other endangered animals, such as birds, use artificial insemination as part of a captive breeding programme. Describe how artificial insemination is carried out as part of a captive breeding programme.

  • Semen (sperm) is collected from a chosen male, which can be from a different breeding programme or zoo to maximise genetic variation.
  • The sperm is checked for quality and can be frozen and transported or stored.
  • The female may be given hormones (drugs) to make her ovulate.
  • At the right time for ovulation, the sperm is placed into the female’s reproductive tract (vagina, uterus or oviduct, or the cloaca in birds).
Why this scores
Five creditworthy steps for four marks: collecting sperm, choosing unrelated males for genetic variation, screening / freezing, inducing ovulation and inserting at the right time.
0610/43/M/J/23 Q6(b)(iii) · May/June 2023 · [5 marks]

Source material: It is estimated that 40% of plant species are at risk of extinction. Seed banks can store seeds from many species for a long time.

Describe why conservation projects such as seed banks are important.

  • They reduce the extinction of plant species and so maintain biodiversity.
  • Animals that depend on these plants for food or shelter are also protected from extinction.
  • They help protect vulnerable ecosystems, so processes such as nutrient cycling and energy flow through food webs continue.
  • Plants are a possible source of new medicinal drugs and fuels.
  • Stored seeds keep genes that may be useful in future, for example for selective breeding of new crop plants resistant to disease or drought.
  • Seeds can be used for future research, and humans have a moral responsibility to protect other species.
Why this scores
Six developed points for five marks. The examiner report says most candidates stopped at extinction, biodiversity and ecosystems; the extra marks came from drugs, genes, crop plants and future research.
0610/41/O/N/23 Q5(c) · October/November 2023 · [6 marks]

Describe and explain how fish stocks can be managed sustainably and how overfishing can be prevented through conservation.

  • Quotas: laws limit the mass of fish each boat can catch, so fish are not removed faster than they are replaced by reproduction.
  • Net mesh size: a larger mesh lets young fish escape, so they survive to reach breeding age and replenish the population.
  • Closed seasons: no fishing during the breeding season, so fish can reproduce.
  • Protected areas (no-catch or nursery zones) where fish can breed and grow undisturbed.
  • Monitoring of populations so quotas can be adjusted, with licences and fines to enforce the rules.
  • Fish farming and restocking with captive-bred young, plus education so consumers buy fish from sustainable stocks, reducing demand.
Why this scores
Seven or more separate methods, each with a reason (quotas, mesh size, closed seasons, protected areas, monitoring and enforcement, fish farms / restocking, education). The best answers explain how the method lets young fish survive to breed, as the 2024 examiner report on a similar question notes.
