Topic 1 · Characteristics & Classification

Characteristics and Classification of Living Organisms

What makes something alive, and how biologists sort the huge variety of life into groups. Expect definition marks, dichotomous key questions and "identify the group from these features" questions.

The seven characteristics of living organisms

Every living organism shows all seven of these. A car moves and "respires" fuel, but it does not grow, reproduce or show sensitivity in the biological sense, so it is not alive.

Movement Respiration Sensitivity Growth Reproduction Excretion Nutrition
CharacteristicDefinition (mark-scheme wording)
MovementAn action by an organism or part of an organism causing a change of position or place
RespirationThe chemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules and release energy for metabolism
SensitivityThe ability to detect and respond to changes in the internal or external environment
GrowthA permanent increase in size and dry mass
ReproductionThe processes that make more of the same kind of organism
ExcretionThe removal of waste products of metabolism and substances in excess of requirements
NutritionThe taking in of materials for energy, growth and development
Common Mistake
Writing "growth is getting bigger". You must say permanent increase in size and dry mass. A plant that swells with water has not necessarily grown. Similarly, respiration is not "breathing": it is a chemical process inside cells.

Why classify, and how

  • Organisms are sorted into groups using shared features. This makes the diversity of life manageable and helps scientists identify and communicate about organisms.
  • A species is a group of organisms that can reproduce to produce fertile offspring.
  • The hierarchy runs from large groups to small: kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species.
  • Supplement Classification systems aim to reflect evolutionary relationships: organisms in the same group share a more recent common ancestor.
  • Supplement Classification is traditionally based on studies of morphology and anatomy, but the sequences of bases in DNA and of amino acids in proteins are now used. The more similar the base sequences, the more closely related the two species.

The binomial system

The binomial system is an internationally agreed system in which the scientific name of an organism is made up of two parts showing the genus and the species.

Example
Panthera leo (lion)
Genus

Common names vary between languages and regions; the binomial name is the same worldwide, so there is no confusion. Panthera leo and Panthera tigris share a genus, so they are closely related.

Dichotomous keys

A dichotomous key identifies an organism through a series of paired statements, each giving two choices. You follow the choice that fits until you reach a name.

1
Pick one visible featureChoose something that splits the group into two, such as "has wings / has no wings".
2
Write it as a yes/no pairEach statement must be observable and not a matter of opinion (avoid "large" or "pretty").
3
Keep splittingRepeat with new features for each sub-group until every organism is on its own.
4
CheckFor n organisms you need n − 1 pairs of statements. Test the key with each organism.
Examiner Tip
When constructing a key, use features you can actually see in the drawing (number of legs, presence of antennae, shape of wings). Features such as "lives in water" or "eats meat" usually cannot be checked from a picture and will not be credited.

Model answer
Explain how a red blood cell is adapted to its function. [3]
It contains haemoglobin, which combines with oxygen (1). It has no nucleus, so there is more space for haemoglobin and more oxygen can be carried (1). Its biconcave shape gives a large surface area to volume ratio for faster diffusion of oxygen (1). It is small and flexible so it can squeeze through capillaries (1).

Topic 3 · Cells & Organisation

Movement into and out of Cells

How substances cross cell membranes by diffusion, osmosis and active transport. Precise definitions and water potential explanations are heavily tested, often through potato or dialysis tubing practicals.

Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration (i.e. down a concentration gradient), as a result of their random movement.

  • The energy for diffusion comes from the kinetic energy of random movement of molecules and ions. No energy from respiration is needed (it is a passive process).
  • Some substances move into and out of cells by diffusion through the cell membrane.
  • Importance to organisms: oxygen and carbon dioxide exchange in alveoli and leaves; absorption of digested food in the small intestine; movement of urea from cells into blood.

Common Mistake
Leaving out "net" or "random movement" from the definition. Particles move in both directions; diffusion describes the overall (net) movement. Also avoid saying particles move "along" the gradient; say "down" the gradient.

Water as a solvent

Water dissolves many substances, which matters for:

  • Digestion: enzymes and food molecules are in solution so reactions can happen.
  • Excretion: urea and excess salts are removed dissolved in urine.
  • Transport: glucose, ions and hormones dissolve in blood plasma; mineral ions and sucrose dissolve in xylem and phloem sap.

Osmosis

  • Water diffuses through partially permeable membranes by osmosis. The cell membrane is partially permeable.
  • Supplement Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential (dilute solution) to a region of lower water potential (concentrated solution), through a partially permeable membrane.
  • Pure water has the highest water potential. Adding solute lowers the water potential.

  • Supplement Plants absorb water because the cell sap in root hair cells has a lower water potential than the soil water, so water moves into root hairs by osmosis.
  • Supplement Animal tissues must be bathed in fluid of the right water potential (the blood plasma and tissue fluid), otherwise cells are damaged.

2

Examiner Tip
In osmosis explanations always name water (not "solution" or "sugar") as the thing that moves, state the direction in terms of water potential (Supplement) and mention the partially permeable membrane. These are three separate marking points.

Active transport

Active transport is the movement of particles through a cell membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration (i.e. against a concentration gradient), using energy from respiration.

  • Example: root hair cells take up mineral ions (e.g. nitrate) from the soil, where they are at a lower concentration than inside the cell.
  • Other examples: absorption of glucose in the small intestine and reabsorption of glucose in the kidney.
  • Supplement Protein carriers in the membrane carry out active transport: the particle binds to the carrier; energy from respiration changes the carrier's shape; the particle is released on the other side.
  • Cells that do a lot of active transport have many mitochondria. Anything that stops respiration (no oxygen, cyanide, low temperature) stops active transport.

Model answer
A strip of potato was placed in concentrated salt solution. After an hour it was soft and bendy. Explain why. [4]
The salt solution had a lower water potential than the potato cells (1). Water left the cells by osmosis (1) through the partially permeable cell membranes (1). The vacuoles shrank and the cells lost turgor / became flaccid (or plasmolysed), so the tissue was no longer firm (1).

Enzymes

How enzymes speed up reactions, why they are specific, and how temperature and pH affect them. Graph interpretation and the "explain the shape of this curve" question are classic high-mark items.

Key definitions

  • A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction and is not changed by the reaction.
  • Enzymes are proteins that function as biological catalysts and are involved in all metabolic reactions.
  • Enzymes are important because they keep reaction rates fast enough, at body temperature, to sustain life. Without them, reactions such as respiration and digestion would be far too slow.

How an enzyme works

1

2

3

4

  • The same model works for breaking molecules down (e.g. amylase: starch → maltose) and for building them up (e.g. joining glucose to make glycogen).
  • Supplement Specificity: each enzyme only catalyses one reaction (or one type of reaction) because the shape of its active site is complementary to the shape of only one substrate. Amylase cannot digest protein because protein molecules do not fit its active site.
Common Mistake
Saying the active site and substrate have "the same shape". They are complementary (they fit together like a key into a lock). Also, enzymes are not living and cannot be "killed"; they are denatured.

Effect of temperature

  • As temperature rises towards the optimum, the rate increases.
  • Above the optimum, the rate falls quickly and eventually stops.
  • Most human enzymes have an optimum of about 37°C.
  • Supplement Rising to the optimum: molecules gain kinetic energy and move faster, so there are more frequent effective collisions between enzyme and substrate, and more enzyme-substrate complexes form.
  • Supplement Above the optimum: the enzyme vibrates so much that bonds holding its shape break. The active site changes shape, so the substrate no longer fits and fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form. The enzyme is denatured; this is permanent.
  • At low temperatures enzymes are inactive but not denatured; activity returns on warming.

Effect of pH

  • Each enzyme has an optimum pH at which its activity is greatest.
  • Pepsin in the stomach works best at around pH 2; most enzymes in the small intestine, such as trypsin and lipase, work best at around pH 7 to 8.
  • Supplement Moving away from the optimum pH (either more acidic or more alkaline) changes the shape of the active site, so the substrate fits less well and fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form. Extreme pH denatures the enzyme.

Required practical: investigating temperature or pH

A typical method uses amylase and starch, testing for the disappearance of starch with iodine.

1
Set up spotting tilePut a drop of iodine solution in each well.
2
EquilibratePlace separate tubes of starch solution and amylase in a water bath at the chosen temperature for 5 minutes so they reach that temperature. For pH, add a buffer solution of known pH instead and keep temperature constant.
3
Mix and timeMix the two and start a stopwatch.
4
SampleEvery 30 seconds, transfer a drop of the mixture to a fresh iodine well.
5
End pointRecord the time when the iodine no longer turns blue-black (stays orange-brown): all starch has been digested. Rate = 1 ÷ time.
6
RepeatRepeat at several temperatures (e.g. 20, 30, 40, 50, 60°C) and repeat each to calculate a mean.

Control variables: volume and concentration of starch and amylase; pH (when varying temperature); temperature (when varying pH); sampling interval. Other suitable investigations include catalase breaking down hydrogen peroxide (measure the volume of oxygen collected) or protease acting on milk or egg white.

Examiner Tip
When describing a graph, quote data: "the rate increased from 20°C to a peak at 40°C and then fell to zero at 60°C." When explaining, use the key phrases kinetic energy, collisions, enzyme-substrate complexes, active site changes shape and denatured.
Model answer
Explain why the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction falls when the temperature rises above the optimum. [4]
At high temperature the enzyme molecule vibrates and bonds holding its shape break (1). The shape of the active site changes (1), so it is no longer complementary to the substrate / the substrate cannot fit (1). Fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form (1). The enzyme is denatured, which is irreversible (1).

Topic 6 · Nutrition

Plant Nutrition

How green plants make their own food by photosynthesis, what limits the rate, and how the leaf is built to do the job. Practical-based questions on starch testing and rate experiments appear on almost every paper.

Photosynthesis: the definition

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants synthesise carbohydrates from raw materials using energy from light.

  • Raw materials: carbon dioxide (from the air, through stomata) and water (from the soil, through roots and xylem).
  • Chlorophyll is a green pigment found in chloroplasts. It absorbs light energy and transfers light energy into chemical energy in molecules, for the synthesis of carbohydrates.
  • Oxygen is released as a by-product.

Equations

Word equation
carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (in the presence of light and chlorophyll)
Supplement Balanced symbol equation
6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2
Common Mistake
Writing that chlorophyll “makes” or “produces” energy. Energy cannot be created. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy and transfers it into chemical energy.

Practical: testing a leaf for starch

Starch in a leaf is evidence that photosynthesis has happened. Plants are destarched first by leaving them in the dark for 48 hours so any starch present afterwards must be new.

2

4

Showing each factor is needed

  • Chlorophyll: use a variegated leaf (green and white). Only green areas turn blue-black.
  • Light: cover part of a leaf with black paper or foil. The covered area stays orange-brown.
  • Carbon dioxide: enclose a leaf or plant in a sealed bag or flask with soda lime or sodium hydroxide (absorbs CO2). Control: identical set-up without the absorber.

Practical: rate of photosynthesis

  • Use an aquatic plant such as pondweed (Elodea) in water with added sodium hydrogencarbonate (a source of CO2).
  • Count oxygen bubbles per minute, or collect gas in a measuring cylinder or gas syringe.
  • Light intensity: move a lamp to different distances. Use a heat shield (a beaker of water) or LED lamp so temperature stays constant.

Supplement Xylem structure and function

  • Thick walls strengthened with lignin: support the plant and stop vessels collapsing under tension.
  • No cell contents (dead cells): nothing blocks the flow of water.
  • Cells joined end to end with no cross walls: form a continuous hollow tube.

Water uptake

  • Root hair cells are the site of water and mineral ion uptake. Water enters by osmosis.
  • Their long, thin extension gives a large surface area, increasing the rate of uptake of water by osmosis and mineral ions by active transport.

  • Stand a celery stalk or white flower in water containing a dye (e.g. food colouring or eosin) for a few hours.
  • Cut thin cross-sections and view with a hand lens or microscope: only the xylem is stained.

Transpiration

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from plant leaves by evaporation of water at the surfaces of the mesophyll cells followed by diffusion of water vapour through the stomata.

  • Supplement The mesophyll cells have a large surface area and the leaf interior has air spaces, so a lot of water evaporates; the air spaces become saturated with water vapour.

Supplement How water moves up the plant

1

2

3

4
ReplacementMore water enters the roots from the soil to replace what moved up.

Practical: investigating transpiration

  • Use a potometer: a leafy shoot sealed into a water-filled tube with a capillary and air bubble.
  • Cut the shoot under water and assemble under water so no air enters the xylem; seal joints with petroleum jelly.
  • Measure the distance the air bubble moves in a set time. A potometer actually measures water uptake, which is close to the rate of water loss.
  • Vary temperature (e.g. in rooms of different temperatures) or wind speed (a fan at different distances or settings), keeping other factors constant.
  • Alternative: weigh a potted plant over time with the soil surface covered by a plastic bag.

Supplement Wilting

  • If a plant loses water faster than it is taken up, cells lose water by osmosis and become flaccid.
  • The cells no longer press against each other, so the leaves and stem lose support and droop.
Common Mistake
Saying “water evaporates out of the stomata”. Evaporation happens at the mesophyll cell surfaces inside the leaf; the water vapour then diffuses out through the stomata.

Supplement Translocation

Translocation is the movement of sucrose and amino acids in phloem from sources to sinks.

Source
Part of a plant that releases sucrose or amino acids (e.g. photosynthesising leaves, a storage organ in spring)
Sink
Part of a plant that uses or stores sucrose or amino acids (e.g. roots, growing buds, fruits, developing seeds)
  • Some parts act as a source at one time and a sink at another. A potato tuber is a sink in summer (storing starch) and a source in spring (supplying sugar to new shoots before leaves can photosynthesise).
Examiner Tip
Use precise names: sucrose (not glucose or “food”) moves in phloem, and mineral ions (not “minerals” or “nutrients”) move in xylem.
Model answer
Explain why a plant transpires faster on a hot, windy day. [4]
At a higher temperature water molecules have more kinetic energy (1), so water evaporates faster from the surfaces of the mesophyll cells (1). Wind removes water vapour from around the leaf (1), which maintains a steep concentration gradient so water vapour diffuses out of the stomata faster (1).

Transport in Animals

The circulatory system: how the heart pumps blood, how blood vessels are built for their jobs, and what blood is made of. Heart diagrams, vessel comparisons and coronary heart disease questions are regulars.

Circulatory systems

The circulatory system is a system of blood vessels with a pump and valves to ensure one-way flow of blood.

  • Fish have a single circulation: blood passes through the heart once per circuit (heart → gills → body → heart).
  • Supplement Mammals have a double circulation: blood passes through the heart twice per circuit. The pulmonary circuit goes to the lungs; the systemic circuit goes to the rest of the body.
  • Supplement Advantages of double circulation: blood returns to the heart after the lungs so it can be pumped at high pressure to the body, delivering oxygen and glucose quickly; lower pressure in the lungs avoids damaging delicate capillaries.

Structure of the heart

  • Muscular wall of cardiac muscle that contracts to pump blood.
  • Septum separates the left and right sides, so oxygenated and deoxygenated blood do not mix.
  • Right and left atria (upper chambers) receive blood; right and left ventricles (lower chambers) pump blood out.
  • One-way valves prevent backflow.
  • Coronary arteries on the surface supply the heart muscle with oxygen and glucose.
  • Supplement Ventricles have thicker walls than atria because they pump blood out of the heart, not just into the ventricles below.
  • Supplement The left ventricle wall is thicker than the right because it pumps blood at high pressure all around the body; the right only pumps to the nearby lungs.
  • Supplement Atrioventricular valves lie between atria and ventricles; semilunar valves lie at the bases of the aorta and pulmonary artery.

Blood flow through the heart

1

2

3
Pulmonary vein → left atriumOxygenated blood returns from the lungs.
4

Supplement The cardiac cycle

  • The atria contract, pushing blood through the open atrioventricular valves into the ventricles.
  • The ventricles contract: pressure rises, closing the atrioventricular valves (preventing backflow into the atria) and forcing open the semilunar valves so blood enters the arteries.
  • The ventricles relax: pressure falls, and the semilunar valves close, preventing backflow from the arteries into the ventricles.

Heart activity and exercise

  • Heart activity can be monitored by an ECG, by measuring pulse rate, and by listening to the sounds of valves closing with a stethoscope.
  • Physical activity increases heart rate.
  • Supplement Explanation: muscles respire faster during exercise and need more oxygen and glucose, and more carbon dioxide must be removed. A faster heart rate delivers blood to the muscles more quickly. If oxygen supply is insufficient, anaerobic respiration produces lactic acid, and heart rate stays high after exercise to remove it.

Coronary heart disease (CHD)

  • CHD is caused by blockage of the coronary arteries (fatty deposits build up in the artery walls), so heart muscle gets less oxygen and glucose. Heart muscle cells cannot respire and may die, causing a heart attack.
  • Risk factors: diet high in saturated fat and salt, lack of exercise, stress, smoking, genetic predisposition, increasing age, and sex (males are at higher risk).
  • Supplement Reducing risk: a diet low in saturated fat and salt reduces fatty deposits and blood pressure; regular exercise strengthens heart muscle, reduces blood pressure and helps maintain a healthy body mass.

Key definitions

Pathogen
A disease-causing organism

  • Pathogens include bacteria, viruses, fungi and protoctists.
  • Not all diseases are transmissible. Some are caused by the immune system attacking the body’s own cells, e.g. type 1 diabetes, where the insulin-secreting cells of the pancreas are destroyed.

Body defences

Mechanical barriers

  • Skin: a tough outer layer that pathogens cannot easily cross.
  • Hairs in the nose: trap larger particles in inhaled air.

Chemical barriers

  • Mucus in the airways traps pathogens; cilia sweep it away from the lungs.
  • Stomach acid (hydrochloric acid) kills pathogens in food and drink.

Cells

  • White blood cells: phagocytes carry out phagocytosis; lymphocytes produce antibodies.
  • Vaccination also protects the body by giving immunity in advance.

Controlling the spread of disease

  • A clean water supply.
  • Hygienic food preparation: washing hands, cooking thoroughly, keeping raw and cooked foods apart, refrigeration.
  • Good personal hygiene: handwashing, covering coughs and sneezes.
  • Waste disposal: collecting rubbish so it does not attract flies and rats.
  • Sewage treatment: prevents human waste contaminating drinking water.

Supplement Antibodies and antigens

  • Antigens are molecules (often proteins) on the surface of pathogens. Each pathogen has its own antigens with a specific shape.
  • Antibodies are proteins made by lymphocytes that bind to antigens.
  • Each antibody has a specific shape that is complementary to one particular antigen, so it fits only that antigen.
  • Antibodies work by directly destroying pathogens (or clumping them together) or by marking pathogens for destruction by phagocytes.

Supplement Active immunity

Active immunity is defence against a pathogen by antibody production in the body. It is gained after an infection by a pathogen or by vaccination, and is long-term because memory cells are produced.

Passive immunity is short-term defence against a pathogen by antibodies acquired from another individual, e.g. across the placenta or in breast milk.

  • Memory cells are not produced, so the protection fades as the antibodies are broken down.
  • Breast-feeding is important because it passes antibodies to the infant, protecting it while its own immune system is still developing.

Topic 12 · Gas Exchange & Respiration

Respiration

How every living cell releases energy from glucose, with or without oxygen. Equations, definitions and the oxygen debt appear in almost every paper, so learn them word for word.

Key definitions

  • Respiration: the chemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules and release energy for metabolism.
  • Aerobic respiration: the chemical reactions in cells that use oxygen to break down nutrient molecules to release energy.
  • Anaerobic respiration: the chemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules to release energy without using oxygen.

Respiration is controlled by enzymes, so its rate is affected by temperature. It happens in all living cells, all the time, including plant cells.

Uses of energy in the body

  • Muscle contraction
  • Protein synthesis
  • Cell division
  • Active transport
  • Growth
  • Passage of nerve impulses
  • Maintaining a constant body temperature

Aerobic respiration

Supplement Lactic acid and the oxygen debt

During vigorous exercise the heart and lungs cannot supply oxygen to muscles fast enough, so muscles also respire anaerobically. Lactic acid builds up in the muscles and blood, causing an oxygen debt: the oxygen needed afterwards to break the lactic acid down.

Practical: temperature and yeast respiration

  • Mix yeast with glucose solution and place tubes in water baths at different temperatures (e.g. 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 °C).
  • Measure the rate by counting CO2 bubbles per minute, measuring gas volume in a syringe, or timing how long limewater (or hydrogencarbonate indicator) takes to change colour.
  • Control variables: mass of yeast, concentration and volume of glucose, time allowed to acclimatise.
  • Expected result: rate rises to an optimum (around 35–40 °C) and then falls as the enzymes are denatured.

Respiration in germinating seeds or small invertebrates can also be shown with a respirometer; a soda-lime absorbs CO2 so that movement of a liquid bead shows oxygen uptake.

Model answer
Explain why a sprinter continues to breathe deeply and quickly for several minutes after a race. [4]
During the race the muscles respired anaerobically because not enough oxygen reached them. This produced lactic acid, which built up in the muscles and blood, creating an oxygen debt. After the race, deep and fast breathing supplies extra oxygen, and a high heart rate carries lactic acid to the liver, where it is broken down by aerobic respiration.
Examiner Tip
Use the word release when talking about energy. Energy is released by respiration, never “made” or “produced”. Examiners regularly refuse answers that say respiration “creates energy”.
Common Mistake
Confusing respiration with breathing. Breathing (ventilation) is the movement of air in and out of the lungs; respiration is a chemical process inside every cell. Also remember that anaerobic respiration in human muscle does not produce carbon dioxide.

Supplement The liver and deamination

Coordination and Response

How animals and plants detect changes and respond: nerves, the eye, hormones, homeostasis and plant tropisms. This is one of the largest topics on the syllabus and a rich source of long-answer questions.

The nervous system

  • The mammalian nervous system is made of the central nervous system (CNS), the brain and spinal cord, and the peripheral nervous system (PNS), the nerves outside the CNS.
  • It coordinates and regulates body functions using electrical impulses that travel along neurones.
  • Sensory neurones carry impulses from receptors to the CNS; relay neurones (inside the CNS) connect sensory and motor neurones; motor neurones carry impulses from the CNS to effectors (muscles or glands).
  • Reflex action: a means of automatically and rapidly integrating and coordinating stimuli with the responses of effectors (muscles and glands).

Supplement Synapses

  • A synapse is a junction between two neurones, with a narrow gap called the synaptic cleft.
  • An impulse arriving at the end of the first neurone causes vesicles to release neurotransmitter molecules into the cleft.
  • The neurotransmitter diffuses across the gap and binds to receptor proteins on the next neurone, stimulating a new impulse.
  • Synapses ensure impulses travel in one direction only, because only the first neurone releases neurotransmitter and only the second has receptors.

Homeostasis

  • Homeostasis: the maintenance of a constant internal environment.
  • Supplement It works by negative feedback: when a condition moves away from the set point, changes are made to return it to the set point.

Drugs

What a drug is, how antibiotics treat bacterial infections, and how antibiotic resistance develops. A short topic, but the resistance explanation links to natural selection and is a common 4-mark question.

What is a drug?

  • Drug: any substance taken into the body that modifies or affects chemical reactions in the body.
  • Drugs can be medicinal (e.g. antibiotics, painkillers) or recreational. For this syllabus the focus is on antibiotics.

Antibiotics

Example

  • Antibiotics target structures and processes found in bacterial cells, such as the cell wall or bacterial enzymes and ribosomes.
  • Because these targets are not found in human cells, the antibiotic can harm the bacteria without damaging the patient’s cells.

Supplement Why antibiotics do not affect viruses

  • Viruses are not cells: they have no cell wall, no ribosomes of their own and no metabolism.
  • Viruses reproduce inside host cells, using the host’s own machinery.
  • So there is nothing for an antibiotic to act on, and killing the virus would mean damaging the host cell.
  • This is why antibiotics are not prescribed for colds or influenza.

Supplement Antibiotic resistance

Some bacteria are resistant to antibiotics. A well-known example is MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus), which is resistant to many common antibiotics and is hard to treat, especially in hospitals.

1

2

3

4

5

Reducing the development of resistant strains

  • Use antibiotics only when essential, e.g. not for viral infections or minor infections the body can fight off.
  • Complete the full course as prescribed, so that all the bacteria are killed and none are left to develop or spread resistance.
  • Other helpful measures: reduce use of antibiotics in farming, good hygiene in hospitals to stop resistant strains spreading.

Reproduction

Asexual and sexual reproduction in plants and humans, from flowers and pollination to gametes, pregnancy, hormones and sexually transmitted infections. Diagrams of flowers and reproductive systems come up often, as do comparison questions.

Asexual and sexual reproduction

  • Asexual reproduction: a process resulting in the production of genetically identical offspring from one parent. Examples: binary fission in bacteria, potato tubers, runners in strawberry plants, budding in yeast.
  • Sexual reproduction: a process involving the fusion of the nuclei of two gametes to form a zygote and the production of offspring that are genetically different from each other.
  • Fertilisation: the fusion of the nuclei of gametes.
  • Gametes are haploid (one set of chromosomes); the zygote is diploid (two sets).

Topic 17 · Genetics

Inheritance

How genetic information is stored, copied by mitosis and meiosis, and passed from parents to offspring. Genetic diagrams appear on almost every paper, so precise vocabulary and neat working earn easy marks.

Chromosomes, genes and alleles

  • Chromosome: a thread-like structure of DNA, carrying genetic information in the form of genes.
  • Gene: a length of DNA that codes for a protein.
  • Allele: an alternative form of a gene.
  • Sex is determined by one pair of chromosomes: females are XX, males are XY. Every egg carries an X; half of sperm carry X and half carry Y, so there is a 50% chance of either sex at each fertilisation.
  • Supplement Haploid nucleus: a nucleus containing a single set of chromosomes (e.g. gametes). Diploid nucleus: a nucleus containing two sets of chromosomes (e.g. body cells). In humans the diploid number is 46 (23 pairs); the haploid number is 23.

Supplement From gene to protein

  • The sequence of bases in a gene determines the sequence of amino acids in a protein.
  • Different sequences of amino acids give different shapes of protein, so different functions (e.g. enzyme active sites, antibodies, receptors).
  • All body cells contain the same genes, but most cells express only some of them. A cell only makes the proteins it needs, which is why a muscle cell differs from a liver cell.

Mitosis

Mitosis: nuclear division giving rise to genetically identical cells.

  • Used for growth, repair of damaged tissues, replacement of cells and asexual reproduction.
  • Supplement Before mitosis, each chromosome is replicated (copied) so it consists of two identical strands. During mitosis the copies separate and move to opposite poles, so each daughter cell gets a full, identical set of chromosomes.
  • Supplement Stem cells are unspecialised cells that divide by mitosis to produce daughter cells that can become specialised for specific functions.
  • Supplement Uncontrolled cell division by mitosis can result in cancer.

Meiosis

Meiosis: a reduction division in which the chromosome number is halved from diploid to haploid, resulting in genetically different cells.

  • Meiosis produces gametes (sperm, eggs, pollen nuclei, ovules).
  • Halving the number means fertilisation restores the diploid number.
  • The gametes are genetically different from each other, which is a source of variation.

  • Heterozygous × homozygous recessive gives a 1 : 1 ratio.
  • Pedigree diagrams show inheritance through a family. If two unaffected parents have an affected child, the condition must be recessive and both parents are heterozygous.

Supplement Test crosses, codominance and sex linkage

  • Test cross: cross the individual with the dominant phenotype (unknown genotype) with a homozygous recessive individual. All dominant offspring suggests homozygous dominant; any recessive offspring (roughly 1 : 1) shows it is heterozygous.
  • Codominance: a situation in which both alleles in a heterozygous organism contribute to the phenotype.
  • ABO blood groups: alleles IA and IB are codominant; Io is recessive to both. Group A = IAIA or IAIo; group B = IBIB or IBIo; group AB = IAIB; group O = IoIo.
  • Sex-linked characteristic: a feature in which the gene responsible is located on a sex chromosome, making it more common in one sex than the other.
  • Red-green colour blindness is carried on the X chromosome. Write alleles as XN and Xn. Males (XnY) need only one recessive allele to be affected because the Y carries no copy; females need two, so a heterozygous female (XNXn) is a carrier.
Model answer
Explain why red-green colour blindness is more common in males than in females. [3]
The allele is recessive and carried on the X chromosome (sex-linked). Males have only one X chromosome and the Y chromosome does not carry the gene, so a single recessive allele causes colour blindness. Females have two X chromosomes, so they must inherit two recessive alleles; with one they are unaffected carriers.
Examiner Tip
Always give a key for your alleles, circle the gametes and finish with a clear statement of phenotypes with a ratio or probability. Marks are awarded for each stage, so a correct layout scores even if you slip on the final answer.
Common Mistake
Confusing gene and allele. Eye colour is controlled by a gene; “blue” and “brown” versions are alleles. Also, a 3 : 1 ratio is a probability, not a guarantee: a family of four children need not contain exactly one with the recessive phenotype.

  • I can explain how sex is inherited using X and Y chromosomes.
  • I can compare mitosis and meiosis and state the role of each.

Variation and Selection

Why individuals of the same species differ, how features suit organisms to their habitats, and how natural and artificial selection change populations. Natural selection is a classic 4-6 mark sequence question, so learn the steps in order.

Variation

Variation: differences between individuals of the same species.

FeatureContinuous variationDiscontinuous variation
PatternA range of phenotypes between two extremesA limited number of distinct phenotypes, no intermediates
ExamplesHeight, body mass, leaf lengthABO blood group, seed shape in peas, tongue rolling

Supplement CauseBoth genes and the environment; usually many genesGenes only; usually a single gene
  • Genetic variation is inherited; environmental variation is caused by factors such as diet, light, temperature or accidents and is not passed on.

Mutation

  • Mutation: genetic change.
  • Gene mutation: a random change in the base sequence of DNA.
  • Mutation is the way in which new alleles are formed.
  • The rate of mutation is increased by ionising radiation (e.g. X-rays, gamma rays) and some chemicals (e.g. chemicals in tobacco smoke).

Sources of genetic variation

Mutation Meiosis Random mating Random fertilisation

Remember “MMRR”: new alleles come from mutation, meiosis produces genetically different gametes, and which individuals mate and which gametes fuse are both matters of chance.

Adaptive features

  • Adaptive feature: an inherited feature that helps an organism to survive and reproduce in its environment.
  • Supplement Adaptive features are the inherited functional features of an organism that increase its fitness.
  • Supplement Fitness: the probability of an organism surviving and reproducing in the environment in which it is found.

Supplement Plants in extreme water conditions

Natural selection

1
VariationIndividuals in a population show genetic variation.
2
Over-productionMore offspring are produced than the environment can support.
3
CompetitionIndividuals compete for resources such as food, mates and space; there is a struggle for survival.
4

5

6

Supplement Adaptation: the process, resulting from natural selection, by which populations become more suited to their environment over many generations.

Supplement Antibiotic resistance in bacteria

  • A random mutation gives one bacterium an allele for resistance.
  • When antibiotic is used, non-resistant bacteria are killed; the resistant bacterium survives.
  • It reproduces rapidly by binary fission, passing the resistance allele on.
  • The population becomes mostly resistant, e.g. MRSA.
  • Reduce the risk: prescribe antibiotics only when needed (not for viral infections) and always finish the course.
Model answer
Explain how a population of bacteria can become resistant to an antibiotic. [4]
A random mutation produces an allele that gives resistance to the antibiotic. When the antibiotic is used, bacteria without the allele are killed while resistant bacteria survive. The survivors reproduce and pass on the resistance allele to their offspring. Over many generations the proportion of resistant bacteria in the population increases.

Selective breeding (artificial selection)

  1. Choose individuals with the desired feature (e.g. high milk yield, disease resistance, large fruit).
  2. Cross (breed) these individuals together.
  3. From the offspring, select those showing the feature most strongly.
  4. Repeat over many generations.

Examiner Tip
Use the word allele in natural-selection answers: “pass on the allele for resistance” scores; “pass on resistance” often does not. Key marking words are variation, competition, survive, reproduce, allele, generations.
Common Mistake
Writing that bacteria “become resistant because of the antibiotic” or “adapt to it”. The antibiotic does not cause the mutation; the mutation is random and already present. The antibiotic only selects which bacteria survive.

Topic 19 · Ecology

Organisms and their Environment

How energy flows and nutrients cycle through ecosystems, and what controls the size of populations. Expect food web interpretation, pyramid sketches, carbon and nitrogen cycle sequences and population curve analysis.

Energy flow

  • The Sun is the principal source of energy input to biological systems.
  • Energy flows through living organisms: light energy is converted to chemical energy by photosynthesis, passed along food chains, and is eventually lost to the environment as heat. Energy is not recycled, unlike nutrients.
Food chain
Shows the transfer of energy from one organism to the next, beginning with a producer
Food web
A network of interconnected food chains
Producer
An organism that makes its own organic nutrients, usually using energy from sunlight, through photosynthesis
Consumer
An organism that gets its energy by feeding on other organisms
Herbivore
An animal that gets its energy by eating plants
Carnivore
An animal that gets its energy by eating other animals
Decomposer
An organism that gets its energy from dead or waste organic material
Trophic level
The position of an organism in a food chain or food web
  • Consumers are classed as primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary according to their position. Arrows point in the direction of energy flow (from the eaten to the eater).
  • Removing or adding a species changes a food web: e.g. if a predator is removed, its prey may increase, then overgraze the producers.

Ecological pyramids

PyramidWhat each bar showsAlways pyramid-shaped?
NumbersNumber of organisms at each trophic levelNo: one oak tree supports thousands of caterpillars (inverted base)
BiomassDry mass of organisms at each levelUsually; better than numbers because size is taken into account
Supplement EnergyEnergy transferred at each level over timeAlways, because energy is lost at every transfer

Supplement Efficiency of energy transfer

  • Transfer between trophic levels is inefficient: only about 10% of energy is passed on.
  • Energy is lost through respiration (released as heat), movement, excretion (urine, faeces) and parts that are not eaten (bones, roots, fur).
  • This limits most food chains to fewer than five trophic levels.
  • It is more energy-efficient for humans to eat crop plants than to eat livestock fed on crops, because a trophic level (and its energy losses) is removed, so more people can be fed per unit of land.

The carbon cycle

1
PhotosynthesisProducers take CO2 from the air and make carbohydrates.
2
FeedingCarbon compounds pass to consumers when they eat.
3
RespirationPlants, animals and decomposers release CO2 back into the air.
4
DecompositionDecomposers break down dead organisms and waste, respiring and releasing CO2.
5
FossilisationWhere decay is prevented, remains form fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) over millions of years.
6
CombustionBurning fossil fuels and wood releases CO2.

Deforestation and combustion both raise atmospheric CO2: fewer trees remove CO2 by photosynthesis, and burning or decaying wood adds CO2.

Supplement The nitrogen cycle

  • Nitrogen fixation: nitrogen-fixing bacteria (free-living in soil, or in root nodules of legumes such as peas and clover) convert N2 gas into ammonium/nitrogen compounds. Lightning also fixes nitrogen.
  • Absorption: plants take up nitrate ions by active transport through root hairs and use them to make amino acids and then proteins.
  • Feeding: animals obtain proteins by eating, digest them to amino acids and build their own proteins.
  • Deamination: excess amino acids are broken down in the liver to urea, which is excreted.
  • Decomposition: decomposers break down proteins in dead organisms and urea into ammonium ions.
  • Nitrification: nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium ions to nitrite and then to nitrate ions (needs oxygen, e.g. well-drained soil).
  • Denitrification: denitrifying bacteria convert nitrate back to N2 gas (in waterlogged, anaerobic soil).
Model answer
Explain the roles of microorganisms in recycling nitrogen from a dead animal into a form a plant can absorb. [4]
Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down the proteins in the dead animal and release ammonium ions. Nitrifying bacteria oxidise the ammonium ions to nitrite ions and then to nitrate ions. The plant absorbs the nitrate ions through its root hair cells by active transport and uses them to make amino acids and proteins.

Populations

Population
A group of organisms of one species, living in the same area, at the same time
Community
All of the populations of different species in an ecosystem
Ecosystem
A unit containing the community of organisms and their environment, interacting together

Factors affecting the rate of population growth: food supply, competition, predation and disease.

Sigmoid population growth curve (e.g. yeast in a flask)

1
Lag phaseSlow growth: few individuals, adjusting to conditions and producing enzymes before reproducing.
2
Exponential (log) phasePlenty of food and space, little competition; birth rate far exceeds death rate, so numbers double repeatedly.
3
Stationary phaseLimiting factors (food shortage, competition, build-up of toxic waste, disease) mean birth rate equals death rate.
4
Death phaseFood runs out and toxic waste accumulates; death rate exceeds birth rate.

Supplement Be ready to explain each phase in terms of birth rate versus death rate and the limiting factors acting at that time.

Examiner Tip
In the nitrogen cycle, name the type of bacteria and the ions precisely: “nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium ions to nitrate ions”. Vague answers such as “bacteria turn it into nitrogen” score nothing.
Common Mistake
Saying energy is “used up” or “recycled”. Energy is transferred and eventually lost as heat to the surroundings; it is nutrients such as carbon and nitrogen that are recycled.

  • I can explain why energy transfer is inefficient and why eating plants feeds more people (Supplement).
  • I can describe the carbon cycle including photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, fossilisation and combustion.

Topic 20 · Biotechnology

Biotechnology and Genetic Modification

How microorganisms and enzymes are used to make food, fuel and medicines, and how genes can be moved between organisms. Questions often ask you to apply enzyme and respiration knowledge to an industrial setting or to sequence the steps of genetic modification.

Why bacteria are useful

  • They reproduce rapidly, so large populations are grown quickly.
  • They can make complex molecules (e.g. proteins such as insulin, antibiotics).
  • Supplement There are few ethical concerns over their manipulation and growth.
  • Supplement They contain plasmids (small circular loops of DNA), which are easily cut and used as vectors to carry genes.
  • Supplement The genetic code is shared by all organisms, so bacteria can read a human gene and make the human protein.

Genetic modification

Genetic modification: changing the genetic material of an organism by removing, changing or inserting individual genes.

Examples

  • Insertion of the human insulin gene into bacteria to produce human insulin for people with diabetes.
  • Insertion of genes into crop plants to give resistance to herbicides (weedkiller can be sprayed without killing the crop).
  • Insertion of genes into crop plants to give resistance to insect pests (e.g. plants that make a toxin harmful to caterpillars).
  • Insertion of genes to give crops additional vitamins (e.g. rice producing beta-carotene, a source of vitamin A).

Explain why the same restriction enzyme is used to cut both the human DNA and the plasmid. [3]
A restriction enzyme cuts DNA at a specific base sequence, leaving sticky ends. Using the same enzyme gives the gene and the opened plasmid complementary sticky ends. These bases pair up so that DNA ligase can join the gene into the plasmid to form a recombinant plasmid.

Topic 21 · Ecology

Human Influences on Ecosystems

How food production, habitat destruction and pollution affect ecosystems, and how species and resources can be conserved. Eutrophication and deforestation are favourite extended-answer questions.

Food supply

Modern technology has greatly increased food production through:

  • Agricultural machinery to farm larger areas more efficiently.
  • Chemical fertilisers to improve yields by supplying mineral ions (nitrate, phosphate, potassium).
  • Insecticides to kill insect pests and herbicides to kill weeds that compete with crops.
  • Selective breeding to improve crop and livestock yields.

Negative impacts of large-scale farming

Monocultures (one crop over a large area)Intensive livestock production
Reduced biodiversity; habitats such as hedgerows removedLarge amounts of waste can pollute water
Pests and diseases spread easily, so more pesticide is usedDisease spreads quickly in crowded animals; overuse of antibiotics
Soil loses nutrients, so more fertiliser is neededAnimal welfare concerns
Pesticides may kill non-target speciesCattle release methane; land cleared for grazing and feed crops

Supplement Famine and food shortage have social, economic and environmental causes: increasing human population, unequal distribution of food, poverty, war, drought, flooding, climate change, pests, and poor transport or storage.

Habitat destruction

Reasons

  • Increased area needed for food crops, livestock and housing.
  • Extraction of natural resources (e.g. mining, logging).
  • Freshwater and marine pollution.

Humans alter food webs by removing species or introducing new ones, which can cause populations of other species to rise or fall.

Undesirable effects of deforestation

Extinction Loss of soil Flooding Carbon dioxide increase
  • Extinction and loss of biodiversity as habitats are destroyed.
  • Loss of soil: without roots to hold it, soil is eroded by rain; nutrients are leached out.
  • Flooding: less water is absorbed and transpired, so runoff increases.
  • Increase of CO2 in the atmosphere: less photosynthesis, and burning or decomposing trees release CO2.

Pollution

  • Plastics are non-biodegradable, so they persist. In water they entangle or are swallowed by animals (e.g. turtles, seabirds), and break into microplastics that enter food chains. On land they litter habitats and release toxic chemicals when burned.
  • Untreated sewage and excess fertiliser entering water cause eutrophication.
  • Supplement Female contraceptive hormones in sewage enter rivers and can cause feminisation of male fish (reduced fertility) and may contribute to reduced sperm counts in men.

The enhanced greenhouse effect

  • Carbon dioxide (from combustion and deforestation) and methane (from cattle, rice paddies, landfill) are greenhouse gases.
  • They absorb and re-radiate infrared (heat) energy that would otherwise escape from the Earth, warming the atmosphere.
  • Increasing levels cause climate change: rising sea levels, melting ice, more extreme weather, changes in species distribution.
Model answer
Explain why fish die in a lake after fertiliser runs off nearby fields. [4]
Nitrate and phosphate ions from the fertiliser cause rapid growth of algae. The algae block light, so plants below cannot photosynthesise and die. Decomposing bacteria feed on the dead plants and their population increases. The bacteria respire aerobically and use up the dissolved oxygen, so fish die from lack of oxygen.

Conservation

  • Sustainable resource: one which is produced as rapidly as it is removed from the environment so that it does not run out.
  • Some resources, such as fossil fuels, cannot be replaced; others, such as forests and fish stocks, can be conserved.

Endangered species

Species become endangered or extinct because of climate change, habitat destruction, hunting, overharvesting, pollution and introduced species.

Conservation methods: monitoring and protecting species and habitats, education, captive breeding programmes and seed banks.

Supplement Deeper conservation points

  • Sustainable development: development providing for the needs of an increasing human population without harming the environment; it requires cooperation between local, national and international groups.
  • A population that becomes very small has reduced genetic variation, so it is less able to adapt to change (e.g. new disease) and is at greater risk of extinction.
  • Captive breeding may use artificial insemination and in vitro fertilisation (IVF), and may move animals between zoos to increase genetic variation.
  • Reasons for conservation: reduce extinction, protect vulnerable environments, and maintain ecosystem functions such as nutrient cycling and resource provision (food, drugs, fuel and genes).
Examiner Tip
In eutrophication answers, it is the bacteria (decomposers) respiring that use up the oxygen, not the algae. Say “dissolved oxygen” and “aerobic respiration” to secure the mark.
Common Mistake
Confusing the greenhouse effect with ozone depletion, or saying greenhouse gases “trap the Sun’s rays”. They absorb the infrared radiation emitted by the warmed Earth; the natural greenhouse effect is essential for life, and it is the enhanced effect that causes climate change.
