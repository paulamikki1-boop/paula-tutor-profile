1.1 States of Matter and Diffusion

These questions test kinetic particle theory: naming changes of state, using melting and boiling points to deduce a state, and linking the rate of diffusion to relative molecular mass.

What examiners keep seeing
Saying a gas diffuses faster because it is “lighter” gets no credit — examiners want “lower relative molecular mass”. When describing diffusion, mention both the particles and their random movement.
0620/42/F/M/24 Q5(a) · February/March 2024 · [1 mark]

Suggest why but-1-ene diffuses quicker than butane.

But-1-ene has a lower relative molecular mass (C4H8, Mr = 56) than butane (C4H10, Mr = 58), so its molecules move faster at the same temperature.

Why this scores
One mark for the idea of a lower relative molecular mass. Quoting both Mr values makes the comparison clear; “smaller” or “lighter” is vaguer than the mark-scheme wording.
0620/42/O/N/25 Q1(c)(i) · October/November 2025 · [1 mark]

Source material: Gases diffuse.

Describe, in terms of particles, why gases diffuse.

Gas particles are in constant, random motion, so they move about and spread out until they are evenly mixed.

Why this scores
The mark is for the random movement of the particles. Examiner reports note that describing only the result (“the gas spreads out”) or writing “particles move” without “random” is not enough.
0620/42/F/M/21 Q2(c)(ii) · February/March 2021 · [2 marks]

Source material: The elements shown are gases at room temperature and pressure. … hydrogen nitrogen oxygen chlorine … When separate samples of each of these gases are placed in a container they will diffuse.

State which of these four gases has the highest rate of diffusion. Explain your answer.

Gas: hydrogen

Explanation: hydrogen (H2, Mr = 2) has the lowest relative molecular mass of the four gases, so its molecules move fastest at the same temperature.

Why this scores
One mark for hydrogen and one for “lowest relative molecular mass”. The examiner report says “hydrogen is the lightest gas” was written often and gained no credit.
0620/41/M/J/21 Q3(f)(i) · May/June 2021 · [2 marks]

Source material: The melting points and boiling points of chlorine and potassium chloride are shown. … chlorine –101 –35

Deduce the physical state of chlorine at –75 °C. Use the data in the table to explain your answer.

Physical state: liquid

Explanation: −75 °C is above the melting point of chlorine (−101 °C) but below its boiling point (−35 °C).

Why this scores
One mark for liquid and one for comparing −75 °C with both the melting point and the boiling point. The examiner report notes that many answers used only one of the two values.
0620/42/O/N/21 Q1(b)(i) · October/November 2021 · [2 marks]

Source material: Substances can change state.

Boiling and evaporation are two ways in which a liquid changes into a gas. Describe two differences between boiling and evaporation.

  • Boiling happens only at one fixed temperature (the boiling point), but evaporation happens at any temperature below the boiling point.
  • Boiling takes place throughout the liquid and forms bubbles of gas, but evaporation happens only at the surface of the liquid.
Why this scores
One mark for each genuine difference: a fixed temperature versus any temperature, and bubbles throughout the liquid versus only at the surface. The examiner report warns that “boiling occurs throughout” and “evaporation happens at the surface” are the same point, so writing both on separate lines scores only once.
0620/42/O/N/25 Q1(b) · October/November 2025 · [3 marks]

Source material: The three states of matter are solid, liquid and gas.

Substances can physically change. Name each physical change: … solid to liquid … gas to liquid … solid to aqueous.

  • solid to liquid: melting
  • gas to liquid: condensation
  • solid to aqueous: dissolving
Why this scores
One mark for each name. “Aqueous” means dissolved in water, so the third change is dissolving; a 2021 examiner report notes that weaker answers wrote “mixing” or “diffusing” instead.

2.1 Atoms, Isotopes and Mixtures

These questions test atomic structure: defining isotopes, explaining why isotopes react the same way, calculating relative atomic mass from abundances and matching electronic configurations of atoms and ions.

What examiners keep seeing
In relative atomic mass calculations, examiners see students simply averaging the two isotope masses or copying the Periodic Table value with no working. Multiply each mass by its abundance, add, divide by the total, and show every line.
0620/42/M/J/24 Q5(a)(ii) · May/June 2024 · [1 mark]

Source material: A sample of sulfur contains two isotopes,32S and34S.

State why these isotopes have identical chemical properties.

Both isotopes have the same number of electrons (16), so they have the same electronic configuration (2,8,6).

Why this scores
One mark for the same electronic configuration. “Same number of protons” alone does not explain chemical behaviour — reactions depend on the electrons.
0620/42/M/J/25 Q5(d)(iii) · May/June 2025 · [1 mark]

Source material: Rubidium has only two naturally occurring atoms,85Rb and87Rb.

The relative atomic mass of rubidium to one decimal place is 85.5. Determine the relative abundance of85Rb in rubidium. Express your answer as a percentage.

Let the percentage of85Rb be x, so the percentage of87Rb is (100 − x).
85x + 87(100 − x) = 85.5 × 100
8700 − 2x = 8550
x = 75
75%

Why this scores
One mark for 75%. The examiner report lists common wrong answers such as 85, 72.7 and 99.4 from dividing the masses; setting up one equation in x and (100 − x) avoids this.
0620/41/O/N/25 Q1(b)(i) · October/November 2025 · [2 marks]

Source material: Some elements have many isotopes.

Define the term isotopes.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

Why this scores
One mark for atoms of the same element with the same number of protons, one for different numbers of neutrons. “Different masses” is weaker; the mark scheme names neutrons.
0620/41/O/N/24 Q2(e)(ii) · October/November 2024 · [2 marks]

Source material: A sample of thallium, Tl, contains two isotopes,203Tl and205Tl.

The relative abundance of203Tl :205Tl is in the ratio 3 : 7. Calculate the relative atomic mass of thallium in the sample to one decimal place.

Total mass of 10 atoms = (3 × 203) + (7 × 205) = 609 + 1435 = 2044
Ar = 2044 ÷ (3 + 7) = 2044 ÷ 10
Ar = 204.4

Why this scores
One mark for multiplying each isotope mass by its share of the ratio and adding (2044), one for dividing by 10 to get 204.4. Ar is a relative value, so it has no unit.
0620/43/M/J/25 Q2(c) · May/June 2025 · [2 marks]

Ions are atoms or groups of atoms that have gained or lost one or more electrons. An oxygen atom has the electronic configuration of 2,6. Give the formula of one atom and one positive ion that has the same electronic configuration as O2–.

O2− has gained two electrons, so its configuration is 2,8.

Atom: Ne
Positive ion: Na+

Why this scores
One mark for Ne and one for a positive ion with configuration 2,8 (Na+, Mg2+ or Al3+). The examiner report notes that many wrote names instead of formulae, gave O as the atom, or gave impossible ions such as F+.
0620/41/M/J/20 Q1(c) · May/June 2020 · [3 marks]

Source material: Element Y has only two different types of atom,69Y and71Y. The ratio of atoms present in element Y is shown.69Y :71Y = 3 : 2

Calculate the relative atomic mass of element Y to one decimal place. Identify element Y.

Total mass of 5 atoms = (3 × 69) + (2 × 71) = 207 + 142 = 349
Ar = 349 ÷ 5
Ar = 69.8

Element Y is gallium (Ga), which has Ar = 70 in the Periodic Table.

Why this scores
One mark for the working (3 × 69) + (2 × 71), one for 69.8 and one for gallium. Divide by the total of the ratio (5), not by 2 or 100.

2.4 Ionic Bonding

These questions test how ions form, what an ionic bond is, and how the giant ionic lattice explains high melting points and conduction only when molten or aqueous.

What examiners keep seeing
Describing electron transfer does not define an ionic bond: examiners want the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. Never write “molecules” or “intermolecular ionic bonds” for an ionic compound.
0620/41/M/J/22 Q2(a)(iii) · May/June 2022 · [1 mark]

Explain why magnesium ions have a charge of 2+.

A magnesium atom (2,8,2) loses its two outer electrons, so the ion has 12 protons but only 10 electrons: two more protons than electrons.

Why this scores
The mark is for “two more protons than electrons”. “It loses two electrons” on its own describes how the ion forms; link it to the proton and electron count.
0620/42/M/J/24 Q5(b)(ii) · May/June 2024 · [1 mark]

Source material: Sulfur reacts with magnesium to form magnesium sulfide, MgS, an ionic compound.

State why MgS has a high melting point.

The ionic bonds (electrostatic attractions between Mg2+ and S2− ions) in the giant lattice are strong, so a lot of energy is needed to overcome them.

Why this scores
One mark for “ionic bonds are strong”. Saying only “it has ionic bonds” misses the key word strong.
0620/42/F/M/25 Q2(a) · February/March 2025 · [2 marks]

State what is meant by the term ionic bond.

An ionic bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.

Why this scores
One mark for attraction between ions, one for the ions having opposite charges. The examiner report notes that weaker answers described electron transfer to form full outer shells, which explains how ions form but not what the bond is.
0620/42/F/M/20 Q3(f)(ii) · February/March 2020 · [2 marks]

Source material: Lithium fluoride has ionic bonding.

Give two physical properties of ionic compounds.

  • They have high melting and boiling points.
  • They conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water, but not when solid.
Why this scores
One mark each from: high melting/boiling point, conducts when molten or aqueous, soluble in water. “Conducts electricity” without “when molten or aqueous” is wrong, because solid ionic compounds do not conduct.
0620/41/M/J/21 Q3(f)(ii) · May/June 2021 · [3 marks]

Source material: The melting points and boiling points of chlorine and potassium chloride are shown.

Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, why potassium chloride has a much higher melting point than chlorine. Your answer should refer to the: … types of particle held together by the forces of attraction … types of forces of attraction between particles … relative strength of the forces of attraction.

Potassium chloride is a giant ionic lattice of K+ and Cl− ions held together by ionic bonds (strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions).

Chlorine is made of simple Cl2 molecules. Melting only overcomes the forces of attraction between the molecules; the covalent bonds inside each molecule do not break.

The attraction between molecules in chlorine is much weaker than the ionic bonds in potassium chloride, so far less energy is needed to melt chlorine.

Why this scores
One mark for ionic bonds (between ions) in KCl, one for attraction between molecules in Cl2, and one for stating that the attraction in Cl2 is weaker. The examiner report notes that many answers described only KCl, called the bonds in chlorine “weak covalent bonds”, or never made the comparison.

2.5 Covalent, Giant Covalent and Metallic Structures

These questions test the difference between breaking forces between molecules and breaking covalent bonds, the structures of graphite and silicon(IV) oxide, and which mobile particles let a substance conduct.

What examiners keep seeing
Students often say simple molecules melt or boil because “the covalent bonds are weak” — covalent bonds are strong; it is the attraction between molecules that is weak. For giant structures, examiners want the word “giant”, not “large” or “lattice”.
0620/42/M/J/22 Q3(c)(i) · May/June 2022 · [1 mark]

Explain why transition elements and Group I elements conduct electricity.

Metals have delocalised (mobile) electrons that can move through the lattice of positive ions and carry charge.

Why this scores
The mark is for mobile (delocalised) electrons. On a 2022 graphite question the examiner report noted that “free electrons” gained no credit, so use “mobile” or “delocalised”.
0620/42/M/J/25 Q2(a) · May/June 2025 · [2 marks]

State what is meant by a covalent bond.

A covalent bond is a pair of electrons shared between two atoms.

Why this scores
One mark for a pair of electrons, one for shared between two atoms. The examiner report notes common errors: “two or more shared pairs” (one bond is one pair) and “between two or more atoms”.
0620/42/O/N/22 Q1(d) · October/November 2022 · [2 marks]

Source material: Diamond and graphite are different solid forms of carbon. The carbon atoms in diamond and graphite are arranged in different ways.

Describe the arrangement of atoms in graphite.

The carbon atoms are arranged in flat layers made of hexagonal rings, with each carbon atom covalently bonded to three others.

The layers are stacked on top of each other with only weak forces between them.

Why this scores
One mark for layers, one for hexagonal rings. The examiner report says layers were well known but the hexagonal arrangement within each layer was often left out.
0620/43/M/J/23 Q3(c)(ii) · May/June 2023 · [2 marks]

Source material: The melting points of silicon tetrachloride and silicon(IV) oxide are shown in Table 3.1.

Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, why silicon(IV) oxide has a high melting point.

Silicon(IV) oxide has a giant covalent structure in which every silicon and oxygen atom is joined to its neighbours by covalent bonds.

These covalent bonds are strong and very many must be broken to melt it, which needs a lot of energy.

Why this scores
One mark for covalent bonds, one for strong bonds in a giant structure. The examiner report notes answers that called the structure “large” or a “lattice” instead of giant, or mentioned intermolecular forces, which contradicts a giant covalent structure.
0620/42/M/J/25 Q2(b)(iii) · May/June 2025 · [3 marks]

Source material: Chlorine(I) oxide, Cl2O, is a simple molecule with covalent bonds.

Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, why Cl2O boils at a low temperature and does not thermally decompose into its constituent elements, Cl2 and O2.

Cl2O is made of simple molecules. When it boils, only the weak forces of attraction between the molecules are overcome, which needs little energy, so it boils at a low temperature.

To decompose into Cl2 and O2, the covalent bonds between the Cl and O atoms inside each molecule would have to break.

These covalent bonds are much stronger than the attraction between molecules, so heating makes Cl2O boil long before it decomposes.

Why this scores
One mark for attraction between molecules being overcome on boiling, one for covalent bonds breaking on decomposition, and one for comparing weak attraction between molecules with strong covalent bonds. The examiner report notes that “intermolecular forces between atoms” contradicts itself and loses the mark.
0620/43/O/N/24 Q3(d)(ii) · October/November 2024 · [3 marks]

Source material: Tin(IV) chloride, SnCl4, is covalently bonded.

Tin(II) oxide, SnO, is ionically bonded. … Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, why SnCl4 has a much lower melting point than SnO.

SnCl4 (melting point −33 °C) is made of simple molecules; melting only overcomes the weak attraction between the molecules (intermolecular forces).

SnO (melting point 1080 °C) has a giant ionic structure held together by strong electrostatic attraction between Sn2+ and O2− ions.

The attraction between SnCl4 molecules is much weaker than the ionic bonds in SnO, so much less energy is needed to melt SnCl4.

Why this scores
One mark for intermolecular forces in SnCl4, one for the giant ionic structure of SnO, and one for saying which attraction is weaker. A comparison question needs both substances described and an explicit “weaker/stronger” statement.

3.1 Formulae and Equations

These questions test whether you can build correct formulae from ion charges and turn information given in words into a balanced equation; examiners give one mark for the right formulae in the right places and a second for balancing, so never change a formula to make it balance.

What examiners keep seeing
Examiner reports keep finding formulae copied wrongly even when the paper prints them (HgO written incorrectly, oxygen written as O instead of O2) and equations left unbalanced once the formulae are right; ionic equations also lose marks for missing state symbols or for including spectator ions.
0620/41/M/J/24 Q6(d)(ii) · May/June 2024 · [1 mark]

When zinc oxide neutralises aqueous sodium hydroxide, sodium zincate is formed. The formula of the zincate ion is ZnO22–. Deduce the formula of sodium zincate.

Na2ZnO2

(Two Na+ ions are needed to balance the 2− charge on one ZnO22− ion.)

Why this scores
One mark for Na2ZnO2. Treat the zincate ion like any other 2− ion such as SO42−: total positive charge must equal total negative charge. Writing NaZnO2 (charges not balanced) is the usual slip.
0620/43/O/N/25 Q3(f) · October/November 2025 · [2 marks]

Deduce the formula of: … gallium(III) bromide … gallium(III) sulfate.

gallium(III) bromide: GaBr3 (Ga3+ needs three Br−)

gallium(III) sulfate: Ga2(SO4)3 (2 × 3+ = 6+ balances 3 × 2− = 6−)

Why this scores
One mark for each formula. The Roman numeral (III) tells you the ion is Ga3+, so it behaves exactly like Al3+. Brackets around SO4 are essential: Ga2SO43 or Ga2S3O12 will not be credited.
0620/41/M/J/23 Q4(f) · May/June 2023 · [2 marks]

Oxygen can also be produced by the decomposition of mercury(II) oxide, HgO. The only products of this decomposition are mercury and oxygen. Write a symbol equation for this decomposition.

2HgO → 2Hg + O2

Why this scores
One mark for the correct formulae (HgO, Hg, O2) on the correct sides and one for balancing. The examiner report noted oxygen written as O, Hg2 for mercury and HgO miscopied even though it was printed in the question; oxygen is always diatomic, O2.
0620/43/O/N/22 Q4(c) · October/November 2022 · [2 marks]

Phosphorus oxychloride has the formula POCl3. Phosphorus oxychloride is the only product of the reaction between phosphorus(V) chloride, PCl5, and phosphorus(V) oxide, P4O10. Write a chemical equation for the reaction between phosphorus(V) chloride and phosphorus(V) oxide.

6PCl5 + P4O10 → 10POCl3

Check: P 6 + 4 = 10; Cl 30 = 30; O 10 = 10.

Why this scores
One mark for all three formulae on the correct sides and one for the fully balanced equation. Balance oxygen first: P4O10 gives 10 O, so you need 10POCl3, then 6PCl5 supplies the 30 Cl. The examiner report says many candidates had the right formulae but did not balance.
0620/42/M/J/23 Q4(d) · May/June 2023 · [3 marks]

Complete the word equation for the reaction of NaOH(aq) with ammonium chloride.

sodium hydroxide + ammonium chloride → sodium chloride + ammonia + water

Why this scores
One mark for each product. This is the reaction of an ammonium salt with an alkali, which always gives a salt, ammonia gas and water. The examiner report flagged ‘ammonium’ written instead of ammonia and ‘hydrogen’ instead of water.
0620/43/M/J/24 Q5(a)(iii) · May/June 2024 · [3 marks]

Source material: Barium sulfate, BaSO4, is an insoluble salt and is made by precipitation.

Write an ionic equation for the precipitation reaction which produces barium sulfate. Include state symbols.

Ba2+(aq) + SO42−(aq) → BaSO4(s)

Why this scores
One mark for BaSO4 as the only product, one for Ba2+ and SO42− as the only reactants and one for the state symbols: ions in solution are (aq), the precipitate is (s). Leave out spectator ions such as Na+ and Cl−.

3.3 The Mole and Calculations

These questions test the mole chain: convert what you are given into moles, use the ratio from the equation, then convert into what is asked for; examiners give a mark for each step, so clear working earns credit even if the final number slips.

What examiners keep seeing
The commonest losses are ignoring the mole ratio (treating 2 : 1 as 1 : 1), forgetting to divide cm3 by 1000, and in percentage-by-mass questions counting only one atom of an element (e.g. 56 instead of 112 for Fe in Fe2O3). Examiners also refuse fractions as final answers and Mr values left as an unworked sum.
0620/42/F/M/24 Q1(b)(i) · February/March 2024 · [2 marks]

Calculate the percentage by mass of iron in iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3.

Mr of Fe2O3 = (2 × 56) + (3 × 16) = 160
mass of iron in one formula = 2 × 56 = 112
percentage of iron = (112 ÷ 160) × 100 = 70.0%

Why this scores
One mark for Mr = 160 and one for using 112 to reach 70%. The examiner report notes many candidates used 56 (one Fe) and got 35%, and some used the atomic number of iron instead of its Ar.
0620/43/O/N/24 Q4(f)(i) · October/November 2024 · [2 marks]

Source material: 0.325 g of Zn is added to dilute sulfuric acid which contains 0.0100 moles of H2SO4. The equation for this reaction is shown. Zn + H2SO4 → ZnSO4 + H2

Determine whether Zn or H2SO4 is the limiting reactant. Explain your answer.

moles of Zn = 0.325 ÷ 65 = 0.00500 mol

Zn is the limiting reactant: Zn and H2SO4 react in a 1 : 1 ratio, and there are fewer moles of Zn (0.00500) than of H2SO4 (0.0100), so the zinc is used up first and the acid is in excess.

Why this scores
One mark for 0.00500 mol of Zn and one for concluding Zn is limiting with the reason: fewer moles and a 1 : 1 ratio. Comparing masses (0.325 g against 0.0100 mol) instead of moles earns nothing.
0620/43/O/N/25 Q6(a) · October/November 2025 · [3 marks]

Organic compound Q has the following composition by mass. C, 50.00%; H, 5.56%; O, 44.44% Calculate the empirical formula of compound Q.

C: 50.00 ÷ 12 = 4.17; H: 5.56 ÷ 1 = 5.56; O: 44.44 ÷ 16 = 2.78
divide by the smallest (2.78): C 1.5 : H 2 : O 1
multiply by 2 to get whole numbers: C 3 : H 4 : O 2
empirical formula = C3H4O2

Why this scores
One mark for dividing each percentage by its Ar, one for the ratio 1.5 : 2 : 1 (or 3 : 4 : 2) and one for C3H4O2. Do not round 1.5 to 2; a ratio ending in .5 means multiply everything by 2. Examiners also see atomic numbers used instead of Ar values.
0620/41/M/J/24 Q5(d)(ii) · May/June 2024 · [4 marks]

In an experiment, 1.405 g of CoSO4•xH2O is heated until all the water is given off. The mass of CoSO4 that remains is 0.775 g. [Mr: CoSO4, 155; H2O, 18] … Determine the value of x using the following steps.
Calculate the number of moles of CoSO4 that remains.
Calculate the mass of H2O given off.
Calculate the number of moles of H2O given off.
Determine the value of x.

moles of CoSO4 = 0.775 ÷ 155 = 0.00500 mol
mass of H2O = 1.405 − 0.775 = 0.630 g
moles of H2O = 0.630 ÷ 18 = 0.0350 mol
x = 0.0350 ÷ 0.00500 = 7

Why this scores
One mark per step: 0.00500 mol, 0.630 g, 0.0350 mol and x = 7. x is the ratio of moles of water to moles of anhydrous salt, so divide water by CoSO4, not the other way round, and give a whole number.
0620/41/O/N/24 Q4(a)(iii) · October/November 2024 · [4 marks]

Source material: When magnesium nitrate is heated strongly, magnesium oxide is formed. The equation for this reaction is shown. 2Mg(NO3)2 → 2MgO + 4NO2 + O2

Calculate the volume of NO2 gas, at r.t.p., formed when 7.40 g of Mg(NO3)2 is heated. Use the following steps.
Calculate the Mr of Mg(NO3)2.
Calculate the number of moles of Mg(NO3)2 used.
Determine the number of moles of NO2 formed.
Calculate the volume of NO2 gas, in cm3, at r.t.p.

Mr of Mg(NO3)2 = 24 + 2 × (14 + 3 × 16) = 148
moles of Mg(NO3)2 = 7.40 ÷ 148 = 0.0500 mol
mole ratio Mg(NO3)2 : NO2 = 2 : 4, so moles of NO2 = 0.0500 × 2 = 0.100 mol
volume of NO2 = 0.100 × 24 000 = 2400 cm3

Why this scores
One mark for each step: 148, 0.0500 mol, 0.100 mol and 2400 cm3. The answer is asked for in cm3, so use 24 000 cm3 per mole (24 dm3 would give 2.40, which needs the unit dm3). Forgetting the 2 : 4 ratio halves the answer.
0620/42/M/J/23 Q4(g) · May/June 2023 · [5 marks]

7.1 Acids, Bases and Oxides

These questions test the proton-transfer definitions, strong versus weak acids, neutralisation and the classes of oxide; examiners reward the exact syllabus wording and the right type of arrow.

What examiners keep seeing
Using a one-way arrow for a weak acid’s dissociation was the commonest error in the 2023 and 2024 reports; also keep “strong” (how fully it dissociates) separate from “concentrated” (how much acid per dm3).
0620/42/O/N/22 Q2(c)(i) · October/November 2022 · [1 mark]

Explain in terms of proton transfer what is meant by a base.

A base is a proton acceptor: it accepts a hydrogen ion, H+, from an acid.

Why this scores
One mark for “proton acceptor”, the syllabus definition, which the examiner report says was well known. Talking about OH− ions or pH does not answer a question asked “in terms of proton transfer”.
0620/42/M/J/23 Q4(f)(iv) · May/June 2023 · [1 mark]

Write the ionic equation for the reaction when an acid neutralises a soluble base.

H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l)

Why this scores
One mark for the equation. A soluble base is an alkali, so the only ions that react are H+ from the acid and OH− from the alkali; the examiner report says only the stronger candidates recalled this newer syllabus point.
0620/43/O/N/24 Q3(b)(i) · October/November 2024 · [1 mark]

Source material: SnO2 is an amphoteric oxide.

State the meaning of the term amphoteric.

An amphoteric oxide reacts with both acids and bases, forming a salt and water in each case.

Why this scores
One mark: the answer must say it reacts with both acids and bases (alkalis). “Neutral” or “reacts with acids” alone scores nothing; ending with “to form a salt and water” matches the mark scheme exactly.
0620/42/F/M/24 Q1(d)(i) · February/March 2024 · [2 marks]

Source material: Equations for some of the reactions in the blast furnace are shown. … equation 3 CaO + SiO2 → CaSiO3

Explain why the reaction in equation 3 can be described as an acid–base reaction.

CaO is a metal oxide, so it is a basic oxide.

SiO2 is a non-metal oxide, so it is an acidic oxide. The basic oxide neutralises the acidic oxide to form a salt, calcium silicate.

Why this scores
One mark for CaO being basic and one for SiO2 being acidic. The examiner report says weaker answers just wrote “neutralisation” without saying which oxide is the acid and which is the base.
0620/42/O/N/25 Q4(a)(ii) · October/November 2025 · [2 marks]

Source material: Ethanoic acid, CH3COOH, is a weak acid. One mole of each acid is added separately to 1000 cm3 of distilled water to form a dilute solution.

Explain, in terms of dissociation, why: • nitric acid is described as a strong acid … • ethanoic acid is described as a weak acid.

Nitric acid is completely dissociated into ions in water: HNO3 → H+ + NO3−.

Ethanoic acid is only partially dissociated: most molecules stay as CH3COOH, so an equilibrium forms, CH3COOH ⇌ CH3COO− + H+.

Why this scores
One mark for “completely dissociated” for the strong acid and one for “partially dissociated” for the weak acid. Answers about concentration or pH alone do not score, because strength is about how far the acid dissociates.
0620/42/F/M/24 Q3(e) · February/March 2024 · [3 marks]

Complete the equation to show the dissociation of ethanoic acid, CH3COOH, in aqueous solution.

CH3COOH(aq) ⇌ CH3COO−(aq) + H+(aq)

The reversible arrow is needed because ethanoic acid is a weak acid: it only partially dissociates, so an equilibrium forms.

Why this scores
One mark each for H+, for CH3COO− and for the reversible arrow. The 2023 and 2024 examiner reports say the commonest error was a one-way arrow; some candidates also released 4H+, but only the H in the COOH group ionises.

7.3 Preparation of Salts

These questions test the solubility rules and the three ways of making a salt; examiners reward a method that suits the salt, the key practical steps in order, and observations you can actually see.

What examiners keep seeing
Writing conclusions instead of observations (“no more gas is produced”, “the reaction stops”), and in precipitation questions mixing the two solids without dissolving them first or washing the residue without saying “with distilled water”.
0620/42/F/M/24 Q2(g)(i) · February/March 2024 · [1 mark]

Source material: Aqueous lead(II) ions are added to aqueous chloride ions. A white precipitate of insoluble lead(II) chloride, PbCl2, is formed.

Name a lead(II) compound which can be used in this reaction.

Lead(II) nitrate (all nitrates are soluble, so it supplies Pb2+(aq) ions).

Why this scores
One mark for lead(II) nitrate: the compound must be soluble to give aqueous lead(II) ions. The examiner report says “lead bromide” was the commonest wrong answer, but lead(II) halides are insoluble.
0620/43/M/J/25 Q3(c)(i) · May/June 2025 · [2 marks]

Source material: Zinc sulfate crystals are made by the reaction between zinc carbonate and dilute sulfuric acid, using the following steps. step 1 An excess of powdered zinc carbonate is added to dilute sulfuric acid.

Give two observations which show that the zinc carbonate is in excess in step 1.

1. Extra zinc carbonate stops dissolving: some white solid is left at the bottom of the beaker.

2. There is no more fizzing when more zinc carbonate is added.

Why this scores
One mark for each observation. The examiner report says these were the only two accepted: “no more gas is produced” and “the reaction stops” are not observations, and the leftover solid must not be called a precipitate.
0620/42/O/N/25 Q3(f) · October/November 2025 · [2 marks]

Source material: The student adds aqueous sodium sulfate, Na2SO4(aq), to aqueous barium chloride, BaCl2(aq). … step 3 Stir the mixture and then filter it. step 4 Dry the solid left in the filter paper.

The actual mass of dry BaSO4 collected in step 4 is greater than the expected mass. This is because the student should do an additional step between step 3 and step 4. Suggest what the student should do in this additional step, and explain why the actual mass collected is greater than the expected mass.

The student should wash the residue in the filter paper with a little distilled water before drying it.

Unwashed, the solid is still wet with the filtrate, which contains dissolved sodium chloride. When it dries, this NaCl is left on the barium sulfate and adds to its mass.

Why this scores
One mark for washing (rinsing) the residue with water and one for explaining that the extra mass is soluble sodium chloride left on the solid.
0620/42/O/N/25 Q3(h) · October/November 2025 · [3 marks]

Source material: A student prepares a sample of the insoluble salt barium sulfate, BaSO4. The student adds aqueous sodium sulfate, Na2SO4(aq), to aqueous barium chloride, BaCl2(aq).

State the name of a different: • barium salt that can be used in place of BaCl2 … • sulfate salt that can be used in place of Na2SO4 … • barium salt that can be made by this method.

  • Instead of BaCl2: barium nitrate (soluble).
  • Instead of Na2SO4: potassium sulfate (or ammonium sulfate), which is soluble.
  • Another barium salt made this way: barium carbonate, which is insoluble (e.g. mix barium nitrate and sodium carbonate solutions).
Why this scores
One mark for each. The first two must be soluble (all nitrates, and all sodium, potassium and ammonium salts, are soluble); the third must be insoluble, because precipitation only works when the product is insoluble.
0620/41/M/J/21 Q5(a) · May/June 2021 · [5 marks]

Source material: Lead(II) iodide, PbI2, is insoluble in water. … All sodium salts are soluble in water.

Salts that are insoluble in water are made by precipitation. You are provided with solid lead(II) nitrate, Pb(NO3)2, and solid sodium iodide, NaI. Describe how you would make a pure sample of lead(II) iodide by precipitation. Your answer should include: … practical details … a chemical equation for the precipitation reaction.

  1. Dissolve the lead(II) nitrate in distilled water in one beaker, and the sodium iodide in distilled water in another.
  2. Mix the two solutions and stir: a yellow precipitate of lead(II) iodide forms.
  3. Filter the mixture; the lead(II) iodide stays in the filter paper as the residue.
  4. Wash the residue with distilled water to remove the soluble sodium nitrate.
  5. Dry the solid between filter papers or in a warm oven.

Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2NaI(aq) → PbI2(s) + 2NaNO3(aq)

Why this scores
Marks for dissolving the solids in water, making the two solutions, filtering, washing with water and drying, plus the balanced equation (the ionic equation also scores). The examiner report says many mixed the two solids without dissolving them, said “wash” without saying with water, wrote PbI instead of PbI2, or wasted time crystallising the filtrate.
0620/43/O/N/21 Q5(b) · October/November 2021 · [5 marks]

Source material: 20.0 cm3 of dilute sulfuric acid neutralises 25.0 cm3 of 1.00 mol / dm3 aqueous sodium hydroxide. At the end of the titration the conical flask contains aqueous sodium sulfate with the dissolved indicator as an impurity.

Describe how to prepare a pure sample of sodium sulfate crystals from the original solutions of dilute sulfuric acid and aqueous sodium hydroxide of the same concentrations. You are not required to give details of how to carry out the titration.

  1. Mix 25.0 cm3 of the sodium hydroxide with exactly 20.0 cm3 of the sulfuric acid again, but without the indicator, so the sodium sulfate solution is pure.
  2. Pour the solution into an evaporating basin and heat it to evaporate some of the water.
  3. Stop heating at the saturation point (crystals form on a cold glass rod dipped into it); do not heat to dryness.
  4. Leave the solution to cool so that crystals form.
  5. Filter off the crystals and dry them between filter papers.
Why this scores
One mark each for: repeating with the same volumes but no indicator, heating, stopping at the saturation (crystallisation) point, cooling to crystallise, and describing how to dry. The examiner report says many described the titration anyway, did not say when to stop heating, left out crystallising, or wrote “dry” without saying how.

8.1 Periodic Table, Group I and Noble Gases

These questions test how the Periodic Table is arranged and the trends and reactions of Group I; examiners reward correct directions for trends, real observations and correct formulae.

What examiners keep seeing
Giving conclusions such as “hydrogen is given off” as observations, and writing wrong formulae: sodium oxide is Na2O, and a Group I metal with water gives the hydroxide (KOH), not the oxide.
0620/42/M/J/25 Q1(c) · May/June 2025 · [1 mark]

Source material: Using numbers only, state the:

number of metals in Period 3 of the Periodic Table

3

(Na, Mg and Al are metals; across the period the elements become non-metallic, and Si is not counted as a metal.)

Why this scores
One mark for 3. Across a period, elements change from metallic to non-metallic character. The question says “numbers only”, so write just the number on the answer line.
0620/43/O/N/20 Q5(a) · October/November 2020 · [1 mark]

Describe the trend in the reactivity of Group I elements.

Reactivity increases down the group (lithium is the least reactive).

Why this scores
One mark, but the direction is essential. The examiner report says “reactivity increases” with no direction, or “they are very reactive”, did not score.
0620/42/F/M/25 Q3(f) · February/March 2025 · [1 mark]

Name the noble gas which has the same electronic configuration as a Br – ion.

Krypton. A bromine atom gains one electron to form Br−, which then has 36 electrons, the same as krypton at the end of Period 4.

Why this scores
One mark for krypton. Use the Periodic Table printed in the paper: the examiner report says weaker candidates lost easy marks in this question by not using it.
0620/43/M/J/25 Q6(c)(i) · May/June 2025 · [2 marks]

Give two observations when lithium is added to water.

1. Fizzing (bubbles).

2. The lithium floats on the water and slowly disappears.

Why this scores
One mark for each different observation (fizzing, floats, moves on the surface, solid disappears). The examiner report says “a gas is produced” is not an observation, and “fizzing” plus “bubbling” only scores once because they are the same thing.
0620/42/O/N/21 Q5(d)(v) · October/November 2021 · [2 marks]

Write the chemical equation for the reaction of sodium with oxygen.

4Na + O2 → 2Na2O

Why this scores
One mark for the formula Na2O (two Na+ ions balance one O2− ion) and one for balancing. The examiner report says only a small proportion got the formula of sodium oxide right.
0620/43/M/J/22 Q6(a)(ii) · May/June 2022 · [5 marks]

Source material: The Group I metals react with cold water. Transition elements do not react with cold water.

Describe the observations when potassium is added to cold water. Write a balanced equation for the reaction. Include state symbols.

Observations: the potassium floats and moves quickly around on the surface; it fizzes vigorously; it melts into a ball; the hydrogen catches fire with a lilac flame; the potassium soon disappears.

Equation: 2K(s) + 2H2O(l) → 2KOH(aq) + H2(g)

Why this scores
Two marks for any two observations, then one for KOH or H2 as a product, one for the balanced equation and one for state symbols. The examiner report says K2O was a common wrong product and water was often wrongly given as (aq).

8.3 Group VII: the Halogens

These questions test the trends down Group VII and displacement reactions; examiners reward correct colours, the halogen/halide distinction and correctly balanced (ionic) equations.

What examiners keep seeing
Mixing up the halogen and its halide ion (bromine and bromide), and not knowing that potassium halide solutions are colourless; both are flagged in the 2022 and 2024 examiner reports.
0620/41/M/J/22 Q7(c) · May/June 2022 · [1 mark]

Group VII elements are known as the halogens. Astatine is below iodine in Group VII. Predict the physical state of astatine at room temperature and pressure.

Solid: melting and boiling points increase down Group VII, and iodine, above astatine, is already a solid.

Why this scores
One mark for solid. The examiner report says “gas” was the commonest wrong answer; the states go gas → liquid → solid down the group, so astatine continues the trend.
0620/42/M/J/23 Q2(b) · May/June 2023 · [1 mark]

Explain why Group VII elements have similar chemical properties.

Their atoms all have the same number of electrons in the outer shell (seven).

Why this scores
One mark. The examiner report says answers that left out the word “number” (“same electrons in the outer shell”) did not get credit.
0620/42/M/J/23 Q2(e)(ii) · May/June 2023 · [1 mark]

Source material: Chlorine displaces bromine from aqueous potassium bromide but does not displace fluorine from aqueous sodium fluoride.

State why chlorine does not displace fluorine from aqueous sodium fluoride.

Chlorine is less reactive than fluorine.

Why this scores
One mark. A halogen can only displace a less reactive halogen from its halide, and reactivity decreases down Group VII. Compare the two elements; “chlorine is not reactive enough” does not say what it is compared with.
0620/43/M/J/22 Q6(c)(i) · May/June 2022 · [2 marks]

Source material: When aqueous bromine is added to aqueous potassium iodide a reaction occurs.

Describe the colour change of the solution.

Original colour of potassium iodide solution: colourless.

Final colour of reaction mixture: brown (iodine has been displaced).

Why this scores
One mark for each colour. The examiner report says most candidates did not know that potassium iodide solution is colourless; the brown colour is the iodine formed when bromine displaces it.
0620/41/M/J/22 Q7(d)(ii) · May/June 2022 · [2 marks]

Source material: When aqueous chlorine is added to aqueous potassium bromide a reaction occurs. The ionic half-equations for the reaction are shown. Cl2(aq) + 2e– → 2Cl–(aq) 2Br–(aq) → Br2(aq) + 2e–

Identify the species that is oxidised. Explain your decision.

Species oxidised: bromide ions, Br−.

Explanation: each bromide ion loses an electron, and oxidation is loss of electrons.

Why this scores
One mark for Br− and one for “loses electrons”. The examiner report says “bromine” was the commonest wrong species; bromine is the product, not the species that is oxidised.
0620/41/O/N/23 Q3(b)(ii) · October/November 2023 · [3 marks]

Source material: A student bubbles chlorine gas into a test-tube containing aqueous potassium bromide.

Complete the ionic equation for this reaction. Include state symbols.

Cl2(g) + 2Br−(aq) → 2Cl−(aq) + Br2(aq)

(K+ ions are spectator ions, so they are left out.)

Why this scores
One mark for Br2 and Cl− as products, one for the balanced equation and one for state symbols. Chlorine is (g) here because it is bubbled in as a gas.

8.4 Transition Elements

These questions test the properties that set transition elements apart from Group I metals; examiners reward properties that are special to transition elements and comparisons that name both groups.

What examiners keep seeing
Giving properties shared by all metals (conducting electricity, forming positive ions or basic oxides) or only physical properties when chemical ones are asked for; the 2022 reports also warn against “it” or “they” when comparing two groups.
0620/42/F/M/23 Q4(c)(i) · February/March 2023 · [1 mark]

Source material: Some physical and chemical properties of transition elements are shown. physical properties: … high density … high strength

State one other physical property of transition elements.

High melting point.

Why this scores
One mark. High density and high strength are already given, so repeating them scores nothing; the mark scheme looks for high melting point.
0620/43/O/N/24 Q5(c)(i) · October/November 2024 · [2 marks]

Source material: Manganese(IV) oxide is the catalyst in this reaction.

Explain the meaning of (IV) in manganese(IV) oxide.

(IV) is the oxidation number of manganese in the compound: manganese is +4. Manganese is a transition element with variable oxidation numbers, so the Roman numeral shows which one is present.

Why this scores
One mark for “oxidation number of manganese” and one for +4. “It has four of something” misses both the term and the positive sign.
0620/42/M/J/22 Q3(b)(ii) · May/June 2022 · [2 marks]

Source material: One chemical property of transition elements is that they form coloured compounds.

State two other chemical properties of transition elements.

1. They and their compounds act as catalysts.

2. They have variable oxidation numbers (e.g. iron forms Fe2+ and Fe3+).

Why this scores
One mark each. The examiner report says many gave physical properties, or chemical properties shared by all metals such as forming basic oxides or positive ions.
0620/43/M/J/20 Q7(e)(ii) · May/June 2020 · [2 marks]

Source material: Iron is a transition element.

Give two ways in which the properties of transition elements differ from the properties of Group I metals.

1. Transition elements have much higher melting points than Group I metals.

2. Transition elements and their compounds act as catalysts; Group I metals do not.

Why this scores
One mark each from: higher melting point, higher density, harder, act as catalysts, variable oxidation numbers, coloured compounds. Each point is a comparison, so make clear which group has the property.
0620/43/M/J/25 Q5(b)(iii) · May/June 2025 · [2 marks]

Source material: Ethanoic acid is manufactured in the reaction between methanol and carbon monoxide.

Suggest which element from the list is a suitable catalyst for the reaction. Give a reason for your answer. aluminium carbon cobalt magnesium sodium

Catalyst: cobalt.

Reason: cobalt is a transition element, and transition elements and their compounds often act as catalysts.

Why this scores
One mark for cobalt and one for the reason that it is a transition element. The examiner report says many did not use the Periodic Table to spot cobalt as the only transition element, and chose others for being “very reactive” or “unreactive”.

9.1 Properties, Uses and Alloys of Metals

These questions test what an alloy is, why alloys are harder than pure metals, and how a metal’s use follows from its properties; examiners reward exact property words (ductile, low density, good conductor, resists corrosion) and a particle explanation built on layers.

What examiners keep seeing
Calling an alloy a “compound” or a “combination of elements”, or forgetting that it must contain a metal. In the hardness explanation, saying the particles are “closer together” or the bonds are “stronger” instead of describing different-sized atoms that stop the layers sliding.
0620/43/M/J/25 Q3(b)(i) · May/June 2025 · [1 mark]

Source material: Zinc is present in alloys such as brass.

State the meaning of the term alloy.

An alloy is a mixture of a metal with one or more other elements (for example, brass is copper mixed with zinc).

Why this scores
One mark, and it needs two ideas: the word “mixture” and the presence of a metal. The examiner report says many candidates wrote “compound” or “combination of elements”, or left out the metal, and lost the mark.
0620/41/M/J/25 Q3(b)(i) · May/June 2025 · [1 mark]

Source material: Copper is in alloys such as brass.

State one reason why alloys are more useful than pure metals.

Alloys are harder and stronger than the pure metals they are made from.

Why this scores
“Harder” or “stronger” earns the mark. Answers such as “better” or “more useful” only repeat the question; the examiner report advises learning the syllabus statement word for word.
0620/42/F/M/23 Q4(b)(i) · February/March 2023 · [1 mark]

Source material: Copper can be stretched into wires. Copper wires conduct electricity.

Name the property of metals which means that they can be stretched into wires.

Ductility (metals are ductile).

Why this scores
One mark for ductile / ductility. Do not confuse it with “malleable”, which means it can be hammered into shape.
0620/42/O/N/24 Q2(e) · October/November 2024 · [1 mark]

State why aluminium is used in food containers.

Aluminium resists corrosion: its protective oxide layer stops it reacting with the food or drink.

Why this scores
The mark is for resistance to corrosion. “Light” or “cheap” does not explain why it is safe to keep food in, so always link the use to the property that matters for that use.
0620/41/O/N/23 Q2(d) · October/November 2023 · [2 marks]

State two physical properties of aluminium that make it suitable for use in overhead electrical cables.

1 It is a good conductor of electricity.

2 It has a low density, so the long cables are light enough for the pylons to hold up.

Why this scores
One mark each for good electrical conductor and low density. The question asks for physical properties, so a chemical property such as resisting corrosion is not the answer it wants.
0620/41/O/N/20 Q2(c) · October/November 2020 · [3 marks]

Brass is an alloy of zinc. Explain, in terms of particles, why brass is harder than pure zinc.

In pure zinc the atoms are all the same size and are arranged in regular layers.

Brass also contains copper atoms, which are a different size, and these disrupt the regular layers.

So it is harder for the layers to slide over each other when a force is applied, which makes brass harder.

Why this scores
One mark each for: layers of atoms, different-sized atoms in the alloy, and layers less able to slide. The examiner report found this very difficult for candidates; common wrong ideas were particles “closer together”, “stronger bonds” or “stronger intermolecular forces”.

9.4 Reactivity Series and Corrosion

These questions use the reactivity series to predict reactions with steam and displacement reactions, explain why aluminium seems unreactive, and explain how rusting is stopped; examiners reward comparisons made directly with iron and explanations in terms of electrons or oxidation.

What examiners keep seeing
In sacrificial protection, saying the magnesium or zinc “rusts” instead of the iron (only iron and steel rust), claiming it works as a barrier, or writing “sacrificial protection” without explaining that the more reactive metal loses electrons in preference to iron (examiner reports 2020 and 2021).
0620/41/M/J/21 Q6(d)(ii) · May/June 2021 · [1 mark]

Source material: Steel consists mainly of iron. Iron forms rust when it reacts with water and oxygen. Magnesium blocks can be attached to the bottom of steel boats.

Explain why replacing the magnesium blocks with copper blocks will not prevent the bottom of the boat from rusting.

Copper is less reactive than iron, so the iron would still lose electrons (be oxidised) and rust instead of the copper.

Why this scores
The mark needs a comparison with iron. The examiner report says “copper is less reactive than magnesium” and “copper is unreactive” were common and did not score.
0620/43/M/J/20 Q7(e)(i) · May/June 2020 · [2 marks]

Iron forms hydrated iron(III) oxide when it rusts. Write a word equation to represent the formation of rust.

iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide

Why this scores
One mark for the reactants (iron with both oxygen and water) and one for the product. Leaving out water or oxygen loses a mark, because rusting needs both.
0620/42/M/J/24 Q2(c)(iii) · May/June 2024 · [2 marks]

Source material: Most Group II metals form a gas when placed into cold water. An alkaline solution is also formed.

One Group II metal reacts very slowly when placed in cold water. When heated, the metal reacts with steam to form a white solid. Identify this metal and name the white solid formed.

Metal: magnesium

White solid: magnesium oxide

Why this scores
One mark for magnesium and one for magnesium oxide. With steam a metal forms its oxide (plus hydrogen), not its hydroxide, so “magnesium hydroxide” does not score.
0620/41/M/J/21 Q6(d)(i) · May/June 2021 · [2 marks]

Source material: Steel consists mainly of iron. Iron forms rust when it reacts with water and oxygen. Magnesium blocks can be attached to the bottom of steel boats.

Explain how the magnesium blocks prevent iron from rusting.

Magnesium is more reactive than iron.

So the magnesium loses electrons (is oxidised) in preference to the iron; the magnesium corrodes away and the iron is not oxidised.

Why this scores
One mark for magnesium being more reactive than iron and one for magnesium being oxidised / losing electrons instead of iron. The examiner report says “sacrificial protection” with no explanation, “magnesium rusts instead” and “magnesium forms a barrier” were common answers that did not score.
0620/43/O/N/24 Q3(e)(ii) · October/November 2024 · [2 marks]

Source material: Part of the reactivity series is shown. magnesium most reactive aluminium tin copper least reactive

An aqueous solution of tin(II) sulfate contains Sn2+ ions. Two experiments are carried out. Experiment 1 Copper is added to aqueous tin(II) sulfate. Experiment 2 Magnesium is added to aqueous tin(II) sulfate. Write an ionic equation for any reaction that occurs in each experiment. If no reaction occurs, write ‘no reaction’.

Experiment 1: no reaction (copper is below tin, so it cannot displace it).

Experiment 2: Mg + Sn2+ → Mg2+ + Sn

Why this scores
One mark for “no reaction” in Experiment 1 and one for the ionic equation in Experiment 2. Sulfate ions do not change, so they are spectator ions and must not appear in the ionic equation.
0620/41/O/N/23 Q2(e) · October/November 2023 · [2 marks]

Source material: A student determines the concentration of a solution of dilute sulfuric acid, H2SO4, by titration with aqueous sodium hydroxide, NaOH. … 25.0 cm3 of 0.200 mol / dm3 NaOH is transferred into a conical flask.

Name the piece of apparatus used to measure exactly 25.0 cm3 of 0.200 mol / dm3 NaOH in step 1.

(Volumetric) pipette.

Why this scores
One mark for pipette. The examiner report says “measuring cylinder” was the commonest wrong answer: the volume is given to one decimal place (25.0 cm3), which only a pipette measures accurately enough.
0620/41/M/J/22 Q3(c)(iii) · May/June 2022 · [1 mark]

Source material: The titration is repeated several times until a suitable number of results is obtained.

State how the student decides that a suitable number of results have been obtained.

When at least two titres are concordant: they agree to within 0.20 cm3 of each other (ideally 0.10 cm3).

Why this scores
One mark for two results within 0.2 cm3 (or less) of each other, so a number is essential. The examiner report did not credit vague words such as “similar” or “close”, or “take the average”.
0620/42/M/J/22 Q2(d)(iii) · May/June 2022 · [1 mark]

Source material: A 25.0 cm3 sample of limewater is placed in a conical flask. The concentration of Ca(OH)2 in the limewater is determined by titration with dilute hydrochloric acid, HCl.

As well as limewater and dilute hydrochloric acid, state what other type of substance must be added to the conical flask.

An indicator (e.g. a few drops of methyl orange).

Why this scores
The question asks for the type of substance, so “indicator” is the mark; a named indicator is only credited if the word indicator is there. The examiner report notes universal indicator was wrongly given by weaker candidates.
0620/43/O/N/24 Q4(a)(ii) · October/November 2024 · [1 mark]

Source material: Dilute sulfuric acid and aqueous sodium hydroxide can be used to prepare sodium sulfate crystals using a method that involves titration. … Thymolphthalein is used as an indicator for this titration.

Suggest why universal indicator is not used for this titration.

Universal indicator changes through many colours gradually as the pH changes, so there is no single sharp colour change to show the end-point.

Why this scores
One mark for the idea of too many colour changes (no sharp end-point). Saying it “is not accurate” without the reason does not score.
0620/43/O/N/24 Q4(a)(i) · October/November 2024 · [2 marks]

Source material: Dilute sulfuric acid and aqueous sodium hydroxide can be used to prepare sodium sulfate crystals using a method that involves titration. … Thymolphthalein is used as an indicator for this titration.

State the colour change of thymolphthalein at the end-point of this titration.

From blue to colourless.

Why this scores
One mark for each colour, in the right order: the alkali starts in the conical flask, so the indicator starts blue and turns colourless when the acid neutralises it. Write “colourless”, never “clear”.
0620/41/O/N/25 Q3(f) · October/November 2025 · [2 marks]

Source material: The student adds a few drops of methyl orange indicator to the NaOH(aq) in the conical flask. step 3 The student adds 0.200 mol / dm3 H2SO4(aq) to the flask until the end-point is reached.

State the colour change observed in step 3.

From yellow to orange.

Why this scores
One mark for yellow (methyl orange in alkali) and one for orange (the end-point). Red is the colour in excess acid, so it is not the end-point colour; a similar question in 2022 found most students wrongly started with orange.

12.3 Chromatography, Separation and Purification

These questions test whether you can name and describe separation methods step by step; marks go to the key verbs (filter, wash, dry, heat to saturation, cool) and key terms (locating agent, fractional distillation).

What examiners keep seeing
Mixing up the residue and the filtrate: to get an insoluble solid you wash and dry the residue, while crystallising the filtrate is for a soluble salt. Heating a solution to dryness also loses marks.
0620/43/M/J/20 Q4(c)(iii) · May/June 2020 · [1 mark]

Source material: Chromatography can be used to test the purity of substances.

State how a colourless substance can be made visible on a chromatogram.

Spray the dried chromatogram with a locating agent, which makes the spots show up.

Why this scores
One mark for “locating agent”; the key term is what is credited, so use it rather than a vague “spray a chemical on it”.
0620/43/M/J/24 Q1(f) · May/June 2024 · [1 mark]

Source material: Name the process used to:

separate a mixture of several liquids with different boiling points

Fractional distillation.

Why this scores
One mark for fractional distillation. “Distillation” alone is simple distillation, which only separates a solvent from a solution.
0620/41/O/N/24 Q6(e) · October/November 2024 · [2 marks]

A mixture of the three amino acids, A, B and C, can be separated and the amino acids identiﬁed using paper chromatography. Complete the equation for Rf.

Rf = distance travelled by the substance (spot) ÷ distance travelled by the solvent (solvent front), both measured from the baseline.

Why this scores
One mark for the distance moved by the substance on top and one for the distance moved by the solvent underneath. Writing it upside down gives Rf values above 1, which is impossible.
0620/42/M/J/22 Q2(c)(iii) · May/June 2022 · [2 marks]

Source material: Limewater is a saturated solution of calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2(aq).

Describe how you would make a sample of limewater starting with solid calcium hydroxide.

Add excess solid calcium hydroxide to distilled water and stir until no more dissolves (so the solution is saturated).

Filter off the undissolved solid; the filtrate is limewater.

Why this scores
One mark for adding excess solid to water, one for filtering. The examiner report called this the hardest question on the paper: many left out filtration, and weaker answers used excess water, which does not give a saturated solution.
0620/41/O/N/24 Q3(d)(iii) · October/November 2024 · [2 marks]

Source material: Crystals of hydrated copper(II) sulfate can be obtained from aqueous copper(II) sulfate by crystallisation.

Describe how this crystallisation is done.

Heat the solution in an evaporating basin to evaporate some of the water until it is saturated (a drop on a cold glass rod forms crystals).

Then leave it to cool so that crystals form.

Why this scores
One mark for heating, one for heating only to the saturation (crystallisation) point and then cooling. Heating to dryness does not score, because it drives off the water of crystallisation.
0620/41/M/J/23 Q6(d)(iii) · May/June 2023 · [3 marks]

Source material: Lead(II) sulfate is an insoluble salt.

The precipitate of lead(II) sulfate forms in an aqueous solution. Describe how pure lead(II) sulfate can be obtained from the mixture.

Filter the mixture; lead(II) sulfate stays on the filter paper as the residue.

Wash the residue with a little distilled water to remove the soluble ions left on it.

Dry it between sheets of filter paper (or in a warm oven).

Why this scores
One mark each for filter, wash with distilled water and a way of drying. The examiner report says many students crystallised the filtrate instead: the product is the insoluble residue, not the solution.

12.5 Identification of Ions and Gases

Recall questions on tests for ions and gases; every test needs the reagent and the exact observation (colour, precipitate, whether it dissolves in excess).

What examiners keep seeing
Giving the wrong precipitate colour (white for iodide instead of yellow) or a non-test such as “pungent smell” for ammonia; the examiner reports also note students forgetting to add sodium hydroxide in excess.
0620/42/M/J/21 Q2(c)(i) · May/June 2021 · [1 mark]

Source material: Aqueous silver nitrate is a colourless solution containing Ag+(aq) ions.

Describe what is seen when aqueous silver nitrate is added to aqueous sodium iodide, NaI(aq).

A yellow precipitate forms.

Why this scores
One mark for yellow precipitate (silver iodide). The examiner report says the commonest error was “white precipitate”, which is chloride.
0620/42/F/M/25 Q5(d)(ii) · February/March 2025 · [1 mark]

Describe a test for chlorine gas.

Test: hold damp litmus paper in the gas.

Observation: the litmus paper is bleached (turns white).

Why this scores
The single mark needs both the test and the result. The examiner report notes some students confused chlorine with chloride ions and described a silver nitrate test.
0620/42/F/M/22 Q3(c)(iii) · February/March 2022 · [2 marks]

State the test for ammonia.

Test: hold damp red litmus paper in the gas.

Observation: the litmus paper turns blue.

Why this scores
One mark for damp red litmus, one for it turning blue. The examiner report warns that “pungent smell” is not a valid test.
0620/42/O/N/25 Q2(c)(ii) · October/November 2025 · [2 marks]

Source material: Iron can form two different ions, Fe2+ and Fe3+.

Describe a test for aqueous Fe2+ ions.

Test: add aqueous sodium hydroxide (or aqueous ammonia).

Observation: a green precipitate forms, which is insoluble in excess (and turns brown near the surface on standing).

Why this scores
One mark for the reagent, one for green precipitate. Red-brown is Fe3+, so the colour must be exact.
0620/42/M/J/22 Q2(c)(iv) · May/June 2022 · [3 marks]

Source material: Limewater is a saturated solution of calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2(aq).

Describe how you would test for the presence of calcium ions in a sample of limewater.

Test: add aqueous sodium hydroxide drop by drop, then in excess.

Observations: a white precipitate forms, which is insoluble in excess sodium hydroxide.

Why this scores
One mark for the reagent, one for white precipitate and one for insoluble in excess. The examiner report says some lost the third mark by not adding the alkali in excess; a flame test (orange-red) was also credited.
0620/43/O/N/24 Q4(e)(ii) · October/November 2024 · [5 marks]

Source material: Potassium hydrogen sulfate, KHSO4, dissolves in water to form solution X. Solution X contains K+, H+ and SO42– ions.

State the observations when the following tests are done.
• A flame test is carried out on X.
• Solid copper(II) carbonate is added to X.
• Aqueous barium nitrate acidified with dilute nitric acid is added to X.

Flame test: a lilac flame (K+).

Copper(II) carbonate: bubbles (fizzing) as CO2 is given off; the green solid dissolves; a blue solution forms (H+ reacts like an acid).

Acidified barium nitrate: a white precipitate (SO42−).

Why this scores
Five marks for lilac flame, bubbles, solid dissolves, blue solution and white precipitate. These are observations, so write what you see (“bubbles”), not “carbon dioxide is made”.
