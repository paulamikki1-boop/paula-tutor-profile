Paper 2 · Directed & Composition

Writing a Report

Present relevant information in an ordered way and make carefully considered suggestions. A mixture of fact and opinion, written for someone in authority.

Purpose & Audience

Reports are written for a reader in a position of authority — a principal, employer, committee chair. The tone is formal, impersonal, and objective. You are presenting findings and recommendations, not personal feelings.

Reports can serve two purposes: to offer suggestions based on experience, or to present alternatives so the reader can decide.

Three-Part Structure

1
Overview / Introduction Briefly introduce the topic and outline what the report will cover. The reader should understand the scope from this section alone.
2
Middle / Findings Develop and support relevant facts and opinions. Use subheadings to group related points. Include both positives and areas for improvement.
3
End / Recommendations Summarise suggestions, recommendations, or solutions. Include any new points for future consideration.

Key Features of the Form

Addressing
"To the Principal"
Title
"A report on…"
Subheadings
Yes — essential
Bullet Points
Sparingly, for key lists
Register
Formal & impersonal
Tone
Calm, polite, objective

Language & Tone

  • Impersonal and formal — no slang, no contractions (don't, can't), no humour or sarcasm.
  • Objective perspective — distance yourself from criticism. Report what was said indirectly: "It was suggested that…" rather than "I think…"
  • Calm and polite — avoid emotive language and personal appeals. Your reader needs to trust the evaluation is accurate.
  • Range of topic vocabulary — show subject knowledge through appropriate terminology.
  • Logical connectives — "in conclusion", "as a consequence", "furthermore", "however".
Examiner Tip
The report form is one of the most structured. Use clear subheadings to show organisation — this alone signals to the examiner that you understand the form. Group similar points under each heading.
Common Mistake
Writing a report as if it were a personal letter or diary entry. Reports should not be full of "I felt" and "I think". Distance yourself and report findings objectively.

Quick Checklist

  • Addressed to someone in authority
  • Clear title: "A report on…"
  • Subheadings used to organise sections
  • Formal, impersonal register throughout
  • Balance of fact and opinion
  • Logical linking words between sections
  • Concluding recommendations or suggestions
  • No slang, contractions, humour, or sarcasm
Paper 2 · Directed & Composition

Writing an Article

Inform and persuade readers about a topic, bringing together different perspectives. Articles can present a balanced argument or advocate for a particular viewpoint.

Purpose & Audience

Articles appear in newspapers, magazines, and school publications. You don't know your readers personally, so consider their likely interests and experience. The tone is semi-formal — more relaxed than a report, but still controlled.

An article can either persuade readers to agree with a viewpoint, or present both sides and let readers decide.

Three-Part Structure

1
Introduction Engage the reader's interest. Introduce the argument or main points. A rhetorical question or striking fact works well as a hook.
2
Middle / Development Develop points with evidence, examples, and contrasting viewpoints. Each paragraph should advance the argument or explore a new angle.
3
End / Conclusion Draw points together. Leave the reader with a clear impression of the argument or viewpoints to consider.

Key Features of the Form

Headline
Attention-grabbing title
Subheadings
Optional, for longer pieces
Register
Semi-formal
Viewpoints
Multiple, or one strong
Engagement
Rhetorical Qs, quotes
Expert Voices
Quotations from sources

Language & Tone

  • Semi-formal tone — imagine your article being read aloud. Match the tone to the topic (a film review can be wry; a news piece must be measured).
  • Varied sentence structures — mix short, punchy sentences with longer, complex ones to maintain reader interest.
  • Contrasting viewpoints — acknowledge both sides. Use "however", "on the other hand", "conversely" to signal shifts.
  • Expert quotations — "'You and your real friends may not always be able to meet in person,' explains one lifestyle expert." This adds authority.
  • Linking words — "in addition", "likewise", "similarly", "however", "nevertheless".
Example Opening
"In recent years, technology has taken over our daily lives. Many people claim that they simply couldn't imagine a life without the internet and social media. So are your online friendships doing you more harm than good?"
Examiner Tip
The headline is the first thing the examiner sees. Make it relevant and engaging — not just a restatement of the question. A good headline can set the tone for the entire piece.
Common Mistake
Using the same sentence structures and repetitive vocabulary throughout. Articles require varied grammatical structures and a wide range of vocabulary to keep the reader engaged.

Quick Checklist

  • Engaging headline relevant to the topic
  • Hook in the opening paragraph
  • Contrasting viewpoints explored fairly
  • Evidence: quotes, statistics, examples
  • Semi-formal, varied tone throughout
  • Linking words to connect paragraphs
  • Strong conclusion with a clear final impression
  • Optional subheadings if the article is long
Paper 2 · Directed & Composition

Writing a Speech

Inform, persuade, or motivate a live audience. Written to be spoken aloud — the words must sound natural and carry a speaker's energy on the page.

Purpose & Audience

Speeches are delivered to a specific audience (classmates, parents, a public meeting). The speaker has a relationship with the listeners and can address them directly. Consider whether your audience is likely to agree or disagree with you.

Speeches can inform, persuade, or call the audience to action — "join this club", "change this policy", "think differently about this issue".

Three-Part Structure

1
Opening — Grab Attention Pose a question, use a dramatic statement, or share a striking anecdote. Make the topic and your stance clear immediately.
2
Middle — Developed Argument Support your argument with real-life examples, anecdotes, evidence. Build paragraph by paragraph, each pushing the argument forward.
3
Conclusion — Powerful Close Use a tricolon (rule of three), a thought-provoking question, or a memorable image. Thank the audience. End with momentum, not a fade-out.

Key Features of the Form

Greeting
"Good morning…"
Direct Address
"you", "we", "all of us"
Register
Semi-formal to formal
Closing
"Thank you for listening"
Rhetorical Qs
Central to the form
Inclusive "we"
Unite speaker & audience

Language & Tone

  • Spoken register — imagine saying the words aloud. Vary pace: short, punchy sentences for emphasis; longer ones for development.
  • Inclusive language — "we", "all of us", "our" makes listeners feel you're on the same side.
  • Deal with objections — "A few people may still think, however…" Then defeat the objection.
  • Emotive word choice — choose words that appeal to the emotions of your listeners. Your tone must match your message.
  • Rhetorical devices — questions, tricolons, repetition, dramatic pauses (signalled by short sentences or ellipses).
Example Opening
"Good morning everyone. How did you all spend your weekend? Did you go hiking perhaps or climb the mountains? Guess what I did. Not hiking, not climbing, no — I spent the whole of yesterday afternoon learning vocab with my best friend Tony!"
Examiner Tip
The speech must sound like speech. If it reads like an essay with "Good morning" tacked on, you have not used the form. Think about dramatic pauses, audience reactions, and conversational asides.
Common Mistake
Writing an essay and adding "Good morning" at the start and "Thank you" at the end. A speech needs audience interaction throughout — rhetorical questions, direct address, reactions to imagined audience responses.

Quick Checklist

  • Appropriate greeting for the audience
  • Attention-grabbing opening (question, anecdote, drama)
  • Direct address: "you", "we", "our" throughout
  • Rhetorical questions woven through the speech
  • Anecdotes and real-life examples for evidence
  • Varied pace: short punchy + longer reflective
  • Objections acknowledged and defeated
  • Powerful conclusion with a call to action
  • Thanks to the audience at the close
Paper 1 Q4 · Paper 2 Directed

Writing a Letter

Inform, persuade, or communicate an experience or point of view to a specific reader. Formal letters follow strict conventions; informal letters allow a warmer register.

Purpose & Audience

Letters are written to one specific reader (though you may not know their name). The formality depends entirely on the relationship: a letter to an editor or hotel manager is formal; to a friend, informal.

Purposes include: complaint, persuasion, application, request, sharing experiences, or responding to an article.

Three-Part Structure

1
Opening Paragraph State the overall aim and make clear why you are writing. "I am writing in connection with…" or "I am writing to express my concern about…"
2
Middle / Development Explain and develop your points. Each paragraph addresses one aspect. Support with evidence, examples, details.
3
End / Closing Summarise main points, state the action you expect, or leave the reader with your key message. Close with an appropriate sign-off.

Letter Conventions

ElementFormal (unknown name)Formal (known name)Informal
OpeningDear Sir/MadamDear Mr/Mrs [Name]Dear [First name]
Sign-offYours faithfullyYours sincerelyBest wishes / Regards
RegisterHighly formalFormalConversational
ContractionsNoNoYes
Address/DateYes — both partiesYes — both partiesOptional

Language & Tone

  • Match formality to audience — formal for unknown/authority readers; informal for friends and family.
  • Remain calm and polite — even when complaining. Anger weakens your position.
  • Formal linking words — "furthermore", "however", "in conclusion", "additionally".
  • Time markers — "I am currently", "previously", "prior to that" to make timescales clear.
  • Varied sentence structures — avoid simple, repetitive patterns. Range of complex sentences signals command of language.
Examiner Tip
"Yours faithfully" goes with "Dear Sir/Madam" (you don't know the name). "Yours sincerely" goes with "Dear Mr/Mrs [Name]" (you know the name). Getting this wrong loses form marks.
Common Mistake
Mixing "Yours faithfully" with "Dear Mr Smith" or vice versa. Also: forgetting to acknowledge positives before criticising (in complaint letters), which makes the tone sound unreasonable.

Quick Checklist

  • Correct greeting matched to sign-off
  • Opening paragraph states the purpose clearly
  • Paragraphed development of points
  • Formal linking words and time markers
  • Calm, polite tone even in complaints
  • Balanced view: acknowledge positives if relevant
  • Clear statement of expected action at the end
  • Correct sign-off (faithfully / sincerely)

Persuasive Writing

Build a structured case using evidence, counter-argument, and rhetorical technique. State your position upfront and hold it consistently. The form for candidates who think analytically.

The Five-Section Shape

Within 350–450 words, the persuasive essay has a tight, predictable structure:

Section~WordsPurpose
Introduction50State position, signal angle
Body paragraph 180First main argument (PEEL)
Body paragraph 280Second main argument (PEEL)
Body paragraph 380Third main argument (PEEL)
Counter-argument80Acknowledge + defeat opposing view
Conclusion50Reframe — most memorable version of your case

DAFOREST — Eight Persuasive Techniques

A mnemonic for the techniques to deploy in service of your argument:

Direct address Alliteration Facts Opinion Rhetorical Qs Emotive language Statistics Triplets (rule of 3)

Aim for 4–5 different techniques per essay. Used in service of argument — never as decoration alone.

Three Types of Evidence

Mix all three across the essay. Pure assertion ("I think uniforms are bad") has no evidence and scores poorly.

  • Statistical / data evidence — "Tallinn's free-transport pilot reduced city-centre car traffic by 9% in its first year." Persuades the analytical mind.
  • Examples / case studies — "Hasselt in Belgium ran the world's first universal free-bus scheme for almost two decades." Concrete and memorable.
  • Anecdote / human story — "Take Priya, a sixteen-year-old who used her gap year to volunteer." Persuades the emotional mind.
Inventing Evidence
Cambridge mark schemes accept plausible invented evidence. You can invent named studies, named places, and named individuals. The standard is plausibility — would a casual reader believe this exists?

Counter-Argument — The Top-Band Move

A persuasive essay that ignores the opposing view feels naive. One that acknowledges and defeats it signals analytical maturity. This is the clearest single discriminator for the top band.

The Four Steps

1
ConcedeBriefly admit the strength of the opposing view: "Critics will argue that…" / "The concern is fair."
2
State fairlyDon't strawman. Present the strongest version of the opposing case (steelman it).
3
Defeat with evidenceShow why your position still wins, using data, examples, or anecdote.
4
ReaffirmReturn to your main thread. The reader must know which side you are on.

Rhetorical Tools

Devices serve the argument. They don't decorate it. Use 1–2 instances of each, and 4–5 different devices across the essay.

  • Rule of three — "Less pollution, lower healthcare costs, fewer cars on the road."
  • Antithesis — "The question is not whether we can afford to act — it is whether we can afford NOT to."
  • Concession — "Critics are right that initial costs are real." Signals fairness before defeating it.
  • Rhetorical question — "Are we content to keep paying the higher cost of inaction?" Frames the answer in your favour.
  • Aphoristic claim — "Tradition is not nothing — but it is not, in this instance, enough." Short, quotable, for conclusions.
Common Mistakes
No counter-argument — caps you at mid-band. Argument-by-assertion — "Uniforms are bad because they make students unhappy" with no evidence. Stacking rhetorical questions — three in a row reads as decoration. Sitting on the fence — persuasion is not a balanced report.
Model Counter-Argument Paragraph
"Critics will argue that abolishing uniforms invites distraction, peer pressure, and the same brand competition uniforms were meant to silence. The concern is fair. Yet the same Australian schools that ended the requirement found that a simple dress code produced no rise in distraction and substantial gains in student satisfaction. Replacing uniforms with a thoughtful dress code is not, as critics fear, the absence of policy — it is a more proportionate version of one."

Quick Checklist

  • State position upfront — no fence-sitting
  • Five-section shape: intro, 3 body, counter-arg, conclusion
  • Three evidence types mixed: data, examples, anecdote
  • Counter-argument: concede, state, defeat, reaffirm
  • 4–5 different rhetorical devices across the essay
  • Reframe in conclusion — don't just restate
  • Confident but not aggressive tone
  • 350–450 words

Writing an Interview

Two voices, one structure. Speaker labels, varied question types, a distinctive interviewee voice, and source texts woven naturally into answers. The form for candidates who want to dramatise an argument.

Interview Anatomy

1
Framing Paragraph (~50 words)Sets up who, why, when. "Maya Chen, a Year 11 student, spent eighteen months campaigning for… She spoke to us this week."
2
5–6 Q&A PairsEach pair on a new line with speaker labels. Questions shorter than answers. Variable answer length — mix short and long.
3
Brief CloseEnd on the final answer. No formal sign-off (or one polite line max). The last answer should be the most memorable.

Key Features of the Form

Speaker Labels
Every turn, new line
Label Format
"Interviewer:" / "[Name]:"
Framing Intro
2–3 sentences before Q&A
Word Count
250–300 words typical
Answer Length
Varied: short & long
Source Texts
In interviewee's answers

The Five Question Types

Mix all five across the interview. Each does a different job.

  • Opening question — establish topic. "Maya, you've been doing this for over a year. What got you started?"
  • Open exploratory — invite reflection. "What did you find when you spoke to other students?" Uses "what", "how", "why".
  • Specific factual — get details. "How many incidents have been reported?" Short, concrete answer.
  • Challenging question — raise counter-argument. "Some councillors argued the cost was prohibitive. How did you respond?" This is where the counter-argument lives.
  • Closing question — forward-looking. "What needs to happen next?" Most memorable lines come here.

Building a Distinctive Interviewee Voice

The interviewee should feel like a real person, not a position-paper with a name.

  • Distinctive vocabulary — match to character. A scientist uses precise terms; a teenager uses youthful idiom; an activist uses direct, urgent language.
  • Pacing variation — some answers short and decisive, others longer and reflective. The same person gives both kinds.
  • Self-correction / hesitation — "Well…", "Actually, let me put that differently", "Honestly?" signals authentic speech.
  • Favourite framings — a character who twice uses "the way I see it" has a more memorable voice than one who never repeats.

Weaving Source Texts into Answers

Reading marks (15/40) still apply. Source ideas live in the interviewee's answers, not labelled academically.

DoDon't
"The first article I read described a survey showing that…""Text A says…"
"A friend sent me an article that put numbers on what I'd been feeling…""According to the second source…"
Interviewee uses source data as their own knowledgeQuoting source passages verbatim at length
Examiner Tip
Top-band interviews are praised for "varied question types, authentic voice, and skilful integration of source material". The most-flagged failure is uniform questions and uniform answer length, flattening the rhythm of a real conversation.
Common Mistakes
Repeating Text C instead of developing it — lifting sentences from the source. Every question the same type ("Tell me about…" repeated). No distinctive voice — the interviewee sounds like a generic essay. No challenging question — misses the counter-argument.

Quick Checklist

  • Framing intro: who, why, when
  • Speaker labels on every turn, new line per speaker
  • Five question types: opening, open, specific, challenging, closing
  • Variable answer length — short and long mixed
  • Distinctive interviewee voice: vocabulary, pacing, framings
  • Source ideas in interviewee's answers, in their own voice
  • Counter-argument in the challenging question
  • Memorable final answer

Writing a Journal Entry

A personal, reflective piece written in first person with intimate tone. Record thoughts, feelings, and reflections on an event or experience — honest, private, and introspective.

Purpose & Audience

A journal entry is written for yourself — there is no external audience. This makes it the most personal form in the exam. The tone is intimate, honest, and reflective. You can express doubt, confusion, mixed feelings, and private thoughts that you would not share in a letter or article.

Examiners reward emotional depth and genuine reflection — not just describing what happened, but exploring what it meant to you and what you learned.

Structure

1
Date Write a date at the top — this is essential journal form. E.g. "Monday, 15 June 2026" or "15th June."
2
Opening — Set the Mood Start with a feeling, question, or striking moment: "I still can’t believe what happened today." Hook the reader into your emotional state immediately.
3
Event Description Describe what happened with key details — chronologically or thematically. Include sensory detail to bring the experience alive.
4
Feelings & Reactions Express emotional responses honestly and specifically. "I felt a mix of relief and guilt" beats "I was sad." Name the specific emotions.
5
Reflection & Closing What did it mean? What did you learn? A final thought or forward look: "I realise now that…" or "Tomorrow I’ll try to…"

Key Features of the Form

Date
At the top — essential
Salutation
"Dear Diary" optional, not required
Person
First person: "I", "my", "me"
Register
Informal, personal, reflective
Tone
Intimate, honest, introspective
Contractions
Yes — "can’t", "I’ve", "didn’t"

Language & Tone

  • First person throughout — "I", "my", "me". This is the one form where every sentence should feel personal.
  • Informal register is appropriate — contractions, conversational phrasing, sentence fragments for effect ("What a day.").
  • Specific emotion words — don’t default to "happy" or "sad". Use precise terms: anxious, relieved, humiliated, elated, conflicted, grateful.
  • Sensory and descriptive detail — bring the experience alive through what you saw, heard, felt. This is not a report — it’s a lived experience.
  • Internal questions — journals naturally ask "Why did I do that?" or "What was I thinking?" This creates authentic voice.
  • Reflection, not just narration — describe what happened, then explore what it meant. The reflection is what separates top-band from mid-band.
Examiner Tip
The biggest distinction between a journal and a narrative is reflection. A narrative tells the story; a journal tells the story and thinks about it afterwards. If the question asks you to "reflect," make sure at least one-third of your response is looking back on what the event meant, not just describing it.
Voice Tip
A journal entry should read like someone thinking on paper. Use natural pauses: "I suppose, looking back, I should have seen it coming." Internal monologue, self-correction ("Actually, that’s not quite right — what I really felt was…"), and rhetorical self-questioning all create an authentic journal voice.
Common Mistake
Describing without feeling. Students write a blow-by-blow account of the event without expressing emotions or reflecting on meaning. If the question says "record your thoughts and feelings," the feelings must be there — not implied, but stated.
Common Mistake
Using third person or formal register. "It was observed that the event was enjoyable" is report register, not journal. Write: "I couldn’t stop smiling — I haven’t felt that alive in months."

Quick Checklist

  • Date at the top of the entry
  • First person ("I") throughout
  • Informal, personal, reflective register
  • Opening with emotional impact, not "Today I went to…"
  • Key events described with sensory detail
  • Specific emotion words, not generic "happy/sad"
  • Reflection on what the experience meant or taught you
  • Internal questions and honest self-examination
  • Forward-looking closing thought

Writing a Newspaper Report

A factual, impersonal report for a general readership. Lead with the most important information, attribute opinions to named sources, and maintain an objective third-person register throughout.

Purpose & Audience

A newspaper report is written for a general readership — think BBC News, The Guardian’s news pages, a local paper. The register is impersonal, factual, and objective. You are a reporter presenting facts, not a student sharing opinions.

This is not the same as a magazine article. News reports use third person throughout and never editorialise. Opinions appear only inside attributed quotations from named sources.

The Inverted Pyramid

News reports use the inverted pyramid: most important information at the top, less essential detail at the bottom. The reader can stop at any paragraph and still have the key facts.

1
Headline Short, factual, specific. Active voice. No full stop. E.g. "Pupils haul half a tonne of waste from Mill river in town-first clean-up"
2
Lead Paragraph — The 5 Ws WHO + WHAT + WHEN + WHERE (+ WHY) in 1–2 sentences. The reader gets the whole story from this paragraph alone.
3
Key Facts & Attributed Quotes Background detail, additional facts, and direct quotations from named sources. Each paragraph is less essential than the one before.
4
Context & Future Steps Broader implications, related history, what happens next. Optional but shows maturity.

Key Features of the Form

Headline
Short, factual, active voice
Standfirst
Optional sub-headline summary
Byline
"By [reporter name]" — optional
Person
Third person only — no "I" or "we"
Register
Impersonal, factual, objective
Quotations
Named sources, varied attribution verbs

News Report vs Magazine Article

Cambridge sometimes asks for a news report, sometimes a magazine article. The two look similar but differ in crucial ways:

FeatureNews ReportMagazine Article
VoiceThird-person impersonalFirst-person allowed
ToneNeutral, factualOpinion-led, persuasive
StructureInverted pyramidHook → argument → close
QuotationsNamed direct quotesAnecdotes, paraphrased ideas
EditorialNone — facts onlyYes — author has a position

Direct Quotation & Attribution

News reports carry their argument through named sources. The reporter doesn’t editorialise; the sources do.

  • Format: Quote in double quotation marks, attributed by name and role.
  • Example: “The decision will reshape morning routines for thousands of families,” said Councillor Hayes, the borough’s lead member for transport.
  • Vary attribution verbs: said, told reporters, commented, noted, argued, added, responded, acknowledged.

Inventing Plausible Sources

Cambridge mark schemes accept and reward plausible invented sources. You can invent:

  • A headteacher with a name and school
  • A council officer with a name and role
  • A student with a name and year group
  • A parent, local resident, or expert
Examiner Tip
The inverted pyramid is the single most form-distinguishing feature of a news report. Get the 5 Ws into your lead paragraph and structure detail in descending importance — this alone earns the form mark. Examiner reports praise candidates who “attribute opinions to plausible sources rather than editorialising.”
How to Spot the Form
"Newspaper article" / "news report" / "local newspaper" → news register (impersonal, factual, inverted pyramid).
"Magazine article" / "school magazine" / "opinion piece" → magazine register (first-person allowed, opinion-led, hook-driven).
Common Mistake
Using first-person opinion in a news report. Writing “I was there” or “I think this is unfair” caps the W4 (purpose/audience) mark immediately. The reporter is invisible. Any opinion must be inside an attributed quotation: “‘This decision is unfair,’ said local resident Mrs Patel.”
Common Mistake
Burying the main event. Don’t build up to the news like an essay or anecdote. The lead paragraph must contain the key facts. If the reader has to wait until paragraph three for the news, you’ve lost the inverted pyramid.
Common Mistake
No direct quotations. A news report without attributed quotes reads as a summary, not journalism. Include at least two named sources with varied attribution verbs.

Quick Checklist

  • Factual headline — short, active, specific
  • Lead paragraph: WHO + WHAT + WHEN + WHERE
  • Inverted pyramid — most important first
  • Third person throughout — no "I" or "we"
  • No editorialising — opinions in attributed quotes only
  • At least two named sources with roles
  • Varied attribution verbs: said, told, added, noted
  • Source material integrated naturally, not copied
  • Forward-looking close (optional but strong)
Paper 2 · Directed Writing · 40/40

Model Answer: Formal Letter

A top-band directed writing response demonstrating formal letter conventions, persuasive argument, and effective use of source material.

Task
Your local council plans to close the only public library in your area. Using ideas from the passage about the value of libraries, write a letter to the council arguing against the closure.

Opening

Dear Councillor Reeves,

I am writing to express my deep concern regarding the proposed closure of Elm Road Library, which has served this community for over forty years. While I understand the council faces significant budgetary pressures, I believe that closing the library would cause far greater harm than the savings it would generate.

Why this scores: Correct formal letter conventions: named salutation, clear purpose stated immediately, concession to the opposing view ("I understand the council faces..."). The tone is measured and respectful — not aggressive. This is the register examiners reward.

Paragraph 2 — Community Impact

As the passage makes clear, libraries are not simply repositories of books — they are essential community spaces. For many elderly residents in this area, the library is the only place they visit regularly outside their own homes. Removing it would deepen the isolation that already affects a significant proportion of our ageing population. Furthermore, the library's free internet access is relied upon by families who cannot afford home broadband, and by job seekers who depend on it to search and apply for employment.

Why this scores: Two passage ideas (community space, internet access) are adapted to the local context — the student does not simply repeat the passage but applies it. This is the key to top-band directed writing: use the source material as a springboard, not a script.

Paragraph 3 — Educational Role

The passage also highlights the educational role of libraries, and this is particularly relevant here. Elm Road Library runs a weekly reading group for under-fives and a homework club for secondary students. These are not luxuries; they directly support the attainment of children in an area where educational outcomes already fall below the national average. To remove them would be to widen an inequality that the council claims, in its own strategic plan, to be committed to narrowing.

Why this scores: Passage idea (educational role) + local detail (reading group, homework club) + persuasive turn (using the council's own language against it). The final sentence is rhetorically powerful without being rude. Examiners note this as "sophisticated engagement with audience."

Closing

I would urge the council to explore alternatives before making this decision irreversible: reduced opening hours, shared premises with the community centre, or a volunteer staffing model. Other councils have successfully implemented such measures, and I would welcome the opportunity to discuss them further.

Yours sincerely,
Alex Harrington

Why this scores: Constructive close: offers solutions rather than just complaining. Correct sign-off ("Yours sincerely" after a named salutation). The tone throughout has been formal, persuasive, and audience-aware — all three are rewarded in the mark scheme.
Key Takeaways
Concede before arguing — acknowledging the council's budget pressure makes the writer sound reasonable, not hostile. Extend the source — passage ideas are applied to local detail, not just repeated. Close with solutions — the letter becomes constructive, not just a complaint.
Paper 2 · Directed Writing · 40/40

Model Answer: Magazine Article

A top-band directed writing response demonstrating article conventions, critical engagement with source material, and a distinctive authorial voice.

Task
A school magazine is running a feature on whether technology helps or hinders learning. Using ideas from the passage, write an article giving your views.

Opening

THE SCREEN GENERATION: SMARTER OR JUST FASTER?

Walk into any classroom in 2025 and the first thing you will notice is the glow. Thirty screens, thirty bent heads, thirty pairs of thumbs moving in patterns that would have baffled a typist twenty years ago. Technology has not just arrived in education — it has taken up permanent residence, rearranged the furniture, and changed the locks.

Why this scores: Article conventions: headline in capitals, engaging opening image, direct address ("you will notice"). The extended metaphor ("taken up permanent residence, rearranged the furniture") is witty and original. This signals to the examiner that the student understands the form.

Paragraph 2 — The Case For

The passage argues, persuasively, that digital tools have democratised access to knowledge. A student in rural Kenya can now watch the same lecture as a student at Cambridge, and this is genuinely revolutionary. Model Answer: Narrative Writing

"The Envelope" — a top-band narrative composition demonstrating restraint, precise physical detail, structural repetition, and an emotionally resonant ending.

Task
Write a narrative composition with the title: The Envelope.

Paragraph 1 — The Wait

The envelope had been sitting on the hall table for three days before I opened it. It was not that I did not know what was inside — I knew exactly what was inside. It was a thin, cream-coloured envelope with a typed address and no return label, and the postmark said Cambridge, and Cambridge only ever sent one kind of letter to people like me.

Why this scores: In medias res opening centred on a single object. Tension built through delay ("three days before I opened it") and the narrator's certainty about the contents. "People like me" invites the reader to fill in the gap — inference rather than exposition. The examiner rewards this restraint.

Paragraph 2 — The Mother

My mother had noticed it first. She had come into the hallway with a basket of washing and stopped so suddenly that a pillowcase slid off the top and landed on the floor, and neither of us picked it up. She looked at me. I looked at the envelope. The pillowcase lay between us like a small white surrender.

Why this scores: Character revealed through action, not description. The dropped pillowcase is a tiny, specific physical detail that carries enormous emotional weight. "Small white surrender" is a controlled metaphor — not overwritten. The scene is built from three images: the washing, the eye contact, the pillowcase.

Paragraph 3 — Three Evenings

"You should open it," she said, on the first evening.

"I know," I said.

"You should open it," she said, on the second evening.

"I know," I said.

On the third evening, she did not say anything. She put a cup of tea next to the envelope and went upstairs, and I sat at the hall table in the half-light and thought about all the versions of my life that were folded up inside that cream-coloured paper.

Why this scores: Repetition used structurally — the two-line exchange on days one and two, then silence on day three. This is show-don't-tell: the mother's silence speaks louder than her words. "All the versions of my life" is the thematic heart of the piece. The examiner notes the sentence variety: short dialogue, then a long reflective sentence.

Paragraph 4 — The Opening

I opened it standing up, which I had not planned. I had imagined sitting down, taking a breath, sliding a finger under the seal carefully, the way people do in films. Instead I just picked it up and tore it open, quickly, the way you pull off a plaster, and the sound of the paper tearing was the loudest thing in the house.

Why this scores: The climax is handled through pace and physical detail. The contrast between the imagined version (slow, cinematic) and the real version (fast, messy) is psychologically true. "The loudest thing in the house" is a strong sensory detail — it tells us how silent the house has been, how much has been held back.

Paragraph 5 — The Answer

It said yes.

I read it twice, because the first time I read it I did not believe it, and the second time I read it I believed it but did not know what to do with the believing. I put the letter back on the table and went to the bottom of the stairs and called up to my mother, and my voice, when it came, was steadier than I had expected.

"Mum," I said. "It said yes."

Why this scores: Two-word paragraph for the revelation — maximum impact through minimum language. The emotional processing is described honestly: "did not know what to do with the believing" is a phrase that feels earned rather than manufactured. The final line mirrors the mother's earlier dialogue, bringing the relationship full circle. The examiner reads this as a piece with "a controlled, resonant ending that avoids sentimentality."
Key Takeaways
Small scale, big impact — one envelope, one hallway, three days. No car chases. Physical detail carries emotion — the pillowcase, the sound of tearing paper, the cup of tea. Structural repetition — the repeated dialogue creates rhythm and makes the silence on day three devastating. Short sentences at the climax — "It said yes." is three words that carry the entire piece.
What Weak Narratives Do Instead
Cover too much time — years crammed into 500 words. Tell rather than show — "I was so happy" instead of the physical detail. Dream endings — "and then I woke up." Melodrama — explosions, deaths, twists that feel unearned. The best narratives are small, precise, and emotionally honest.

Why this scores: The remaining source ideas (one-in-four stat, infrastructure conclusion) appear in the lower part of the pyramid — important but less essential than the lead. 5 Ws in the lead — always. Every fact the reader needs should be in the first paragraph. Two named sources minimum — with different roles and varied attribution verbs. Key Takeaways
Speech register from first to last word — greeting, direct address (“you”, “we”, “this room”), rhetorical questions, audience interaction (“let us not forget”), call to action, and “Thank you” are all markers examiners look for under W4. Concede before you argue — acknowledging the genuine benefits of digital payment makes the critique of the proposal far more persuasive. Synthesise, don’t alternate — top-band answers weave ideas from both texts into a single line of reasoning rather than dealing with Text A then Text B separately. Cover all three contexts — the question names individuals, businesses and the wider community; addressing all three shows careful reading of the task (R5). Evaluate attitudes, not just facts — the mark scheme rewards candidates who assess what the writers think and feel, not just what they report.

Techniques Glossary

Key terms, rhetorical devices, and definitions that Cambridge mark schemes credit — with examples for each.

Rhetorical & Persuasive Devices

DeviceDefinitionExample
Rhetorical questionA question that expects no answer, framing the argument"Are we content to keep paying the higher cost of inaction?"
Tricolon (Rule of three)Three items grouped for rhythm and completeness"Cleaner lungs, quieter streets, a town worth walking through."
Direct addressSpeaking to the reader as "you" / "we" to close distance"You deserve better than this. We all do."
AntithesisContrasting two ideas to force the reader into your frame"The question is not whether we can afford to act — it is whether we can afford not to."
Emotive languageWords chosen to provoke an emotional response"choking traffic" instead of "busy roads"
AlliterationRepeated initial consonant sounds for emphasis"save our seas"
AnecdoteA brief personal story to make abstract arguments human"Take Priya, a sixteen-year-old who volunteered…"
ConcessionBriefly admitting the opposing view's strength before defeating it"Critics are right that initial costs are real."
AphorismA short, quotable sentence summarising a position"Tradition is not nothing — but it is not, in this instance, enough."
Inclusive pronounsUsing "we", "our", "us" to unite writer and reader"We owe it to ourselves to demand better."

Examiner Keywords

TermWhat It Means
Stance (thesis)Your single, clear position on the question, stated early and held consistently.
Ethos / Pathos / LogosThree classical appeals: credibility, emotion, reason. Strong writing uses all three.
Counter-argument & rebuttalAn opposing view stated fairly, conceded where true, then defeated. The top-band discriminator.
Call to actionA direct demand on the reader at the close — to vote, sign, write, change their mind.
PEELPoint, Evidence, Explain, Link — paragraph structure for argumentative writing.
Develop (an idea)Going beyond what the text states to its implications, feelings, or consequences. Not repetition.
Speaker labelIdentifier marking each turn in an interview: "Interviewer:" / "Maya:". On every turn, new line.
Probing questionA question aimed at a specific idea that invites a developed answer, not yes/no.
