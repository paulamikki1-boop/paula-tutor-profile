Exam Technique: How to Score Full Marks

Knowing the economics is only half the job. This section shows you how the two papers are built (checked against the official 2027–2029 syllabus and specimen papers), what each of the nine command words is really asking for, and how to turn what you know into full-mark answers under time pressure.

The papers you will sit (2027–2029)

Paper 1
40 multiple-choice questions · 40 marks · 1 hour · 30% of the grade
Paper 2
Structured questions · 80 marks · 2 hours · 70% of the grade
Paper 2 Section A
Question 1, compulsory: unseen data about a real economy, parts (a) to (f) · 20 marks
Paper 2 Section B
Answer three from Questions 2–5 · 20 marks each · 60 marks
Each Section B question
(a) 2 · (b) 4 · (c) 6 · (d) 8 marks
Tiers and calculators
No tiers: everyone sits both papers (grades A* to G). Calculators are allowed in both.
  • Paper 1 tests the whole syllabus. Some questions need a quick calculation or ask you to read a diagram, so practise both. There is no penalty for a wrong answer: never leave one blank.
  • Section A gives you information about a real economic situation: text and data, often with tables or a diagram. You must use that information in your answers. In the specimen paper the six parts carry 2, 2, 2, 4, 4 and 6 marks: a calculation, facts picked out of the data, a short explanation, a diagram to draw, an analysis of a relationship in a table, and a final “Discuss whether or not…”.
  • Section B questions each open with a short paragraph of context. You may use it, or other examples you have studied. The four parts rise in difficulty: typically define or identify, explain, analyse, discuss. Some questions ask you to draw a diagram.
  • The marks in square brackets tell you how much to write. A 2-mark answer is two precise lines; an 8-mark answer is roughly a side of writing.

What each command word needs

The 2027–2029 syllabus lists exactly nine command words. Learn what each one is asking you to do.

A question can also simply tell you to draw a diagram, as Section A part (d) of the specimen paper does. Then all the marks are for the diagram itself (labelled axes, labelled curves, the shift, the old and new equilibrium) and none for written explanation.

Examiner Tip
Match the depth to the command word. Writing a full page for a 2-mark “Define” wastes minutes you need for the 8-mark “Discuss”, and a one-sided list of points for a “Discuss” question can never reach the top level however many points it has.

How the marking works

Most of Paper 2 is point-marked: each correct, relevant point or step of development earns one mark. That covers the whole of Section A and parts (a), (b) and (c) of every Section B question. Only the Discuss [8] in part (d) of Section B is marked in levels: the examiner reads the whole answer, decides which level description fits best, and then chooses a mark within that level.

QuestionHow the marks are earned
Explain two… [4]For each of the two points: 1 mark for identifying it and 1 mark for explaining it. A third point earns nothing extra.
Analyse… [6]One mark for each correct link in your chain. If the question says “two reasons” or “two policy measures”, up to 2 marks are for naming them and the rest for the analysis, so develop both. Repeating the same link in the second chain does not score twice.
Analyse the relationship… [4] (Section A)State the expected relationship, support it with figures from the table, explain why it happens, then point out an exception with its evidence.
Discuss whether or not… [6] (Section A)Point-marked, with at most 4 marks for either side. A one-sided answer therefore cannot score more than 4 out of 6.

Levels for the Discuss [8] in Section B part (d)

LevelMarksWhat the answer looks like
Level 36–8A reasoned discussion of both sides, each developed with clear, logical analysis (one side may go deeper than the other). Accurate economic terms. The best answers also point out what is uncertain or what the outcome depends on.
Level 23–5Reasoned and uses clear analysis, but lacks some depth or is developed on one side only.
Level 11–2Relevant definitions or statements that are not developed.
  • Depth beats breadth. Two fully developed chains score more than six undeveloped points.
  • Evaluation means weighing up: “this depends on…”, “in the short run… but in the long run…”, “this is more likely if…”.
  • A justified conclusion answers the question directly (yes, no, or it depends) and gives the key condition. It should add something new, not repeat the essay.
  • Simply reversing an argument (“revenue may rise… or revenue may fall”) earns no extra credit. The other side needs a different reason.

The chain-of-reasoning technique

Almost every mark above the 2-mark questions is earned by linking ideas. Use connectives such as which means that, this leads to, as a result and therefore to force yourself to write each step.

1
CauseState the change clearly in economic terms, e.g. “The central bank raises the rate of interest.”
2
First effectWhat happens directly: “Borrowing becomes more expensive and saving more rewarding.”
3
Second effectHow behaviour changes: “Households spend less on goods bought on credit and firms postpone investment, so total demand falls.”
4
OutcomeLink back to the question: “With less spending, firms face less pressure to raise prices, so the inflation rate falls.”
5
Evaluate (Discuss only)Add a condition or counter-effect: “However, this may also reduce output and raise unemployment, and the effect takes time.”
Model answer

Explain why a fall in income tax may increase consumer spending. [4]

A cut in income tax increases households’ disposable income (1), which means they have more money left after tax to spend on goods and services, so consumption rises (1). It may also raise the reward from working, encouraging some people to work more hours or to join the labour force (1); their higher earned income then allows them to spend more (1).

Using the case data in Section A

  • Read the questions first, then the case, so you know what to look for. Underline figures and key phrases as you go.
  • Use the data in every part. The paper says: “In your answers to Question 1, you must use the information provided.” Name the figure and the year: “Unemployment rose from 4% in 2024 to 7% in 2026.” For an “Identify” part, only items taken from the information score.
  • Describe trends accurately: rose, fell, fluctuated, rose at a slower rate. Remember that a falling inflation rate still means prices are rising, just more slowly.
  • Calculations: write the formula, substitute, and give units. Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. A share of a total = total × percentage ÷ 100 (for example, a primary sector that is 15% of a GDP of $200bn produces $200bn × 15 ÷ 100 = $30bn).
  • Use the context in explanations and discussions: if the country relies on tourism or oil exports, say so and apply it. Context-free textbook answers lose marks.
  • Do not just copy the text. Take the facts and add the economics (the “why”).

Drawing and labelling diagrams

If a question says “draw a … diagram” or “using a … diagram”, the diagram is part of the mark scheme. An unlabelled or wrongly drawn diagram cannot score. Draw it in pencil, large (at least a third of a page), with a ruler.

PPC checklist

  • Label both axes with two named goods or types of good (e.g. Capital goods, Consumer goods) and mark the origin 0.
  • Draw the curve concave (bowed out) to the origin, touching both axes. Label it PPC, and a new curve PPC1 (or number them PPC1 and PPC2; either is fine as long as old and new are clear).
  • Show a movement along the curve for reallocation (opportunity cost), a point inside for unemployment, and a shift for a change in the quantity or quality of resources.
  • Use arrows to show the direction of a shift and refer to the diagram in your writing: “as shown by the shift from PPC to PPC1…”.

Demand and supply checklist

  • Axes: Price (vertical) and Quantity (horizontal). Demand slopes down, supply slopes up.
  • Label every curve (D, S, D1 or S1) and mark the old and new equilibrium price and quantity (P, Q, P1, Q1) with dashed lines to the axes. A typical 4-mark diagram earns one mark each for: labelled axes, the original labelled curves, the correct shift, and both equilibriums shown.
  • Only shift the curve that the question’s change affects: a change in a non-price determinant of demand shifts D; a change in costs of production shifts S. A change in the product’s own price causes a movement along, not a shift.
  • In the text, explain the diagram: what shifted, why, the direction, and the effect on price and quantity.

Timing

Paper 1: 60 minutes for 40 questions is 1½ minutes per question. Answer the quick recall questions first, circle the calculation and diagram questions that need longer, and come back to them. Aim to finish with five minutes left to check that every question has an answer.

Paper 2: you have 120 minutes for 80 marks, so allow roughly 1½ minutes per mark. Think of it as about a minute of writing per mark plus time to read, plan and check.

  • If you run over on one question, move on. The first marks on a new question are always easier to earn than the last mark on an old one.
  • Jot a 30-second plan for each (d): two points for, two against, one “it depends on”.

Common mistakes

Common Mistake
Writing one-sided answers to “Discuss” questions. “Discuss whether or not…” always needs both “whether” and “whether not”. Finish with a short judgement to show you have weighed the two sides up.
Common Mistake
Stating without explaining: “Higher interest rates reduce inflation.” is only an assertion. The marks are in the steps in between (borrowing costs → spending → demand → prices).
Common Mistake
Confusing a shift of a curve with a movement along it, e.g. saying a rise in price “shifts demand to the left”. A price change causes a contraction or extension of demand.
  • Vague terms: “the economy gets better” instead of naming the aim (economic growth, lower unemployment, stable prices, balance of payments stability, redistribution of income, environmental sustainability).
  • Mixing up similar terms: inflation and prices, income and wealth, exports and imports, the rate of change and the level.
  • Ignoring the context word in the question: a question about “a developing economy” or “a firm” wants answers applied to that case.
  • Forgetting units in calculations or copying the case data without interpreting it.
  • Leaving a part blank. A sensible attempt at a definition or one developed point can still earn marks.
Examiner Tip
Before writing a Discuss conclusion, ask: “What single factor would change my answer?” That factor (the size of the change, the time period, the elasticity, the state of the economy, how the money is spent) is your “it depends on”.

The Basic Economic Problem

These ideas are the vocabulary for the whole course and turn up in almost every Paper 1 and Paper 2 question.

1.1 The nature of the basic economic problem

Finite resources and infinite wants

The basic economic problem is that resources are finite (limited) while wants are infinite (unlimited). Because of this, there is scarcity, and choices have to be made.

  • Scarcity means there are not enough resources to produce everything that people want. It applies to rich and poor countries alike: wants always run ahead of what the available resources can produce.
  • Scarcity forces choice, and every choice means going without something else (see 1.3 Opportunity cost).
  • Scarcity is also an environmental issue: fresh water, fertile land, fish stocks and a stable climate are finite. Using them up now reduces what is available to future generations.

The basic economic problem in four contexts

Everyone in an economy faces the same problem on a different scale. Be ready to give an example for each group.

WhoWhat is limitedExample of the choice they face
ConsumersIncome (and time)A student with $50 wants new headphones, a concert ticket and a meal out, but cannot afford all three
WorkersTime, energy and skillsA nurse has only so many hours in a week: extra paid shifts mean less time for rest, family or study
Producers / firmsLand, workers, machines and financeA bakery with one oven must decide how much bread and how many cakes to bake each morning
GovernmentsTax revenueA government wants better hospitals, schools and roads, but its budget will not cover every project

Resource allocation: the three basic economic questions

Resource allocation is the way an economy’s scarce resources are shared out between different uses. Because resources are scarce, every economy — whether market, planned or mixed — has to answer three questions:

1
What to produce?Which goods and services, and in what quantities? E.g. more hospitals or more roads; solar panels or coal power stations.
2
How to produce?Which combination of factors of production and which methods? E.g. labour-intensive hand-picking or capital-intensive machinery; fossil-fuel energy or renewables.
3
Who to produce for?Who gets the output? (Older papers say “for whom to produce”.) In a market system it goes to those with the income to pay; a government may allocate some goods according to need (e.g. free healthcare).

Economic goods and free goods

Economic goodFree good
Scarce?Yes: wants are greater than the amount availableNo: there is enough for everyone to have as much as they want
Resources needed to supply it?Yes: it takes land, labour, capital and enterpriseNo: nature provides it without any factors of production being used
Opportunity cost?Yes: the resources could have made something elseZero: nothing is given up
ExamplesA laptop, a bus journey, a doctor’s time, bottled waterAir to breathe, sunlight, sea water in the ocean
Common Mistake
A free good is not the same as a good with a price of zero. State schooling or a “free” phone app costs the user nothing, but teachers, buildings and programmers were needed to provide it, so it is an economic good. The same thing can also change category: sea water is a free good, but once it is cleaned and piped to homes it is an economic good.
Examiner Tip
If a question asks you to define the basic economic problem, you need both halves: limited resources AND unlimited wants, leading to scarcity and the need to make choices. Saying only “resources are scarce” gives half the definition, so it is unlikely to earn both marks.

1.2 Factors of production

Factors of production and their rewards

Factors of production are the resources used to produce goods and services. Each has a reward (the income its owner receives).

FactorDefinitionExamplesReward
LandAll natural resources provided by natureFarmland, forests, oil, minerals, rivers, fish in the seaRent
LabourHuman effort, physical and mental, used in productionNurses, factory workers, software engineersWages (and salaries)
CapitalHuman-made goods used to produce other goods and servicesMachinery, tools, factories, delivery vans, computersInterest
EnterpriseThe organising of the other factors and the bearing of riskAn entrepreneur who starts an electric-bike rental businessProfit
Land → Rent Labour → Wages Capital → Interest Enterprise → Profit — “Really Wise Investors Profit”
Common Mistake
Capital in economics is not money. Money is a way of buying factors; capital is the physical, human-made equipment itself (machines, buildings, tools). Also, investment means spending on capital goods, not buying shares.

Quantity and quality of the factors of production

The quantity of a factor is how much of it is available; its quality is how productive each unit is. Both can rise or fall.

FactorQuantity changes when…Quality changes when…
LandRises: new resources are discovered; land is reclaimed from the sea. Falls: minerals are used up, flooding or rising sea levelsRises: irrigation, fertilisers, better drainage. Falls: soil erosion, pollution, over-farming
LabourRises: population growth, net immigration, a higher retirement age, more women joining the labour force. Falls: net emigration, an ageing population, a higher school-leaving ageRises: better education, training and healthcare → higher productivity. Falls: cuts to training, poor health, long-term unemployment (skills fade)
CapitalRises: investment is greater than depreciation (wear and tear). Falls: worn-out equipment is not replaced; war or natural disaster destroys itRises: technological progress, e.g. more energy-efficient machinery, automation. Falls: equipment becomes outdated or poorly maintained
EnterpriseRises: lower taxes on profit, easier business registration, access to finance. Falls: higher taxes on profit, a recession, emigration of entrepreneursRises: business education and management training, experience of running firms. Falls: less business education and training

An increase in the quantity or quality of resources raises the productive capacity of the economy; a decrease lowers it (see shifts of a PPC in 1.4).

Mobility of the factors of production Background

Backgroundnot required in Topic 1 of the 2027–2029 syllabus, but past papers up to 2025 asked about the mobility of all four factors. The new syllabus keeps only the mobility of labour, which is covered in Topic 3 (3.3.4).

Factor mobility is the ease with which a factor can move between uses: geographical mobility is moving from one place to another; occupational mobility is moving from one job or use to another.

FactorGeographical mobilityOccupational mobility
LandImmobile — land cannot physically moveFairly mobile — a field can switch from wheat to a solar farm or housing (subject to planning rules)
LabourLimited by family ties, housing costs, lack of information about jobs, immigration rules, languageLimited by lack of skills, qualifications and training
CapitalSome is mobile (laptops, vans); some is fixed (a steel works, an oil rig)General capital (computers) is mobile; specialised capital (a car assembly robot) is not
EnterpriseUsually highly mobileUsually highly mobile — organising and risk-taking skills transfer across industries

Low mobility matters because resources get stuck in declining industries, causing unemployment and wasted output.

1.3 Opportunity cost

Opportunity cost is the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.

  • It exists because of scarcity: using resources one way means they cannot be used another way.
  • It is the single next best alternative, not every alternative given up.
  • It is not the money price. The price of a $60 concert ticket is $60; its opportunity cost is the thing you would otherwise have bought or done.

Examples of opportunity cost in different contexts

  • Consumers: spending $60 on a concert ticket means not buying new trainers.
  • Workers: taking a full-time job may mean giving up the chance to go to university (or vice versa).
  • Producers / firms: a farmer who plants maize on a field gives up the soya beans that could have been grown there.
  • Governments: spending tax revenue on a new motorway may mean fewer resources for flood defences or schools.

How opportunity cost influences decision-making

A rational decision-maker compares the benefit of a choice with the benefit of the next best alternative, and picks the option that gives more. If the opportunity cost of a choice rises, that choice becomes less attractive.

Decision-makerWhat they are allocatingHow opportunity cost shapes the decision
ConsumersIncomeBuy the product that gives the most satisfaction for the money; if the price of a holiday rises, more other goods must be given up for it, so some choose something else
WorkersTime and skillsChoose between jobs, or between work and leisure or study; if wages for overtime rise, the opportunity cost of an evening off rises, so some work more hours
Producers / firmsFactors of productionUse land, workers and machines for the product expected to earn the most profit; if the price of soya beans rises, growing maize has a higher opportunity cost, so farmers switch fields to soya
GovernmentsTax revenueCompare the benefit to society of each project; funding a new airport is only worthwhile if it is expected to benefit the country more than the hospitals the same money could have built
Worked example
Aisha has one free Saturday. She could work a shift at a café and earn $40, revise for her mock exams, or go to the beach with friends. She ranks them: 1st revising, 2nd the café shift, 3rd the beach. She chooses to revise. Her opportunity cost is the café shift (the $40 she would have earned): the next best alternative only, not the shift and the beach added together. If the café offered $100 for the shift, the opportunity cost of revising would be higher and she might decide differently.
Model answer
Explain, using an example, why a government decision involves an opportunity cost. [4]
A government has limited tax revenue but unlimited demands on its spending, so it faces scarcity and must choose (1). If it decides to spend $2 billion on building a new airport (1), those same funds and resources cannot be used for other purposes (1). The opportunity cost is the next best alternative forgone, for example the new hospitals that could have been built with that money instead (1).

1.4 Production possibility curve (PPC) diagrams

A production possibility curve shows the maximum combinations of two goods (or types of goods) that an economy can produce when all its resources are fully and efficiently used, with a given state of technology.

How to draw a PPC

1
AxesDraw two axes from the origin (0) and label each with a good or a type of good, e.g. capital goods and consumer goods. Never “Price” and “Quantity”.
2
CurveDraw a curve that touches both axes and slopes downwards all the way, bowed outwards from the origin. Label it PPC.
3
PointsMark and letter any production points the question mentions, with dashed lines to the axes if you need to show quantities.
4
ChangesFor a shift, draw a second curve labelled PPC1 with an arrow showing the direction. For a movement, put an arrow from one point to the other.

What the PPC illustrates

  • Scarcity: combinations beyond the curve cannot be reached with current resources.
  • Choice: the economy must pick one point on (or inside) the curve.
  • Opportunity cost: moving along the curve to produce more of one good means giving up some of the other.

Points under, on and beyond a PPC

PositionMeaning
On the curve (A, D)Efficient: all resources are fully employed and producing the maximum possible output; more of one good requires less of the other
Under (inside) the curve (B)Inefficient: unemployed or under-used resources (e.g. during a recession). More of both goods could be produced with no opportunity cost
Beyond (outside) the curve (C)Unattainable with current resources and technology; could only be reached if the PPC shifts outwards

Movements along a PPC and opportunity cost

  • A movement along the curve (A → D in the first figure) shows a reallocation of resources from one good to another.
  • From A to D: more consumer goods, fewer capital goods. The fall in capital goods is the opportunity cost of the extra consumer goods.
  • A movement from under the curve (B) towards the curve shows previously unemployed resources being used, for example as an economy recovers from a recession. More of both goods can be produced, so there is no opportunity cost.

With numbers on the axes, you can read the opportunity cost straight off the diagram. Country X (illustrative figures) can produce these combinations a year:

MovementConsumer goods gainedCapital goods given up (opportunity cost)Capital goods given up per extra consumer good
A → B84 − 36 = 48 million96 − 64 = 32 million32 ÷ 48 = 0.67
B → C96 − 84 = 12 million64 − 36 = 28 million28 ÷ 12 = 2.33

So the opportunity cost of moving from A to B is 32 million capital goods. The move from B to C adds far fewer consumer goods but still costs 28 million capital goods: the opportunity cost of each extra consumer good has risen. A movement the other way (B → A) gains 32 million capital goods at an opportunity cost of 48 million consumer goods.

Why the PPC is usually bowed outwards

Resources are not equally suited to producing both goods. As an economy specialises more in one good, it has to transfer resources that are less and less suited to it, so the opportunity cost of each extra unit rises (as in the table above). A straight-line PPC would mean a constant opportunity cost (resources equally good at both).

Shifts of a PPC: causes

A PPC shifts when the economy’s productive capacity changes, that is, when the quantity or quality of its factors of production changes.

Outward shift (PPC → right)Inward shift (PPC → left)
Increase in quantity of resources: population growth, immigration, discovery of new minerals, investment in capitalLoss of resources: war, natural disaster, emigration of skilled workers, capital wearing out without replacement
Increase in quality of resources: education and training, better healthcare, new technologyFall in quality: disease reducing labour productivity, soil degradation, depletion of fish stocks, climate-related damage to farmland

Shifts of a PPC: consequences for an economy’s growth

  • An outward shift means the maximum possible output of both goods has risen. The economy now has the capacity to grow: combinations that were unattainable (like C in the first figure) come within reach, so output, incomes and living standards can rise.
  • The shift only shows what the economy could produce. Actual output rises only if the extra capacity is used. If the new resources stay idle, the economy is simply further under its new PPC.
  • An inward shift means productive capacity has fallen. Combinations that used to be possible are now beyond the curve, so maximum output is lower and the economy may shrink (negative economic growth). Draw it the same way with PPC1 nearer the origin and the arrow pointing inwards.
  • If only the technology for producing one good improves (e.g. a new crop variety), the PPC pivots outwards along that good’s axis only.
  • Choosing more capital goods today (lower current consumption) adds to the stock of capital, so the PPC tends to shift further outwards in the future: faster growth later at the opportunity cost of fewer consumer goods now.

Chain of reasoning: government spending on education

1
CauseGovernment increases spending on vocational training and schools.
2
EffectWorkers gain skills, so the quality of labour rises and labour productivity increases.
3
EffectMore output can be produced from the same number of workers; occupational mobility also improves.
4
OutcomeProductive capacity increases, so the PPC shifts outwards. The opportunity cost is the other spending (or lower taxes) given up to fund it.
Common Mistake
Do not say a fall in unemployment shifts the PPC outwards. Putting unemployed workers to work moves the economy from a point under the curve towards the curve. The PPC only shifts when the quantity or quality of resources (or technology) changes.

2.1 The role of markets in allocating resources

A market is any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together to exchange a good or service. It does not have to be a physical place: a market exists wherever buyers and sellers can agree a price.

Examples of markets

  • Product markets: a night market selling food, a supermarket, an online shopping platform, the world market for crude oil.
  • Labour markets: workers sell their labour and firms buy it; the price is the wage.
  • Foreign exchange market: currencies are bought and sold; the price is the foreign exchange rate.
  • Housing and financial markets: homes are bought or rented; shares in companies are traded on a stock exchange.

Roles of buyers and sellers

Buyers (consumers)Sellers (producers/firms)
What they bringDemand: they decide what to buy and how much they are willing and able to paySupply: they decide what to produce and the price they are willing to accept
What they wantThe most satisfaction from a limited income, so they look for low prices and good qualityUsually the most profit, so they look for high prices and low costs
How they affect resourcesTheir spending shows producers which goods are wantedThey move factors of production towards goods that are profitable to make

Together, buyers and sellers determine the price. The market system allocates scarce resources through this price mechanism: prices act as signals. Rising prices signal producers to move resources into a market; falling prices signal them to move resources out (see 2.4).

2.2 Demand

Individual and market demand

Demand is the willingness and ability to buy a good or service at a given price over a given period of time. Wanting something is not enough: demand must be backed by the ability to pay. Individual demand is one consumer’s demand; market demand is the sum of all individual demands at each price.

The law of demand: as price falls, quantity demanded rises (and vice versa), ceteris paribus (all other factors staying the same). Plotting the figures gives a demand curve that slopes down from left to right, with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. To interpret it, read across from a price to the curve and down to the quantity demanded at that price.

Movements along a demand curve

A change in the good’s own price is the only cause of a movement along the demand curve:

  • an extension in demand: price falls, so quantity demanded rises;
  • a contraction in demand: price rises, so quantity demanded falls.

Shifts of a demand curve

A change in any factor other than the good’s own price shifts the whole curve. An increase in demand shifts it to the right; a decrease in demand shifts it to the left.

Population Income Related goods Advertising Tastes Expectations State (taxes, benefits) — “PIRATES”
  • Income: higher income raises demand for most goods; income tax cuts raise disposable income. A fall in income decreases demand.
  • Price of substitutes: a rise in the price of petrol cars increases demand for electric cars.
  • Price of complements: a rise in the price of games consoles reduces demand for games.
  • Tastes and fashion, advertising: growing environmental awareness raises demand for reusable bottles; a health scare decreases demand for a food.
  • Population: size and age structure (an ageing population raises demand for healthcare).
  • Expectations of future prices, and interest rates (cheaper borrowing raises demand for houses and cars).
Common Mistake
Never say “price rises, so demand falls, so the demand curve shifts left”. A change in the good’s own price causes a movement along the curve (an extension or contraction). Only non-price factors shift the curve (an increase or decrease in demand).

2.3 Supply

Individual and market supply

Supply is the willingness and ability of producers to sell a good or service at a given price over a given period of time. Individual supply is the supply of one firm; market supply is the sum of all individual firms’ supply at each price. For example, if at a price of $3 one café is willing to sell 40 cups of iced coffee a week, a second 60 and a third 20, market supply at $3 is 120 cups.

As price rises, quantity supplied rises, because higher prices make production more profitable. The supply curve slopes up from left to right. Read it the same way as a demand curve: across from a price, then down to the quantity supplied.

Movements along a supply curve

A change in the good’s own price causes a movement along the supply curve:

  • an extension in supply: price rises, so quantity supplied rises;
  • a contraction in supply: price falls, so quantity supplied falls.

Shifts of a supply curve

A change in any other factor shifts the whole curve. An increase in supply shifts it to the right (more is supplied at every price); a decrease in supply shifts it to the left.

  • Costs of production: higher wages, raw material or energy costs shift supply left; lower costs shift it right.
  • Technology: more productive methods shift supply right.
  • Indirect taxes (shift left) and subsidies (shift right).
  • Weather and natural events: droughts and floods linked to climate change shift the supply of crops left; a good harvest shifts it right.
  • Number of firms in the market, and prices of other goods the firm could produce instead.

2.4 Price determination

The price mechanism

The price mechanism is the way demand and supply interact to set prices, which then allocate resources. It answers the three basic resource allocation questions without anyone planning it:

  • What to produce: firms produce the goods consumers are willing and able to buy at a profitable price. If demand for a good rises, its price rises, and the higher profit attracts more resources into making it.
  • How to produce: firms choose the lowest-cost combination of factors of production to maximise profit.
  • For whom to produce: output goes to those willing and able to pay the market price.

Market equilibrium

Market equilibrium is the situation where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so there is no tendency for price to change. The price at which this happens is the equilibrium price and the amount traded is the equilibrium quantity.

Market disequilibrium

Market disequilibrium is any situation where quantity demanded does not equal quantity supplied, so there is pressure on price to change.

  • Surplus (supply exceeding demand, also called excess supply): the price is above equilibrium. In the schedule, at $4 firms offer 400 but consumers buy only 200. Unsold stock builds up, so sellers cut prices; demand extends and supply contracts until the surplus disappears.
  • Shortage (demand exceeding supply, also called excess demand): the price is below equilibrium. At $2 consumers want 400 but only 200 are offered. Sellers can raise prices; demand contracts and supply extends until the shortage disappears.

2.5 Price changes

Causes of price changes

Equilibrium price changes only when the demand curve or the supply curve shifts. The shift creates a shortage or surplus at the old price, and the price moves until the market clears again.

Consequences of price changes

  • Sales (quantity sold): when price changes because demand shifted, price and sales move in the same direction (first two rows of the table). When price changes because supply shifted, price and sales move in opposite directions (last two rows).
  • Sales revenue (price × quantity): after a demand shift, revenue moves the same way as price. After a supply shift, the effect depends on price elasticity of demand (2.6): if demand is inelastic, the higher price after a fall in supply raises revenue even though fewer units are sold.
  • Resource allocation: a higher price and higher profit draw resources into the market; a lower price pushes them out.

2.6 Price elasticity of demand (PED)

Definition and calculation

Price elasticity of demand is the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price.

PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price   (% change = change ÷ original × 100)

Value (ignoring the minus sign)DescriptionMeaning
0Perfectly inelasticVertical demand curve; quantity demanded does not change when price changes
Between 0 and 1InelasticQuantity demanded changes by a smaller % than price
Equal to 1UnitarySame % change; total spending unchanged
Greater than 1ElasticQuantity demanded changes by a larger % than price
InfinityPerfectly elasticHorizontal demand curve; any price rise loses all sales
Worked calculation
The price of a bus pass rises from $40 to $50 and monthly sales fall from 2000 to 1800. % change in price = 10 ÷ 40 × 100 = +25%. % change in quantity = −200 ÷ 2000 × 100 = −10%. PED = −10 ÷ 25 = −0.4, so demand is price inelastic: quantity demanded changes by a smaller percentage than price.

Determinants of PED

  • Number and closeness of substitutes: more substitutes → more elastic (demand for one brand of bottled water is elastic; demand for drinking water in general is inelastic).
  • Proportion of income spent on the good: a large share (cars, holidays) → more elastic.
  • Necessity or luxury: necessities and habit-forming goods (e.g. cigarettes) → inelastic; luxuries → more elastic.
  • Time period: demand is more elastic in the long run as consumers find alternatives.

PED, consumer expenditure and firms’ revenue

The amount spent by consumers on a product is the same sum of money as the revenue raised by the firms selling it: consumer expenditure = total revenue = price × quantity sold.

PEDPrice risesPrice falls
InelasticSpending and revenue riseSpending and revenue fall
ElasticSpending and revenue fallSpending and revenue rise
UnitaryNo changeNo change
Revenue as a calculation
Inelastic demand (the bus pass, PED = −0.4). Revenue before = $40 × 2000 = $80 000. Revenue after = $50 × 1800 = $90 000. The price rise increases revenue by $10 000.

Elastic demand. A cinema cuts its ticket price from $10 to $8 and weekly sales rise from 500 to 700. % change in price = −20%; % change in quantity = +40%; PED = 40 ÷ −20 = −2. Revenue rises from $10 × 500 = $5000 to $8 × 700 = $5600, so the price cut increases revenue by $600.

On a diagram, revenue is the rectangle under the price line (price × quantity). Compare the rectangle gained with the rectangle lost: with inelastic demand a price rise gains more than it loses; with elastic demand it loses more than it gains.

Significance of PED for decision-making

  • Consumers: where demand is inelastic (essentials, goods with no close substitute) consumers keep buying after a price rise, so they spend more on that good and have less left for others. Where demand is elastic they can switch to a substitute.
  • Workers: if demand for the product they make is inelastic, the firm can pass a wage rise on as a higher price with little loss of sales, so workers are in a stronger position to bargain. If it is elastic, higher prices lose many sales and jobs are less secure.
  • Producers/firms: PED guides pricing. Raise price if demand is inelastic; cut price if it is elastic. Firms try to make demand more inelastic through branding and advertising.
  • Government: taxing goods with inelastic demand (fuel, tobacco) raises high and reliable revenue, but reduces consumption only a little. A subsidy raises consumption more when demand is elastic.

2.7 Price elasticity of supply (PES)

Definition and calculation

Price elasticity of supply is the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price.

PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price (always positive, because price and quantity supplied move in the same direction).

ValueDescriptionMeaning
0Perfectly inelasticVertical supply curve; quantity supplied cannot change (seats in a stadium tonight)
Between 0 and 1InelasticQuantity supplied changes by a smaller % than price
Equal to 1UnitaryQuantity supplied changes by the same % as price
Greater than 1ElasticQuantity supplied changes by a larger % than price
InfinityPerfectly elasticHorizontal supply curve; firms supply any amount at the going price
Worked calculation
The price of strawberries rises from $2.00 to $2.50 per kg and the quantity supplied rises from 4000 kg to 4400 kg a week. % change in price = 0.50 ÷ 2.00 × 100 = 25%. % change in quantity supplied = 400 ÷ 4000 × 100 = 10%. PES = 10 ÷ 25 = 0.4, so supply is price inelastic: farmers cannot grow many more strawberries straight away.

Determinants of PES

  • Time period: supply is more elastic in the long run (new factories, new crops planted).
  • Spare capacity and stocks: firms with unused machinery or stored goods can raise output quickly.
  • Mobility of factors: if resources can switch easily between uses, supply is elastic.
  • Production time: agricultural goods (months to grow) have inelastic supply in the short run.

Why it matters: when supply is inelastic, a rise in demand mainly raises price rather than output (e.g. city-centre housing).

Examiner Tip
For PED and PES, always interpret your number: state whether it is elastic or inelastic and what that means (“quantity demanded changes proportionately less than price”). Many candidates calculate correctly but lose the interpretation mark. Remember PED is usually negative; PES is positive.

2.9 Market failure

Market failure occurs when the market system leads to an inefficient allocation of resources, i.e. too much or too little of a good is produced and consumed.

Key terms

Public good
Non-excludable and non-rival, e.g. street lighting, flood defences, national defence
Merit good
More beneficial than consumers realise, e.g. education, vaccinations
Demerit good
More harmful than consumers realise, e.g. cigarettes, sugary drinks
Monopoly
A market with a single seller (or one dominant firm) of a product, so consumers have no close alternative
  • Private costs: costs paid by the consumer or producer involved (e.g. a factory’s fuel bill).
  • External costs: costs imposed on third parties not involved in the transaction (e.g. air pollution, CO2 emissions causing climate change, noise from an airport).
  • Social cost = private cost + external cost.
  • Private benefits: benefits to the consumer or producer involved (e.g. the revenue a firm earns, the enjoyment a consumer gets).
  • External benefits: benefits to third parties (e.g. vaccination protects others; a new park improves neighbours’ wellbeing).
  • Social benefit = private benefit + external benefit.
Backgroundnot required by the 2027–2029 syllabus, but useful for contrast: a private good is excludable and rival (e.g. a sandwich), the opposite of a public good.

In each case resources are misallocated: too many or too few are used to make the good.

Common Mistake
A public good is not simply “a good provided by the government”. Education and healthcare are provided by governments but are merit goods (people can be excluded and they are rival). A public good must be non-excludable and non-rival.

These notes cover maximum and minimum prices in product markets, as the syllabus specifies here. A national minimum wage (NMW) is a minimum price in a labour market and is covered in Topic 3.

Indirect taxation

  • Definition: an indirect tax is a tax on spending on goods and services, e.g. VAT/GST, excise duties on fuel, tobacco and sugary drinks, and carbon taxes on emissions.
  • How it works: it raises firms’ costs, shifting supply to the left: price rises and quantity falls.
  • Advantages: reduces consumption of demerit goods; makes consumers and producers pay towards external costs and so promotes environmental sustainability; raises government revenue that can fund merit goods; still leaves the choice to consumers.
  • Disadvantages: if demand is price inelastic, consumers bear most of the tax and consumption falls only a little; it is regressive (takes a larger share of low incomes); it may encourage smuggling; it is hard to set the tax equal to the external cost.

Subsidies

  • Definition: a subsidy is a payment from the government to producers (or sometimes consumers) to lower costs and encourage production or consumption, e.g. subsidies for solar panels, public transport, electric vehicles.
  • How it works: supply shifts to the right: price falls and quantity rises.
  • Advantages: increases consumption of merit goods and goods with external benefits; makes them more affordable for low-income households; supports incomes and jobs in key industries; speeds the switch to low-carbon energy.
  • Disadvantages: opportunity cost of government spending (or higher taxes); firms may become inefficient and dependent on support; consumption rises only a little if demand is price inelastic; firms may keep part of the subsidy as profit instead of cutting price.

Explain how an indirect tax on petrol could reduce an external cost. [4]
Burning petrol creates an external cost, such as air pollution and CO2 emissions that harm third parties (1). An indirect tax raises the costs of sellers, shifting the supply curve to the left (1). With less petrol consumed, fewer emissions are produced, so the external cost falls and resources are allocated closer to the socially efficient level (1).

3.1 Money and banking

Money is anything that is generally accepted as a means of payment for goods and services and settling debts. Barter (swapping goods directly) needs a double coincidence of wants, which money removes.

Forms of money

  • Cash: notes and coins issued by the central bank.
  • Bank deposits: money held in bank accounts. This is the largest part of the money in a modern economy. Debit cards, bank transfers and phone payments are not money themselves; they are ways of moving bank deposits from one account to another.
  • Electronic (digital) money: balances held in e-wallets and payment apps, and in some countries a digital currency issued by the central bank.

Functions of money

  • Medium of exchange — used to buy and sell, removing the need for barter.
  • Unit of account (measure of value) — allows prices of different goods to be compared.
  • Store of value — can be saved and used later (weakened by inflation).
  • Standard of deferred payment — allows borrowing now and repaying later.
Medium Unit Store Deferred — “Money Usually Stays Deferred”

Characteristics of money

To do its job well, money needs to be:

  • Generally acceptable: people must be willing to take it in payment.
  • Durable: it must last and not rot or wear out quickly.
  • Portable: easy to carry around.
  • Divisible: can be split into smaller units to pay for cheap items and give change.
  • Homogeneous (uniform): every unit of the same value is identical.
  • Limited in supply and difficult to forge: so that it keeps its value.

Role of central banks and commercial banks

A central bank is the bank responsible for a country’s money and banking system (in Malaysia, Bank Negara Malaysia). A commercial bank is a business that provides banking services to households and firms in order to make a profit.

Importance of banks

  • Central bank: by controlling inflation it protects the value of money, so households and firms can plan with confidence. As lender of last resort and regulator it keeps the banking system stable, so people trust that their deposits are safe. Its interest rate decisions affect borrowing, spending and saving across the whole economy.
  • Commercial banks, for households: a safe place for savings that pays interest, loans and mortgages to buy expensive items such as homes, and quick, safe ways to pay.
  • Commercial banks, for firms: loans and overdrafts to start up, invest and cover day-to-day costs, plus payment services for selling to customers at home and abroad.
  • Commercial banks, for the economy: they channel savers’ money to borrowers who can use it productively. More investment by firms can raise output and employment.

3.3 Workers

Factors affecting an individual’s choice of occupation

  • Wage factors: basic pay, overtime, bonuses, commission.
  • Non-wage factors: job security, working hours and holidays, promotion prospects, training, working conditions, fringe benefits (company car, pension, health insurance), job satisfaction, location, flexibility such as remote working, and the employer’s environmental or ethical record.

A person may accept a lower wage for a job with strong non-wage benefits, for example a nurse who values job satisfaction and security.

Wage determination: demand for and supply of labour

In a free labour market the wage rate is set where the demand for labour (from firms) equals the supply of labour (from workers).

  • Demand for labour is a derived demand: it depends on demand for the product the workers make. It also depends on labour productivity and the price of capital that could replace workers.
  • Supply of labour to an occupation depends on the wage, non-wage factors, qualifications and training needed, and the size of the working population.
  • Wages can also be influenced by trade unions, governments (national minimum wage, public-sector pay) and employer bargaining power.

Wage determination: trade unions and their relative bargaining power

A trade union is an organisation of workers that aims to protect and improve the interests of its members.

  • Role: negotiate higher wages (collective bargaining, where the union bargains with the employer on behalf of all its members), better working conditions, job security, training, and represent members in disputes; lobby government on issues such as the minimum wage.
  • Industrial action is how a union puts pressure on an employer: strikes, work-to-rule, overtime bans, go-slows.

Relative bargaining power means how strong the workers’ side is compared with the employer’s side. The stronger the union, the further it can push the wage above the level the market alone would set.

Wage determination: government policy and the national minimum wage (NMW)

A national minimum wage (NMW) is the lowest wage rate that employers are allowed by law to pay. To have any effect it must be set above the equilibrium wage; if it is set below, the market wage is already higher and nothing changes.

Reading the diagram: at the higher wage, firms cut back the number of workers they want (a contraction in demand for labour, Q to Qd), while more people want to work (an extension in supply of labour, Q to Qs). Workers who keep their jobs earn more; some lose their jobs or cannot find one.

Other government policies that affect wages: the government sets pay for many public-sector workers (teachers, nurses, police); laws on equal pay and against discrimination; laws that strengthen or limit trade unions; and spending on education and training, which increases the supply of skilled workers.

Reasons for differences in wages

The syllabus gives four reasons why some workers are paid more than others. (Older papers call this “differences in earnings”.)

  • Demand for and supply of labour: wages are high where demand is high (productive workers, a product in strong demand) and supply is low (long training, rare skills, unpleasant or dangerous work that few will do). Wages are low where many people are able and willing to do the job.
  • Relative bargaining strengths: workers in a strong trade union, or with scarce skills, can negotiate higher pay than workers who bargain alone.
  • Discrimination: some workers are paid less, or kept out of better-paid jobs, for reasons that have nothing to do with their productivity, such as gender, ethnic group or age.
  • Government policy: a NMW lifts the lowest wages; the government decides public-sector pay; equal pay laws narrow gaps.

Mobility of labour

  • Occupational mobility is the ability of workers to move from one type of job to another (e.g. a factory worker retrains as an electrician).
  • Geographical mobility is the ability of workers to move from one place to another to take a job (e.g. from a village to the capital city, or to another country).

Consequences of higher mobility

  • Workers who lose jobs in declining industries or regions can move to where the vacancies are, so unemployment is lower.
  • Firms can fill vacancies faster, so labour shortages and upward pressure on wages are reduced, and output can grow.
  • Wage differences narrow: workers move into high-paid jobs and areas (raising supply there) and out of low-paid ones.
  • The economy adjusts more quickly to changes in demand and technology, making better use of its resources.
  • A possible cost: the regions or countries that workers leave may lose their youngest and most skilled workers.

Consequences of lower mobility: the opposite. Unemployment lasts longer in some areas and occupations while firms elsewhere cannot find staff, and wage gaps between jobs and regions stay wide.

3.4 Firms

Different types of firms

  • By sector: primary (extracting natural resources: farming, fishing, mining); secondary (manufacturing and construction); tertiary (services: banking, retail, tourism). As economies develop, the relative importance usually shifts from primary to secondary to tertiary.
  • Private sector firms are owned by individuals/shareholders and usually aim for profit. Public sector organisations are owned by the government and often aim to provide a service.
  • By size: measured by number of employees, value of capital employed, sales revenue or market share.

Advantages and disadvantages of small and large firms

3.5 Firms and production

Production is the process of converting factors of production (inputs) into goods and services (output).

Demand for factors of production

A firm’s demand for land, labour, capital and enterprise is a derived demand. It depends on:

  • Demand for the product: if more of the product is wanted, more factors are needed to make it.
  • The price of different factors: if wages rise compared with the price of machines, firms use more capital and less labour (where one can replace the other).
  • Their availability: a firm can only use the factors it can get. Where skilled workers are in short supply, firms may turn to machines; where land is scarce, they build upwards.
  • Their productivity: the more a factor produces for what it costs, the more of it firms want to use.

Labour-intensive and capital-intensive production

Labour-intensive production uses a high proportion of labour relative to capital (hairdressing, handmade crafts, tea picking). Capital-intensive production uses a high proportion of capital (car assembly, oil refining).

Reasons for the choice: firms choose capital-intensive methods when labour is expensive or scarce, output is large and standardised, and the technology and finance are available. Labour-intensive methods suit personal services, hand-made or customised products, small outputs and economies where wages are low.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Labour-intensiveLow start-up cost; flexible, as workers can change tasks and make customised products; personal service; workers can suggest improvements; creates jobsHigher cost per unit when output is large; quality can vary between workers; risk of strikes, absence and wage demands; costs rise if wages rise
Capital-intensiveLarge output at a low cost per unit (technical economies of scale); consistent quality; machines can run day and nightHigh cost of buying, maintaining and replacing machines; often needs borrowing; less flexible if demand or fashion changes; a breakdown can stop production; workers’ tasks may be repetitive

Production and productivity

Production
Total output of goods and services
Productivity
Output per unit of factor input per time period
Labour productivity
Total output ÷ number of workers

Productivity vs production: production is total output; productivity is how efficiently inputs are turned into output. Higher productivity lowers average costs, can raise wages and profits, and improves international competitiveness.

Worked example
A factory with 20 workers makes 400 chairs a week. It then employs 30 workers and makes 540 chairs a week.
  • Before: labour productivity = 400 ÷ 20 = 20 chairs per worker per week.
  • After: labour productivity = 540 ÷ 30 = 18 chairs per worker per week.
  • Production rose (400 to 540) but productivity fell (20 to 18).
  • Influences on production: the demand for the product, and the quantity and quality of factors of production the firm uses (more workers, machines or land raise output).
  • Influences on productivity: education and training, the amount and quality of capital, new technology, motivation and management, and division of labour.

Effects of changes in investment on productivity

Investment is spending on capital goods such as machinery, equipment, buildings and software.

  • A rise in investment gives each worker more or better equipment, so output per worker rises. For example, a bakery that replaces hand-mixing with an industrial mixer can make more loaves with the same staff. Higher productivity lowers average total cost and makes the firm more competitive.
  • The gain is not automatic. Workers may need training to use the new equipment, production may be disrupted while it is installed, and the money has an opportunity cost (and interest, if it is borrowed).
  • A fall in investment means worn-out machines are not replaced and the firm falls behind on technology, so productivity grows slowly or falls and the firm becomes less competitive.

Two checks that are useful in calculations: ATC = AFC + AVC, and fixed costs still have to be paid when output is zero, so at zero output TC = FC.

Calculation of costs of production

Worked example
A bakery pays rent of $600 a week. Each loaf costs $0.80 in ingredients and energy and sells for $2.00. It sells 1,000 loaves.
  • VC = 1,000 × $0.80 = $800; TC = $600 + $800 = $1,400
  • ATC = $1,400 ÷ 1,000 = $1.40 per loaf; AFC = $600 ÷ 1,000 = $0.60; AVC = $800 ÷ 1,000 = $0.80
  • TR = 1,000 × $2.00 = $2,000; Profit = $2,000 − $1,400 = $600
  • If output doubles to 2,000, AFC falls to $0.30 and ATC to $1.10: fixed costs are spread over more units.

How changes in output affect costs

  • FC is a horizontal line: it is the same at every output, including zero.
  • VC starts at the origin and rises as output rises. TC has the same shape but starts at the level of FC.
  • AFC always falls as output rises, because the same fixed cost is divided by more units.
  • ATC is usually U-shaped. It falls at first, mainly because AFC is falling, then rises when rising AVC outweighs the fall in AFC (at the stall, extra staff get in each other’s way and overtime has to be paid).

Definitions and calculation of revenue

  • Total revenue (TR) is the total amount a firm receives from selling its product: TR = price × quantity sold.
  • Average revenue (AR) is the revenue received per unit sold: AR = TR ÷ quantity sold. If every unit is sold at the same price, AR is equal to the price.
Worked example
The juice stall sells 800 bottles in a week at $2.50 each.
  • TR = $2.50 × 800 = $2,000
  • AR = $2,000 ÷ 800 = $2.50 (the price)
  • Profit = TR − TC = $2,000 − $1,320 = $680
  • Working backwards: a firm with TR of $4,500 from 1,500 units has AR = $4,500 ÷ 1,500 = $3.00.

The influence of sales on revenue

  • If the price stays the same, selling more raises TR in proportion: at $2.50 a bottle, 800 bottles bring in $2,000 and 1,000 bottles bring in $2,500. AR stays at $2.50.
  • If the firm must cut its price to sell more, AR falls, and what happens to TR depends on price elasticity of demand (see 2.6):
    • Price cut from $2.50 to $2.00 and sales rise from 1,000 to 1,400: TR rises from $2,500 to $2,800. Sales rose by 40% after a 20% price cut, so demand is price elastic.
    • Price cut from $2.50 to $2.00 and sales rise only from 1,000 to 1,100: TR falls from $2,500 to $2,200. Sales rose by 10% after a 20% price cut, so demand is price inelastic.
  • More sales do not always mean more profit: compare the extra revenue with the extra cost of producing the extra output.

Objectives of firms

  • Survival — covering costs and staying in business; the main aim for new firms and in a recession.
  • Social welfare — acting in the interests of society rather than for profit alone, e.g. public-sector firms providing an affordable service, charities and social enterprises, or private firms cutting carbon emissions and sourcing ethically.
  • Profit maximisation — making the largest possible gap between TR and TC; the traditional aim of private-sector firms. Profit rewards owners and funds investment.
  • Growth — increasing the size of the firm (output, sales or market share). Larger size brings economies of scale, market power and security.

Objectives can change over time and can conflict: a firm that cuts prices to grow gives up some profit now in the hope of more later.

Backgroundnot required by the 2027–2029 syllabus, but you may see sales revenue maximisation mentioned as a further objective, sometimes preferred by managers whose pay is linked to sales.

3.7 Types of markets

Markets differ in how many firms compete in them. (Older papers call this topic “market structure”.) You need two types: competitive markets and monopoly markets. No diagrams are needed for either.

Competitive markets

A competitive market has a high number of firms competing to sell to the same customers.

  • Characteristics: many firms, each with a small market share; low barriers to entry and exit, so new firms can join easily; similar products; each firm has little or no power over the market price.
  • Advantages: low prices; firms must keep costs down and use resources efficiently; pressure to improve quality and bring out new products; wide choice; firms respond quickly to what consumers want.
  • Disadvantages: firms may be too small to gain economies of scale; low profit leaves little to spend on research and development; some firms may cut quality or wages to keep prices down; firms that cannot compete close, causing job losses.

Monopoly markets

A monopoly is a market with only one firm selling the product. (In practice the term is also used for one firm that dominates a market.)

  • Characteristics: one seller, so the firm is the industry; high barriers to entry (legal barriers such as patents and licences, large economies of scale, control of a key resource, high start-up costs, brand loyalty); a product with no close substitutes; the firm is a price-maker (it can choose its price, but then consumers decide how much to buy).

Model answer
Explain why a surgeon usually earns more than a shop assistant. [4]
A surgeon needs many years of training and high qualifications, so few people are able to enter the occupation and the supply of surgeons is low. The demand for surgeons is high because their skills are essential and their productivity (value of output) is great. High demand and low supply result in a high equilibrium wage. In contrast, shop assistant work requires little training, so supply is large, and each assistant can be replaced easily, so the equilibrium wage is lower.

Common Mistake
Confusing productivity with production. A firm can produce more simply by hiring more workers while productivity (output per worker) stays the same or falls.

4.1 Government macroeconomic intervention

Macroeconomics looks at the economy as a whole: total output, total employment and the general price level. Governments intervene because markets left alone do not always deliver steady growth, jobs for everyone who wants one, or stable prices.

The six macroeconomic aims

Economic growth
A rise in real GDP over time
Full employment / low unemployment
Almost everyone willing and able to work has a job
Stable prices / low inflation
The general price level rises slowly and predictably
Balance of payments stability
Avoid large, persistent current account deficits or surpluses
Redistribution of income
Reduce the gap between high and low incomes
Environmental sustainability
Meet present needs without damaging future generations’ ability to meet theirs

Why governments choose these aims, and the criteria they may set

A criterion is the measure a government uses to judge whether an aim has been met. Many governments turn an aim into a target figure. The figures below are illustrations of the kind of target used, not rules: each government sets its own.

Redistribution of incomeReduces poverty and gives people a fairer chance; very unequal incomes can cause social tensionA fall in the percentage of households below the poverty line, or a narrower gap between the richest and poorest groups
Environmental sustainabilityPollution, climate change and running out of resources threaten future output and healthA target to cut carbon emissions by a set date, or a target share of electricity from renewable sources

Governments rarely treat all six as equally urgent. The priority depends on the problem of the moment: a country with 12% inflation will put stable prices first, while one in a recession will put growth and jobs first.

Possible conflicts between aims

A conflict means that a policy which moves the economy towards one aim moves it away from another. The syllabus names three:

  • Full employment and stable prices: as unemployment falls, more people earn and spend, so total demand rises and may cause demand-pull inflation. Firms also find it harder to recruit, so they offer higher wages, which raises costs and prices.
  • Economic growth and environmental sustainability: producing more usually uses more energy and raw materials and creates more waste, so carbon emissions, pollution and resource depletion rise.
  • Full employment and balance of payments stability: when more people are in work, incomes are higher and households buy more imports (and firms import more raw materials). Goods that could have been exported may be sold at home instead. The current account moves towards deficit.

Other conflicts you could mention: fast growth in total demand can cause inflation, and very high taxes used to redistribute income may weaken incentives to work and invest, slowing growth.

Examiner Tip
Conflicts are possible, not certain. Supply-side policy can raise growth and employment while holding prices down, and investment in green technology can support both growth and sustainability. Saying this earns evaluation marks.

The role of government Background

Backgroundnot required by the 2027–2029 syllabus, but it appeared in older past papers. Governments act locally (refuse collection, local roads), nationally (defence, education, healthcare, benefits, regulation, managing the macroeconomy) and internationally (trade agreements, cooperation on climate change). A government is also a producer, through state-owned enterprises, and an employer.

4.2 Fiscal policy

The government budget

  • Government budget: a statement of the government’s planned spending and planned revenue (mainly from taxes) for the year ahead.
  • Government budget deficit: government spending is greater than government revenue. The gap is covered by borrowing, which adds to the national debt.
  • Government budget surplus: government revenue is greater than government spending. The surplus can be used to repay past borrowing.
  • A balanced budget means the two are equal.
Budget balance
Government revenue − government spending (negative = deficit, positive = surplus)
Worked example · budget deficit and surplus

Country X (illustrative figures): tax revenue $385 billion, government spending $420 billion.
Budget balance = 385 − 420 = −$35 billion, so there is a budget deficit of $35 billion.
If GDP is $700 billion, the deficit is 35 ÷ 700 × 100 = 5% of GDP.

Country Y: government revenue $510 billion, government spending $495 billion.
Budget balance = 510 − 495 = +$15 billion, a budget surplus of $15 billion.

Common Mistake
Confusing a budget deficit with the national debt. The deficit is the shortfall in one year; the national debt is the total the government owes from all past borrowing. Also give the answer with its sign in words (“a deficit of $35 billion”) and its units.

Government spending is also used to influence total demand: raising it to boost output and jobs, cutting it to reduce inflation. Every area of spending has an opportunity cost, because the money could have been spent elsewhere or left with taxpayers.

Reasons for taxation

The syllabus lists six:

  • Raising revenue to pay for government spending.
  • Discouraging consumption of demerit goods: taxes on tobacco, alcohol and sugary drinks raise their prices.
  • Reducing imports: a tariff (a tax on imports) makes foreign goods dearer, so consumers switch to home-produced goods.
  • Redistributing income: progressive taxes take proportionately more from high earners, and the revenue funds benefits and services for those on low incomes.
  • Influencing total demand: higher taxes reduce spending and inflation; lower taxes raise spending, output and employment.
  • Encouraging environmental sustainability: carbon taxes, fuel duty and landfill taxes make polluters pay for the external costs they create.

Worked example · why an indirect tax can be regressive

Two people each pay $300 a year in tax on the fuel they buy. Asha earns $10 000 a year: 300 ÷ 10 000 × 100 = 3% of her income. Ben earns $60 000: 300 ÷ 60 000 × 100 = 0.5% of his income. The amount is the same, but the percentage of income is higher for the lower earner, so the tax is regressive.

Common Mistake
Defining a progressive tax as one where “the rich pay more”. Under a proportional tax the rich also pay a larger amount. A progressive tax takes a higher proportion (percentage) of income as income rises.

Impact of taxation

  • Consumers: direct taxes cut disposable income; indirect taxes raise prices. Both reduce purchasing power, and indirect taxes change what people buy (less of the taxed product).
  • Workers: higher income tax lowers take-home pay and may weaken the incentive to work extra hours, seek promotion or enter the labour force.
  • Producers/firms: corporation tax reduces profit after tax, leaving less to invest; indirect taxes raise costs and may reduce sales; higher income tax on customers lowers demand.
  • The government: tax is its main source of revenue. A higher tax rate usually raises more, but not if it sharply reduces work, sales or encourages tax avoidance.
  • The economy: higher taxes reduce total demand, which may slow growth and raise unemployment but lower inflation and imports. Progressive taxes reduce inequality; environmental taxes reduce pollution.

Qualities of a good tax Background

Backgroundnot required by the 2027–2029 syllabus, but useful for evaluating a tax. A good tax is equitable (based on ability to pay), certain (people know how much and when to pay), convenient to pay, economical to collect, flexible, and does not badly damage incentives to work, save or invest.

Fiscal policy is the use of government spending and taxation to influence total demand (aggregate demand) and so the level of economic activity. The two measures are changes in taxes and changes in government spending.

4.3 Monetary policy

Definitions

  • Money supply: the total amount of money in an economy, made up of notes and coins in circulation plus the deposits held in bank accounts.
  • Monetary policy: decisions, usually taken by the central bank, on the interest rate, the money supply and the foreign exchange rate, made in order to influence total demand.

Monetary policy measures

  • Changes in the interest rate: the central bank sets a key rate, and commercial banks change the rates they charge borrowers and pay savers in line with it.
  • Changes in the money supply: the central bank can make it easier or harder for commercial banks to lend, or create new money and use it to buy government bonds from banks, giving them more funds to lend.
  • Changes in the foreign exchange rate: the central bank can buy or sell its own currency, or change the interest rate, to move the currency’s value. A lower exchange rate makes exports cheaper and imports dearer; a higher one does the opposite.

Monetary policy is a blunt tool for the other two aims: an interest rate change cannot be aimed at low-income households or at polluters, although lower rates do make it cheaper to borrow for green investment.

Limits: it can take a year or more for an interest rate change to have its full effect; if confidence is low, households and firms may not borrow even when rates are cut; the rate cannot be cut far below zero; a higher rate hurts borrowers and discourages investment, which harms long-run growth.

4.4 Supply-side policy

Supply-side policy is government action to increase the economy’s productive capacity (its total supply) by raising the quantity and quality of resources and making markets work more efficiently. On a PPC diagram, successful supply-side policy shifts the curve outwards.

Effects of supply-side policy on the macroeconomic aims

  • Economic growth: more and better resources raise productive capacity, allowing output to keep rising in the long run.
  • Full employment: training and better job information reduce structural and frictional unemployment; stronger incentives bring more people into work.
  • Stable prices: higher productivity lowers firms’ costs, and more competition holds prices down, so growth can happen without inflation.
  • Balance of payments stability: lower costs and better quality make exports more competitive and home-produced goods more attractive than imports.
  • Redistribution of income: education and training can raise the earnings of low-paid workers, but lower direct taxes and benefit reforms may widen the gap.
  • Environmental sustainability: investment in renewable energy and clean technology helps; deregulation may harm it.

Evaluation: supply-side policy is the only type that can raise growth and employment while lowering inflation, so it avoids some conflicts between aims. But it is costly, takes a long time to work, has uncertain results, and does little if the problem is a lack of total demand, since extra capacity is no use if nobody is buying.

4.5 Economic growth

Definition and measurement

  • Economic growth is an increase in the real output of an economy over time.
  • Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the total value of the goods and services produced in a country in a given period, usually a year.
  • Real GDP is GDP adjusted for inflation, so it changes only when the volume of output changes. Growth is measured by the percentage change in real GDP. (Nominal GDP is measured at current prices, so it can rise just because prices rose.)
  • Real GDP per head (real GDP ÷ population) is used to compare living standards: see Topic 5.
Economic growth rate
(Real GDP this year − real GDP last year) ÷ real GDP last year × 100
Worked example · growth rate

Country X’s real GDP rises from $500 billion to $515 billion. Growth rate = (515 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 = 15 ÷ 500 × 100 = 3%.

Why “real” matters: if nominal GDP rose by 6% in a year when prices rose by 4%, output itself grew by only about 2%.

Causes of economic growth

  • An increase in total demand: more consumer spending, investment, government spending or net exports. Firms respond by using spare capacity and unemployed workers to produce more (a movement from inside the PPC towards the curve).
  • An increase in the quantity of resources: a larger labour force (immigration, a higher retirement age, more women working), more capital goods from investment, newly discovered natural resources.
  • An increase in the quality of resources: better education, training and healthcare raise labour productivity; new technology makes capital more productive.

More or better resources shift the PPC outwards, so the economy can produce more. Demand must also rise for that extra capacity to be used.

Recession: definition and causes

A recession is a fall in real GDP for two consecutive quarters (six months) or more.

  • A decrease in total demand: a fall in consumer or business confidence, a rise in interest rates or taxes, cuts in government spending, a banking crisis, or a downturn abroad that reduces exports.
  • A decrease in the quantity of resources: net emigration of workers, natural disasters or war destroying capital, natural resources running out.
  • A decrease in the quality of resources: a serious outbreak of disease lowering workers’ health and productivity, skills lost during long periods of unemployment, capital wearing out because firms stop replacing it.

Producers/firmsLower sales revenue and profit; investment is cut; some firms close. Firms selling low-priced basics may see demand rise, and surviving firms find it easier to recruit.
The governmentLess revenue from income tax, sales taxes and corporation tax, and more spending on unemployment benefits, so the budget moves towards a deficit (or a larger one). Pressure to act to raise total demand.

The best results usually come from a mix: demand-side policy to use existing capacity now, and supply-side policy to expand capacity for the future. Many governments also aim for growth that is environmentally sustainable, for example by investing in renewable energy.

4.6 Employment and unemployment

Definitions

  • Employment: people who have a paid job, working for an employer or for themselves.
  • Unemployment: people of working age who are willing and able to work and are actively looking for a job, but do not have one.
  • Full employment: the lowest level of unemployment that can realistically be reached. It is not zero, because there will always be some people between jobs.
  • The labour force is the employed plus the unemployed. Students, full-time carers, retired people and others who are not looking for work are outside the labour force, so they are not counted as unemployed.

Measurement of unemployment

Unemployment is measured with a labour force survey. A large sample of households is interviewed at regular intervals and asked whether each adult has a job and, if not, whether they have recently looked for work and are available to start. The results are scaled up to estimate the figure for the whole country.

  • Strengths: it counts everyone who is looking for work, whether or not they claim benefits, and it uses an internationally agreed definition, so countries can be compared.
  • Weaknesses: it is an estimate from a sample, so there is some sampling error; it is costly and takes time to collect; it does not show people who have given up looking, or part-time workers who want more hours.
Unemployment rate
Number of unemployed ÷ labour force × 100
Labour force
Employed + unemployed
Worked example · unemployment rate

Country X has a population of 20 million, of whom 9.2 million are employed and 0.8 million are unemployed.
Labour force = 9.2 + 0.8 = 10 million. Unemployment rate = 0.8 ÷ 10 × 100 = 8%.

Common Mistake
Dividing by the population (0.8 ÷ 20 gives 4%) or by the number employed. The unemployment rate is always a percentage of the labour force.
Backgroundnot required by the 2027–2029 syllabus, but older past papers also mention the claimant count, which simply counts the people receiving unemployment benefits. It is cheap and quick but leaves out unemployed people who do not, or cannot, claim.

  • Producers/firms: unemployed people spend less, so sales fall. On the other hand there are more applicants for each vacancy and less pressure to raise wages.
  • The government: less revenue from income tax and sales taxes, and more spending on benefits, so the budget moves towards a deficit. Money spent on benefits has an opportunity cost.
  • The economy: output is below its potential (a point inside the PPC), so resources are wasted; inequality and poverty may rise, and crime may increase. Inflation is likely to be lower because demand is weak.

4.7 Inflation

Definitions

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level over time. Each unit of money buys less, so the purchasing power of money falls.
  • Deflation: a sustained fall in the general price level.
Common Mistake
Thinking that a fall in the inflation rate means prices are falling. If inflation falls from 6% to 3%, prices are still rising, just more slowly. Only negative inflation (deflation) means the price level is falling.

Causes of inflation

5.1 Living standards

Living standards describe the quality of life and material well-being of people in a country. Economic development is a broader idea than economic growth: it means an improvement in people’s welfare, including income, health, education and freedom of choice.

The syllabus names two indicators of living standards: real GDP per head and the Human Development Index (HDI). You need to know what each one measures and the advantages and disadvantages of each.

Real GDP per head

  • Real GDP per head (per capita) = real GDP ÷ population. It shows the average output (and income) per person, adjusted for inflation.
  • “Real” removes the effect of price changes, so a rise shows more goods and services, not just higher prices.
  • “Per head” allows for population size, so a large country is not automatically ranked above a small one.

Real GDP ÷ population

Real GDP grows faster than population

Worked example (illustrative figures)
Country A has a real GDP of $240 billion and a population of 30 million.
Real GDP per head = $240,000 million ÷ 30 million = $8,000.
Next year real GDP rises by 3% to $247.2 billion, but the population rises by 5% to 31.5 million.
Real GDP per head = $247,200 million ÷ 31.5 million = $7,848 (to the nearest dollar), a fall of about 1.9%. The economy grew, yet the average person is worse off because population grew faster than output.

Advantages of real GDP per head as an indicator

  • Simple and widely available: almost every country measures GDP and population, so figures are easy to find and to compare over time.
  • Adjusted for inflation and population: it shows the real quantity of goods and services available to the average person.
  • Income matters: higher average income usually means people can afford more and better food, housing, healthcare and education, so it is a reasonable guide to material living standards.
  • Objective: it is a single figure in money terms, not an opinion about what a good life is.

Disadvantages of real GDP per head as an indicator

  • It is an average — it hides the distribution of income. A few very rich people can raise the average while most people remain poor.
  • Informal (hidden) economy — unrecorded activity (cash-in-hand work, illegal trade) is excluded, so GDP may understate true output, especially in low-income countries.
  • Non-marketed output — subsistence farming, housework and voluntary work are not counted.
  • Quality of life factors are ignored — working hours, leisure time, pollution, crime, political freedom and stress.
  • Environmental damage — output from deforestation or heavy CO2 emissions raises GDP but reduces sustainability and future welfare.
  • Composition of output — high spending on defence adds to GDP but may not improve living standards as much as spending on schools or hospitals.
  • International comparisons — figures must be converted into one currency; foreign exchange rates may not reflect differences in the cost of living.

  • Broader than real GDP per head: it includes health and education, not just income.
  • Income is adjusted for differences in the cost of living (purchasing power).
  • One figure between 0 and 1 makes it easy to rank countries and to track one country’s progress over time.

Disadvantages of HDI as an indicator

  • Still uses averages, so it ignores inequality between groups, regions and genders.
  • Leaves out many aspects of well-being: environmental quality, political freedom, crime, working conditions, housing.
  • Years of schooling measure quantity, not the quality of education.
  • Data in low-income countries may be inaccurate or out of date.
  • Two countries can have the same HDI with very different mixes of income, health and education.

5.2 Poverty

Absolute and relative poverty

  • Absolute poverty: a situation where people do not have enough income to meet their basic needs — food, clean water, shelter, clothing and basic healthcare. It is often measured using an international poverty line (an income per day, in purchasing-power terms).
  • Relative poverty: a situation where people are poor compared with others in the same society, often defined as having an income below a certain percentage (e.g. 50% or 60%) of the median income. They can meet basic needs but cannot afford the standard of living most people enjoy.

Common Mistake
Saying relative poverty only exists in poor countries. Relative poverty exists in every country, including the richest, because it is measured against typical incomes in that country. Absolute poverty can be eliminated in theory; relative poverty is much harder to remove entirely while incomes are unequal.

Causes of poverty

The syllabus names five causes. Learn these first.

  • Unemployment — no earned income; the household may depend on low state benefits, or on nothing at all where there is no benefit system. The longer someone is out of work, the more their skills fade and the harder it is to get back in.
  • Low wages — people in low-skilled, low-productivity jobs may work full time and still not earn enough (“the working poor”). Workers in the informal sector have no minimum wage or legal protection.
  • Illness — poor health or disability limits the ability to work and earn, and medical bills use up income and savings where healthcare is not free. A carer in the family may also have to give up work.
  • Age — the elderly have stopped earning and may have little or no pension or savings; children depend on their parents’ income, so large families with one low earner are at risk; young workers have little experience and often the lowest pay.
  • Environmental factors — droughts, floods, storms and earthquakes destroy crops, homes and capital; poor soil, lack of clean water and a changing climate keep farm output low; pollution damages health. Poor households are hit hardest because they often live in the most exposed places and have no insurance or savings to recover.

Other causes worth knowing: lack of education and training (limits access to better-paid jobs), poor infrastructure and lack of access to finance, conflict, inflation reducing the real value of low fixed incomes, and a very unequal distribution of wealth and land.

5.3 Population

Factors that affect population growth: definitions

  • Birth rate: the number of live births per 1,000 of the population in a year.
  • Death rate: the number of deaths per 1,000 of the population in a year.
  • Immigration: people coming into a country to live there.
  • Emigration: people leaving a country to live elsewhere.
  • Net migration: the difference between immigration and emigration over a period (immigration − emigration). Positive = net immigration; negative = net emigration.

Example: a birth rate of 22 per 1,000 and a death rate of 8 per 1,000 gives a natural increase of 14 per 1,000, i.e. 1.4% a year before migration.

Worked example (illustrative figures)
Country X has a population of 20 million, a birth rate of 30 per 1,000 and a death rate of 10 per 1,000. In the year, 150,000 people immigrate and 50,000 emigrate.
Births = 30 × 20,000 = 600,000. Deaths = 10 × 20,000 = 200,000. Natural increase = 400,000.
Net migration = 150,000 − 50,000 = +100,000.
Population change = 400,000 + 100,000 = +500,000, which is 500,000 ÷ 20,000,000 × 100 = 2.5% growth.

Reasons why birth rates, death rates and net migration vary between countries

  • High birth rates in many low-income countries because:
    • children can work and support parents in old age (no state pensions);
    • high infant mortality encourages families to have more children;
    • limited access to, or knowledge of, contraception;
    • cultural and religious traditions; early marriage;
    • fewer women in education and paid work.
  • Low birth rates in high-income countries because children are expensive to raise and educate, more women pursue careers, people marry later, and contraception is widely available.
  • Death rates have fallen almost everywhere due to better healthcare, vaccinations, nutrition, clean water and sanitation. They remain higher where these are lacking or where there is conflict or disease, and in countries with a large share of elderly people.
  • Net migration: people move to countries with more jobs, higher wages, better public services or political stability (pull factors), and leave countries with conflict, persecution, poverty or climate-related disasters (push factors). Government immigration rules also matter.

The concept of an optimum population

  • The optimum population is the size of population that, combined with the country’s other resources and current technology, maximises output (real GDP) per head.
  • Under-population: too few people to make full use of resources — an increase in population would raise real GDP per head.
  • Over-population: too many people for the resources available — real GDP per head would be higher with a smaller population.
  • The optimum is not fixed: improvements in technology, capital and education raise it over time.

Effects of changes in the gender distribution

Gender distribution is the balance between males and females in the population. It is usually close to equal, but it can shift.

  • More women than men at older ages: women live longer on average, so an ageing population has a rising share of elderly women, many living alone. This raises demand for pensions, healthcare and care services.
  • More men than women (for example, where many male workers immigrate for construction or mining jobs): the labour force is larger relative to the population, but fewer women of child-bearing age means a lower birth rate and slower population growth in future.
  • More women than men of working age (for example, where many men emigrate to find work): families may receive remittances, but the home country can face labour shortages in jobs traditionally done by men, and the birth rate may fall.
  • Any change in the balance alters the pattern of demand for goods and services, and the supply of labour to occupations where one gender is over-represented.

Dependency ratio Background

  • The dependent population are those not of working age: young dependants (usually under 15/16) and elderly dependants (usually over 64/65).
  • Dependency ratio = (number of dependants ÷ number of people of working age) × 100.
  • A high ratio means each worker supports more dependants, putting pressure on taxes and government spending. It is a handy way to explain the cost of an ageing or very young population.

Model answer
Explain two reasons why an ageing population may reduce a country’s economic growth. (4 marks)
First, an ageing population means a smaller proportion of people are of working age. The labour force shrinks, so the country has fewer workers to produce goods and services, reducing its productive capacity and real GDP. Second, the government has to spend more on state pensions and healthcare for the elderly. This may require higher taxes on workers and firms, reducing incentives to work and invest, or it may mean less government spending on infrastructure and education, which lowers future growth.

6.1 Specialisation and free trade

Specialisation by country

Specialisation by country occurs when a country concentrates on producing a limited range of goods and services, and trades to obtain the others.

The basis for specialisation. A country should specialise in the products where its resources are allocated best and/or where it can produce at low cost compared with other countries. This depends on the factors of production it has: climate and land, natural resources, the size and skills of its labour force, capital and technology. Resources then go to the use where they give up the least other output (the lowest opportunity cost).

  • Examples: Saudi Arabia (oil), Bangladesh (garments), Switzerland (financial services, pharmaceuticals), New Zealand (dairy).
  • Illustration: Country X has fertile land and a warm climate, so it can grow rice more cheaply than Country Y. Country Y has skilled engineers and modern factories, so it can make machinery more cheaply. If each specialises and they trade, total output of both goods can be higher than if each tried to make everything.

Definition of globalisation

Reasons for trade restrictions

  • Protect infant (sunrise) industries: new industries need time to grow and gain economies of scale before they can compete with established foreign rivals.
  • Protect declining (sunset) industries: slowing the decline avoids sudden structural unemployment and gives workers time to retrain and move to other jobs.
  • Protect strategic industries: food, energy and defence, so the country does not depend on others for essentials.
  • Avoid dumping: dumping is when foreign firms sell in another country at a price below their cost of production, to drive out domestic competitors.
  • Reduce a deficit on the current account of the balance of payments: restrictions cut spending on imports.
  • Raise tax revenue: tariffs bring in revenue for the government.
  • Restrict the import of demerit goods: e.g. high tariffs or bans on imported cigarettes, alcohol or unsafe products, to protect consumers’ health.
  • Promote environmental sustainability: e.g. tariffs on goods made with high carbon emissions, bans on imports of illegally logged timber or products from endangered species; this protects the environment and stops firms in countries with strict rules being undercut by polluters.

Overall, restricting free trade means countries specialise less, so world output and living standards tend to be lower. Protection is easier to justify when it is temporary and has a clear purpose (an infant industry, dumping) than when it shelters inefficient firms for years.

6.3 Foreign exchange rates

Definition of foreign exchange rate

The foreign exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another (e.g. RM1 = US$0.25). The foreign exchange market is where currencies are bought and sold.

Reasons for buying and selling foreign currencies

Anyone who buys a currency adds to the demand for it; anyone who sells it adds to its supply. The examples use the Malaysian ringgit (RM).

Determination of the foreign exchange rate in the foreign exchange market

Floating exchange rate
Set by market forces: the demand for and supply of the currency
Appreciation

Depreciation

Equilibrium rate

  • The demand curve for a currency slopes down: when the ringgit is cheaper, Malaysian exports cost foreigners less, so they buy more of them and need more ringgit.
  • The supply curve slopes up: when the ringgit is worth more, imports cost Malaysians less, so they buy more of them and sell more ringgit.
  • The equilibrium foreign exchange rate is where the demand for the currency equals its supply. A change in any of the reasons above shifts demand or supply and moves the rate.

Causes of foreign exchange rate fluctuations

  • Changes in demand for exports and imports: higher export sales raise demand for the currency (appreciation); higher spending on imports raises its supply (depreciation). For example, if a country’s inflation is higher than its trading partners’, its exports become less competitive and imports more attractive, so the currency depreciates.
  • Changes in the interest rate: a higher interest rate attracts savings from abroad (“hot money”), raising demand for the currency, so it appreciates. A lower interest rate does the opposite.
  • Speculation: if speculators expect a currency to rise they buy it, which raises demand and makes it rise; if they expect a fall they sell it, which raises supply and makes it fall.

The other reasons for buying and selling currencies shift the curves in the same way: for example, MNCs investing in a country raise demand for its currency, and a central bank selling its own currency raises supply.

Worked example: a depreciation
The ringgit depreciates from RM1 = US$0.25 to RM1 = US$0.20 (illustrative figures).

Fixed exchange rates Background

Backgroundnot required by the 2027–2029 syllabus, but you will meet these terms in older past papers. A fixed exchange rate is set by the government or central bank against another currency and kept there by buying or selling the currency with reserves, or by changing interest rates. It gives certainty to traders and investors but needs large reserves to defend. When a government raises a fixed rate it is a revaluation; when it lowers it, a devaluation. (Appreciation and depreciation are the words for a floating rate.)

Causes of current account deficit and surplus

Reasons for a deficit:

  • High domestic incomes / consumer boom — more spending on imports.
  • Lack of international competitiveness — high inflation, low productivity, high costs or poor quality.
  • A high (appreciated) exchange rate — exports dearer, imports cheaper.
  • Recession abroad — lower demand for the country’s exports.
  • Lack of natural resources, so raw materials and fuel must be imported; outflows of profits to foreign MNCs.

Reasons for a surplus: the reverse. Strong competitiveness (low inflation, high productivity, good quality), a low exchange rate, high world demand for its exports (e.g. commodities at high prices), low domestic demand for imports, or large inflows of profit and remittances from abroad.

  • A surplus is not all good news: residents consume fewer goods and services than the country could afford, and trading partners with deficits may respond with trade restrictions.

Policies to achieve balance of payments stability

Balance of payments stability means avoiding a large, lasting deficit or surplus on the current account. Most exam questions ask about reducing a deficit:

Model answer
Explain how a rise in a country’s inflation rate could cause a current account deficit. (4 marks)
If the country’s inflation rate rises above that of its trading partners, its goods and services become relatively more expensive. Foreign buyers switch to cheaper products from other countries, so the value of the country’s exports falls. At the same time, domestic consumers find imports relatively cheaper than domestically produced goods, so spending on imports rises. With export revenue falling and import spending rising, debits exceed credits, causing a deficit on the trade in goods and services and so on the current account.

Example 1: Reading opportunity cost off a PPC

Practice question · Topic 1.4 · [6 marks]

Country X uses all its resources to produce two goods, wheat and machines. The diagram shows its production possibility curve (PPC), drawn to scale. (a) Calculate the opportunity cost of increasing the output of machines from 10 thousand to 20 thousand. [2] (b) Describe how the opportunity cost of machines changes as more machines are produced. Use figures from the diagram. [2] (c) Explain what point U shows about Country X’s economy. [2]

(a) This is a movement along the PPC from A to B. Wheat output falls from 80 million tonnes to 50 million tonnes (1). The opportunity cost of the extra 10 thousand machines is 80 − 50 = 30 million tonnes of wheat (1).

(b) The opportunity cost rises as more machines are made (1). The first 10 thousand machines cost 10 million tonnes of wheat (90 to 80), the next 10 thousand cost 30 million tonnes (80 to 50) and the last 10 thousand cost 50 million tonnes (50 to 0) (1).

(c) Point U is under (inside) the PPC: Country X is producing 10 thousand machines and 50 million tonnes of wheat, which is less than it is able to produce (1). Some resources are unemployed or being used inefficiently, for example workers without jobs or farmland left idle (1). Moving from U up to A would give 30 million tonnes more wheat with no machines given up, so there is no opportunity cost.

Why this scores
Opportunity cost is always stated in units of the other good: the answer to (a) is “30 million tonnes of wheat”, not “10 thousand machines” and not a sum of money. In (b) the figures prove the pattern. The PPC bows outwards because resources are not equally good at making both goods, so each extra batch of machines takes resources that were better suited to wheat.

Example 2: A PPC shift and economic growth

Practice question · Topic 1.4 · [6 marks]

Country Y produces agricultural goods and manufactured goods. After several years of investment in new factories, irrigation and worker training, its production possibility curve shifts from PPC to PPC1 and the economy moves from point A to point B. (a) Calculate the percentage increase in the maximum possible output of manufactured goods. [2] (b) Explain why the shift from PPC to PPC1 shows economic growth. [2] (c) Explain, using the figures at A and B, why the move from A to B was not possible before the shift. [2]

(a) Maximum output of manufactured goods rises from 60 million units to 90 million units, an increase of 30 million (1). Percentage increase = 30 ÷ 60 × 100 = 50% (1).

(b) An outward shift means the economy’s productive capacity has increased: with all resources fully used, Country Y can now make more of both goods than before (1). This happened because the quantity and quality of its factors of production rose: more capital goods (factories, irrigation) and more skilled labour (1).

(c) At A, Country Y produces 15 million tonnes of agricultural goods and 45 million units of manufactured goods. At B it produces 30 million tonnes and 50 million units, so output of both goods is higher (1). B lies beyond the original PPC, so it was unattainable with the old resources: on PPC, producing 30 million tonnes of agricultural goods would have left no resources for manufactured goods at all (1).

Examiner Tip
Keep two ideas apart. A movement along a PPC reallocates the same resources, so more of one good means less of the other. A shift of the PPC changes what is possible. In this example agricultural capacity also grows by 50% (30 to 45 million tonnes), so the whole curve moves out. Growth is not free, though: the resources used to build the factories could have made consumer goods at the time.

Example 4: PED and the two revenue rectangles

Practice question · Topic 2.6 · [6 marks]

Aurora Cinema cuts its ticket price from $10 to $8. Weekly ticket sales rise from 400 to 600, as shown by points A and B on the diagram. Example 5: A national minimum wage on a labour-market diagram

Practice question · Topic 3.3 · [8 marks]

The diagram shows the market for fruit pickers in Country W. (a) Identify the equilibrium wage rate and the number of workers employed at equilibrium. [2] (b) The government introduces a national minimum wage (NMW) of $7 per hour. Calculate the excess supply of labour this causes. [2] (c) Analyse how the NMW affects workers in this market. [4]

(a) Demand for labour and supply of labour cross at E: the equilibrium wage rate is $6 per hour (1) and 60 thousand workers are employed (1).

(b) At $7 per hour, firms demand 50 thousand workers and 80 thousand workers are willing to work (1). Excess supply of labour = 80 − 50 = 30 thousand workers (1).

(c) The 50 thousand workers who keep their jobs gain: their wage rises from $6 to $7 per hour, about 17% more, so they can afford more goods and services (1). However, labour is now more expensive, so firms’ demand for labour contracts from 60 thousand to 50 thousand and 10 thousand pickers lose their jobs and their wage income (1). The higher wage also attracts more people into this market: supply of labour extends from 60 thousand to 80 thousand, so another 20 thousand people look for picking work but cannot find it (1). Altogether 30 thousand people who want to work at $7 are unemployed, so the NMW helps those in work at the expense of those who are not (1).

Why this scores
The answer reads three numbers off the NMW line (50, 60 and 80) and explains each gap, instead of just writing “unemployment rises”. The excess supply of 30 thousand has two parts: 10 thousand jobs lost (60 to 50) plus 20 thousand new job-seekers (60 to 80). An NMW only has these effects when it is set above the equilibrium wage. For a “Discuss” question you would add the other side: the size of the job loss depends on how much firms’ demand for labour responds to the wage, and better-paid workers may be more motivated and productive.

0455/23/M/J/20 (c) · [6 marks]

Analyse the influences on the mobility of two factors of production.

Backgroundnot required by the 2027–2029 syllabus in this form. Mobility of land, capital and enterprise has been removed; only the mobility of labour remains (Topic 3, 3.3.4). The labour paragraph is still useful; the capital paragraph is for past-paper practice only.

Labour can be geographically and occupationally mobile. Its geographical mobility (moving between places) is influenced by differences in the cost of housing, family and social ties, and access to information about jobs elsewhere. Geographical mobility is also influenced by size and weight: laptops can be moved easily, while factories, dams and railway lines are fixed in one place. 0455/22/M/J/20 (d) · [8 marks]

Discuss whether or not an ageing population is a benefit to an economy.

Why it may be a benefit. An ageing population means older people form a rising proportion of the population. Older workers often have valuable experience and skills. If they stay in work for longer, their experience can raise productivity and help train younger workers, increasing the quality of the labour force. Also, many older people have built up savings and pensions and spend them on goods and services such as healthcare, travel and leisure. This creates demand for new industries (sometimes called the “silver economy”), which encourages firms to invest and creates jobs. In addition, retired people often care for grandchildren or volunteer, freeing younger family members to work.

Why it may not be a benefit. An ageing population usually means a smaller share of people are of working age, so the dependency ratio rises. Fewer workers must support more dependants, so the labour force may shrink, causing labour shortages that push up wage costs and reduce the economy’s productive capacity, shifting the PPC inwards. Furthermore, the government must spend more on state pensions and healthcare while receiving less income tax from a smaller workforce. This creates pressure to raise taxes, cut other spending such as education or infrastructure, or borrow more, which may reduce incentives to work and harm future economic growth. Older workers may also be less occupationally mobile and less willing to retrain in new technologies.

Conclusion. An ageing population is more likely to be a burden than a benefit if it causes the labour force to shrink quickly while pension and health costs rise. However, it depends on how the economy responds: if the retirement age is raised, older people stay healthy and productive, immigration adds working-age people, and firms adopt labour-saving technology, the costs can be managed and the spending of older people can even boost demand. It also depends on the speed of ageing, since a gradual change gives the government time to plan.

Why this scores
Two developed arguments on each side (experience, new demand; dependency ratio, government finances) and a conclusion that makes a judgement and states what it depends on (retirement age, immigration, technology, speed of change).

Past paper (b) · [4 marks]

Explain how market forces will eliminate a surplus and a shortage.

Surplus. A surplus occurs when the price is above equilibrium, so quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded. Sellers are left with unsold stock, so they lower their price. As the price falls, quantity demanded extends and quantity supplied contracts until the two are equal at the equilibrium price and the surplus disappears.

Shortage. A shortage occurs when the price is below equilibrium, so quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied. Consumers compete for the limited goods and sellers realise they can charge more, so the price rises. The higher price causes quantity demanded to contract and quantity supplied to extend until the market clears at equilibrium.

Why this scores
Defines each disequilibrium, explains the price movement and uses “extension” and “contraction” correctly to show how equilibrium is restored without government intervention.

Why skilled workers are usually paid more.

The supply of skilled workers is limited. Skills such as those of surgeons or engineers take years of education and training to acquire and require particular abilities, so few people can do the job. The supply of labour is low and inelastic, which pushes the wage up.

Skilled workers also tend to have high productivity: they produce more output, or output of greater value, per hour. Firms’ demand for labour is a derived demand, so they are willing to pay a high wage to workers who add a lot of revenue. High demand combined with low supply gives a high equilibrium wage.

Demand for some skills can be low or falling. A skilled craftworker in a declining industry, or a worker whose skill has been replaced by automation, may earn less than an unskilled worker in a booming sector, because the derived demand for their labour has fallen.

Past paper (d) · [8 marks]

Discuss whether all monopolies have low costs of production.

Why monopolies may have low costs.

A monopoly is the only firm supplying its market, so it usually produces on a very large scale. This allows it to benefit from economies of scale: for example, bulk-buying discounts on raw materials (purchasing economies), cheaper borrowing because lenders see it as low risk (financial economies) and the use of specialist, efficient machinery (technical economies). Average total cost (ATC) falls as output rises.

A monopoly can also keep earning high profits because barriers to entry stop new firms entering the market. It can use these profits to fund research and development, leading to new, more efficient production methods that cut costs further. In industries with very high fixed costs, such as a water supply network, one firm supplying the whole market can spread those costs over the largest possible output, giving a lower average total cost than several small firms could achieve.

Why monopolies may not have low costs.

Without competition, a monopoly has little incentive to keep costs down. It can survive while being inefficient, for example by overstaffing, paying managers high salaries or using out-of-date equipment, because customers have no alternative supplier. Rising costs can simply be passed on as higher prices.

A monopoly may also grow so large that it suffers diseconomies of scale: communication becomes slower, management becomes harder to coordinate and workers feel less motivated, so average costs rise. In addition, a monopoly in a small local market (such as the only shop on an island) may not be large enough to gain significant economies of scale at all.

Conclusion. Not all monopolies have low costs. It depends on their size and on whether they face pressure to be efficient: a large monopoly that reinvests profits and faces the threat of regulation or potential competitors is likely to have low costs, whereas a protected monopoly with no competitive pressure, or one that has grown too large, may have high costs.

Why this scores
Names specific economies and diseconomies of scale, uses monopoly concepts (barriers to entry, high profits, lack of competition) and gives a conclusion that directly answers “all”.

Why growth should be the main objective.

Growth allows a firm to benefit from economies of scale. As output increases, average costs fall through bulk buying, specialist machinery and cheaper finance. Lower costs allow the firm to cut prices, compete more strongly and earn higher profits in the long run.

A larger firm also gains market share and becomes more secure. Small firms may deliberately stay small to keep personal control and offer a specialised service.

Analyse [6]

Past paper (c) · [6 marks]

Analyse how a central bank could reduce inflation.

A central bank can use contractionary monetary policy to reduce inflation, mainly by raising the interest rate (and by reducing the money supply).

A higher interest rate increases the cost of borrowing. Households take out fewer loans and mortgages to buy houses, cars and other consumer goods, and people with existing variable-rate mortgages have to make higher repayments, leaving them with less disposable income. A higher interest rate also increases the reward for saving, so households save more and spend less. As a result, consumer expenditure falls.

Firms also face higher borrowing costs, so some investment projects, such as buying new machinery, become unprofitable and investment falls. In addition, higher interest rates may attract foreign financial capital, raising the foreign exchange rate. This makes imports cheaper, reducing imported cost-push inflation, and makes exports dearer, reducing net exports.

With lower consumption, investment and net exports, total demand (aggregate demand) in the economy falls. Firms face less demand for their products, so they are less able to raise prices and may offer discounts to sell stock. Workers also have less bargaining power for wage rises. This reduces demand-pull inflation, so the general price level rises more slowly and the inflation rate falls.

Why this scores
Names the tool, then links it logically through borrowing, saving, consumption, investment and the foreign exchange rate to lower total demand and a lower inflation rate, using precise terms throughout.

Past paper (c) · [6 marks]

Analyse how a central bank could avoid deflation.

Deflation is a sustained fall in the general price level, often caused by weak total demand. A central bank can avoid it by using expansionary monetary policy: cutting the interest rate and increasing the money supply.

A lower interest rate reduces the cost of borrowing. Households are more willing to take out loans and mortgages to buy houses, cars and furniture, and those with existing mortgages pay less each month, leaving more disposable income. Saving earns a lower return, so people are encouraged to spend rather than save. Consumer expenditure therefore increases.

Firms can borrow more cheaply, so more investment projects become profitable and investment rises. Lower interest rates can also cause financial capital to leave the country, lowering the foreign exchange rate. Exports become cheaper abroad and imports become dearer, so net exports rise and imported goods push up the price level.

If the central bank increases the money supply, for example by buying government bonds, banks have more funds to lend, reinforcing the rise in spending. Higher consumption, investment and net exports increase total demand. Firms face stronger demand and can raise prices rather than cut them, so the general price level stops falling and inflation returns to a low positive rate, avoiding deflation.

Why this scores
Defines deflation, identifies expansionary tools and traces them through each component of total demand to a stable or rising price level.

Past paper (c) · [6 marks]

Analyse the advantages of a low rate of inflation.

International competitiveness. If a country’s inflation rate is low compared with its trading partners, its products become relatively cheaper. Demand for its exports rises and domestic consumers buy fewer imports. This improves the current account of the balance of payments and increases output and employment in export industries.

Certainty and investment. Low and stable inflation makes it easier for firms to predict future costs, prices and profits. With less uncertainty, firms are more confident to invest in capital goods, which increases productive capacity and leads to economic growth in the long run.

Protecting purchasing power. With low inflation, the real value of incomes and savings is not eroded quickly. People on fixed incomes, such as pensioners, keep their standard of living, and savers are not penalised, which encourages saving that banks can lend for investment. There are also fewer menu costs and shoe-leather costs, as firms do not need to change prices frequently.

Discuss [8]

Past paper (d) · [8 marks]

Discuss whether or not a fall in a country’s unemployment rate will reduce poverty in that country.

Why it may reduce poverty.

Unemployed people rely on savings or low government benefits, and many live in poverty. When unemployment falls, more people earn a wage, which is usually higher than benefit payments. Their incomes rise, so they can afford necessities such as food, housing and healthcare, and the number of households in poverty falls.

Lower unemployment also helps the government. It pays out less in unemployment benefits and collects more income tax and indirect tax revenue. These funds can be spent on education, healthcare and targeted benefits for the poor, reducing poverty further. Workers who gain jobs also develop skills and experience, raising their future earnings.

Why it may not reduce poverty.

The new jobs may be low-paid, part-time or temporary. Many workers in poverty are already employed (the “working poor”), especially where there is no national minimum wage (NMW) or it is low. If the fall in unemployment is due to more insecure, low-wage jobs, workers may still be unable to afford necessities.

Poverty also affects people who are not in the labour force, such as pensioners, the sick and disabled, and children in large families. A fall in unemployment does not raise their incomes. In addition, unemployment may fall because people have given up looking for work or emigrated rather than because they found jobs. If falling unemployment leads to higher inflation as demand rises, the real incomes of the poor may even fall.

Conclusion. A fall in unemployment is likely to reduce poverty, especially absolute poverty in countries where most poor people are unemployed. However, it depends on the quality and pay of the new jobs, whether there is an adequate NMW, why unemployment fell, and whether the government also supports those outside the labour force.

Why this scores
Two developed points on each side, including the working poor and groups outside the labour force, and a conclusion that states the conditions under which poverty falls.

Past paper (d) · [8 marks]

Discuss whether or not supply-side policy measures will encourage firms to operate in an economy.

Why supply-side measures may attract firms.

Education and training policies raise the skills and productivity of the labour force. Firms can produce more output per worker, lowering their average costs, and they can find the skilled workers they need. A productive, skilled workforce is a strong reason for firms, including multinational companies (MNCs), to locate in a country.

Cutting corporation tax and reducing regulation (deregulation) increases firms’ after-tax profits and lowers the cost and time of starting and running a business. Improving infrastructure, such as roads, ports, power supplies and broadband, reduces transport and production costs. Higher expected profits give firms an incentive to set up and expand.

Why supply-side measures may not attract firms.

Many supply-side measures take a long time to have an effect. A new education programme may take ten years or more to produce skilled workers, and large infrastructure projects take years to build, so firms deciding where to locate now may not be influenced.

Firms consider many other factors as well. If demand in the economy is low because of a recession, firms will not operate there even with low taxes, because they cannot sell their output. Political instability, high crime, a volatile foreign exchange rate or better incentives offered by other countries may outweigh the benefits. Some measures may also have side effects: cutting regulations on the environment or workers’ rights may attract firms but cause pollution and damage sustainability, while tax cuts reduce government revenue for infrastructure.

Conclusion. Supply-side measures are likely to encourage firms to operate in an economy in the long run, because they reduce costs and raise productivity. However, it depends on which measures are used, how quickly they take effect and whether other conditions, such as strong demand, political stability and competitive policies compared with other countries, are also present.

Why this scores
Names specific supply-side measures and links each to firms’ costs and profits, then evaluates using time lags, demand conditions and competing locations, ending with a conditional judgement.

Past paper (d) · [8 marks]

Discuss whether or not the fall in the price of a product is a disadvantage to an economy.

Why it may be a disadvantage.

If the product is a major export, such as oil or copper for a country that depends on it, a fall in its price reduces export revenue (particularly if demand is price inelastic, so quantity sold rises by proportionally less than price falls). This worsens the current account of the balance of payments, lowers producers’ profits and can cause firms to cut output and make workers redundant, increasing unemployment and reducing economic growth. Government tax revenue from the industry also falls.

If the price falls because demand has fallen, for example during a recession, it is a sign of a weak economy. If falling prices spread across many products and become deflation, consumers may delay purchases expecting prices to fall further, reducing total demand, output and employment even more.

Why it may not be a disadvantage.

Consumers benefit: a lower price increases their real income and purchasing power, allowing them to buy more of this and other products, raising living standards. If the product is a necessity such as food or fuel, low-income households gain the most.

If the price falls because supply has increased, for example through new technology or lower costs, output and consumption rise, which is a sign of efficiency. If the product is an important raw material or input, such as oil for an importing country, firms’ costs fall, reducing cost-push inflation, making exports more competitive and encouraging higher output and employment. A fall in the price of a product that causes pollution could, however, increase its consumption and harm the environment.

Conclusion. A fall in the price of a product is not necessarily a disadvantage. It depends on why the price fell (higher supply is usually beneficial, lower demand is not), whether the country is mainly a producer or consumer of the product, how important it is to exports, and whether the fall is limited to one product or part of a general deflation.

Why this scores
Considers producers, consumers, the balance of payments and the government, distinguishes demand-side from supply-side causes of the price fall, and ends with a justified “it depends” judgement.

Analyse [6]

Past paper (c) · [6 marks]

Analyse the economic effects of a reduction in a country’s export of services.

Exports of services, such as tourism, banking, insurance, transport and education sold to foreigners, bring money into the country and are recorded as credits in the trade in services part of the current account. If these exports fall, for example because foreign tourists stop visiting after a rise in the foreign exchange rate or a global recession, less money flows in. If imports stay the same, the current account moves towards a deficit (or a larger deficit).

Spending by foreigners on exports is also part of the total spending on a country’s output, so lower exports reduce total demand (aggregate demand). Hotels, airlines and banks sell less, so they cut output and need fewer workers. This leads to higher unemployment and slower, or even negative, economic growth. The workers who lose their jobs have lower incomes, so they spend less in other local businesses, spreading the fall in demand further through the economy.

With less demand, pressure on prices eases, so inflation may fall. Lower incomes and profits also mean the government collects less tax revenue while paying more in unemployment benefits, which can worsen its budget position. Less demand for the currency from foreigners buying the country’s services may cause the foreign exchange rate to depreciate, which could eventually make exports cheaper and help them recover.

Why this scores
A clear chain from fewer service exports to the current account, then to total demand, output, jobs and growth, with further effects on inflation, government finances and the foreign exchange rate, all in correct economic terms.

Analyse how cleaning up pollution could benefit an economy.

Pollution is an external cost of production and consumption: it harms third parties who are not part of the transaction. Cleaning it up reduces these external costs and moves the economy towards a better allocation of resources, supporting the government aim of environmental sustainability.

Past paper, Section A (h) · [6 marks]

Discuss whether or not an increase in a country’s import tariff on rice would increase the output of its domestic rice farmers.

Why output may increase. A tariff is a tax on imports, so a higher tariff raises the price of imported rice in the domestic market. Imported rice becomes less competitive compared with rice grown by domestic farmers, so consumers switch to domestic rice. Higher demand for domestic rice and a higher market price increase the profitability of rice farming, so domestic farmers have an incentive to expand production, for example by planting more land or using more fertiliser, and output rises. New farmers may also be attracted into the industry.

Why output may not increase. Domestic farmers may be unable to expand quickly because supply of rice is price inelastic in the short run: it takes a growing season to plant and harvest, and suitable land and water may be limited. Output may also be held back by poor weather or a lack of capital. In addition, other countries may retaliate by putting tariffs on this country’s exports; if the farmers export some of their rice, they lose foreign sales. If demand for rice is price inelastic and the tariff is small, consumers may carry on buying imported rice they prefer, such as a particular variety, so demand for domestic rice rises only slightly.

Conclusion. The tariff is likely to increase domestic output over time, but by how much depends on the size of the tariff, how easily domestic farmers can increase supply, and whether imported and domestic rice are close substitutes. Output will rise most in the long run, when farmers have had time to expand.

Why this scores
A developed argument for (higher import price → switch to domestic rice → higher profits → more output) and developed arguments against (inelastic supply, retaliation, weak substitution), with a conclusion naming what it depends on.

Discuss whether or not an increase in a country’s population will benefit its economy.

Why it may benefit the economy. A larger population, especially one with more people of working age, increases the size of the labour force. Discuss [8]

Past paper (d) · [8 marks]

Discuss whether or not an increase in government subsidies will reduce a deficit on the current account of the balance of payments.

Why it may reduce the deficit. A current account deficit occurs when the money leaving a country to pay for imports of goods and services, and as primary and secondary income, is greater than the money coming in from exports and from income received from abroad. A subsidy is a payment from the government to producers, which lowers their costs of production. If subsidies are given to exporting firms, they can lower the prices of their products abroad. This makes exports more price competitive, so foreign demand rises and export revenue increases, as long as demand is price elastic. Higher export earnings reduce the current account deficit.

Subsidies to domestic firms that compete with imports, such as local food producers, allow them to cut their prices. Domestic consumers then switch from imported goods to cheaper domestically produced goods, so spending on imports falls, again reducing the deficit. In the longer run, subsidies for training, research or new technology can raise productivity and quality, making the country’s firms more internationally competitive.

Why it may not reduce the deficit. If demand for the country’s exports is price inelastic, for example because foreign buyers value brand or quality more than price, a small price cut will raise the quantity sold only slightly, and export revenue may even fall. Firms may also keep the subsidy as extra profit rather than lowering prices. Moreover, subsidies must be financed. If the government pays for them by raising taxes or cutting other spending, or by borrowing, other parts of the economy may be harmed. Other countries may regard export subsidies as unfair and retaliate with tariffs on this country’s goods, reducing exports.

Also, if subsidised firms expand production, they may need more imported raw materials and components, so imports rise. And if the subsidy increases incomes in the economy, households may spend more on imported goods. The deficit may also be caused by factors subsidies do not address, such as large outflows of profits to foreign-owned firms or a high foreign exchange rate.

Conclusion. Subsidies can reduce a current account deficit if they are targeted at exporters and import-competing firms with price elastic demand, and if the price reductions are passed on to buyers. They are less likely to work when demand is inelastic, production relies on imported inputs, or trading partners retaliate. On balance, subsidies are more effective as a long-term supply-side measure that raises productivity than as a quick fix, and the result depends on how they are targeted and financed.

Why this scores
Two developed arguments for (cheaper exports; switching from imports) and at least two against (inelastic demand, retaliation, imported inputs, opportunity cost of financing), finished with a judgement about targeting, elasticity and time.

Past paper (d) · [8 marks]

Discuss whether or not countries specialising benefits consumers.

Why specialisation may benefit consumers. When countries specialise, they concentrate on producing the goods and services they are best at, for example because of their climate, natural resources or skilled workforce, and trade for the rest. Producing on a larger scale allows firms to gain economies of scale and to become more efficient, so average costs fall. These lower costs can be passed on to consumers as lower prices, raising their real incomes and allowing them to buy more goods and services.

Specialisation and trade also give consumers greater choice and quality. Consumers can buy products their own country cannot make, or cannot make well, such as tropical fruit in a cold country or advanced electronics in a country without the technology. Competition from specialist foreign producers pressures domestic firms to improve quality and innovate, which also benefits consumers.

Why specialisation may not benefit consumers. A country that relies on others for key products becomes dependent on imports. If supply is disrupted, for example by war, a natural disaster, a pandemic or export restrictions in the supplying country, consumers may face shortages and sharp price rises, as happened with some food and energy products in recent years. Transport costs and tariffs may also offset the cost advantage. In addition, over-specialisation can harm consumers as workers: if world demand for the country’s main export falls, or a cheaper producer appears abroad, workers in that industry lose their jobs and incomes. Their occupational immobility can lead to long-term unemployment, reducing their ability to buy goods and services.

Specialisation may also lead to a few large global firms dominating markets. If they gain monopoly power, they may raise prices and restrict choice rather than passing lower costs on. Transporting goods over long distances also increases pollution and CO2 emissions, an external cost that consumers bear as members of society.

Conclusion. Specialisation generally benefits consumers through lower prices and wider choice, and this is the main reason countries trade. However, the benefit depends on whether cost savings are passed on through competition, how reliable supply chains are, and how diversified the economy is. A country that specialises but keeps several sources of supply and several export industries is most likely to gain without exposing its consumers to excessive risk.

Why this scores
Developed benefits (economies of scale → lower prices; more choice and quality) balanced against developed drawbacks (dependence and supply disruption, unemployment of consumers as workers, monopoly power), with a conclusion stating the conditions for benefit.
