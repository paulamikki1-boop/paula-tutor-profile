Exam Technique: How to Score Full Marks

Knowing the economics is only half the job. This section shows you how Paper 2 is built, what each command word is really asking for, and how to turn what you know into full-mark answers under time pressure.

The papers you will sit (2027-2029)

Paper 1
40 multiple choice questions · 40 marks
Paper 2
Structured questions · 80 marks · 2 hours
Section A
One compulsory data-response question in six parts · 20 marks
Section B
Answer three from four questions · 20 marks each · 60 marks
Each Section B question
(a) 2 · (b) 4 · (c) 6 · (d) 8 marks
Tiers
None: everyone sits the same papers
  • Section A gives you a short case study about a real or realistic country, with text, figures and often a table or chart. The six parts usually start with quick retrieval or calculation from the data and build up to longer analysis and evaluation.
  • Section B questions are each based on a short stimulus sentence and then test the whole syllabus. The four parts rise in difficulty: define, explain, analyse, discuss.
  • The marks in square brackets tell you how much to write. A 2-mark answer is two precise lines; an 8-mark answer is roughly a side of writing.

What each command word needs

Define2A precise textbook meaning using the key words the mark scheme looks for. An example can secure the second mark.One or two sentences

Explain4Give reasons and show why or how. Usually two points, each identified (1) and developed (1).Two short paragraphs

Discuss8Balanced arguments on both sides, each developed, then a justified conclusion.Four developed points and a conclusion
Examiner Tip
Match the depth to the command word. Writing a full page for a 2-mark “Define” wastes minutes you need for the 8-mark “Discuss”, and a one-sided list of points for a “Discuss” question can never reach the top level however many points it has.

How levels-based marking works

Short questions (2 and 4 marks) are point-marked: each correct, relevant point earns a mark. The Analyse [6] and Discuss [8] questions are marked in levels. The examiner reads the whole answer, decides which level description it best fits, and then places it within that level.

LevelAnalyse [6] looks likeDiscuss [8] looks like
TopA clear, complete chain of reasoning; accurate economic terms; diagram correct, labelled and explained where required.Developed arguments on both sides and a conclusion that makes a judgement and says what it depends on.
MiddleSome correct links but the chain has gaps, or a diagram is drawn but not explained.Good analysis of one side, or both sides covered only briefly; a conclusion that simply repeats earlier points.
BottomRelevant points listed with little or no explanation.Assertions and lists without reasoning; no real evaluation.
  • Depth beats breadth. Two fully developed chains score more than six undeveloped points.
  • Evaluation means weighing up: “this depends on…”, “in the short run… but in the long run…”, “this is more likely if…”.
  • A justified conclusion answers the question directly (yes, no, or it depends) and gives the key condition. It should add something new, not repeat the essay.

The chain-of-reasoning technique

Almost every mark above the 2-mark questions is earned by linking ideas. Use connectives such as which means that, this leads to, as a result and therefore to force yourself to write each step.

1
CauseState the change clearly in economic terms, e.g. “The central bank raises the rate of interest.”
2
First effectWhat happens directly: “Borrowing becomes more expensive and saving more rewarding.”
3
Second effectHow behaviour changes: “Households spend less on credit-financed goods and firms postpone investment, so total (aggregate) demand falls.”
4
OutcomeLink back to the question: “With less spending, firms face less pressure to raise prices, so the inflation rate falls.”
5
Evaluate (Discuss only)Add a condition or counter-effect: “However, this may also reduce output and raise unemployment, and the effect takes time.”
Model answer

Explain why a fall in income tax may increase consumer spending. [4]

A cut in income tax increases households’ disposable income (1), which means they have more money left after tax to spend on goods and services, so consumption rises (1). It may also raise the reward from working, encouraging some people to work more hours or to join the labour force (1); their higher earned income then allows them to spend more (1).

Using the case data in Section A

  • Read the questions first, then the case, so you know what to look for. Underline figures and key phrases as you go.
  • Quote the data when a question says “using the information” or “refer to the data”. Name the figure and year: “Unemployment rose from 4% in 2024 to 7% in 2026.” Without data you cap your mark.
  • Describe trends accurately: rose, fell, fluctuated, rose at a slower rate. Remember that a falling inflation rate still means prices are rising, just more slowly.
  • Calculations: write the formula, substitute, and give units. Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
  • Use the context in explanations and discussions: if the country relies on tourism or oil exports, say so and apply it. Context-free textbook answers lose marks.
  • Do not just copy the text. Take the facts and add the economics (the “why”).

Drawing and labelling diagrams

If a question says “using a … diagram”, the diagram is part of the mark scheme. An unlabelled or wrongly drawn diagram cannot score. Draw it in pencil, large (at least a third of a page), with a ruler.

PPC checklist

  • Label both axes with two named goods or types of good (e.g. Capital goods, Consumer goods) and mark the origin 0.
  • Draw the curve concave (bowed out) to the origin, touching both axes. Label it PPC, and a new curve PPC1.
  • Show a movement along the curve for reallocation (opportunity cost), a point inside for unemployment, and a shift for a change in the quantity or quality of resources.
  • Use arrows to show the direction of a shift and refer to the diagram in your writing: “as shown by the shift from PPC to PPC1…”.

Demand and supply checklist

  • Axes: Price (vertical) and Quantity (horizontal). Demand slopes down, supply slopes up.
  • Label every curve (D, S, D1 or S1) and mark the old and new equilibrium price and quantity (P, Q, P1, Q1) with dashed lines to the axes.
  • Only shift the curve that the question’s change affects: a change in a non-price determinant of demand shifts D; a change in costs of production shifts S. A change in the product’s own price causes a movement along, not a shift.
  • In the text, explain the diagram: what shifted, why, the direction, and the effect on price and quantity.

Timing: making 2 hours fit 80 marks

You have 120 minutes for 80 marks, so allow roughly 1½ minutes per mark. Think of it as about a minute of writing per mark plus time to read, plan and check.

Common mistakes

Common Mistake
Writing one-sided answers to “Discuss” questions. “Discuss whether or not…” always needs both “whether” and “whether not”, plus a conclusion.
Common Mistake
Stating without explaining: “Higher interest rates reduce inflation.” is only an assertion. The marks are in the steps in between (borrowing costs → spending → demand → prices).
Common Mistake
Confusing a shift of a curve with a movement along it, e.g. saying a rise in price “shifts demand to the left”. A price change causes a contraction or extension of demand.
  • Vague terms: “the economy gets better” instead of naming the aim (economic growth, lower unemployment, stable prices, balance of payments stability, redistribution of income, environmental sustainability).
  • Mixing up similar terms: inflation and prices, income and wealth, exports and imports, the rate of change and the level.
  • Ignoring the context word in the question: a question about “a developing economy” or “a firm” wants answers applied to that case.
  • Forgetting units in calculations or copying the case data without interpreting it.
  • Leaving a part blank. A sensible attempt at a definition or one developed point can still earn marks.
Examiner Tip
Before writing a Discuss conclusion, ask: “What single factor would change my answer?” That factor (the size of the change, the time period, the elasticity, the state of the economy, how the money is spent) is your “it depends on”.

  • I can quote and interpret case data, including trends and percentage changes, in Section A.
  • I can draw fully labelled PPC and demand and supply diagrams and explain what they show.

Topic 1 · Basic Economic Problem

The Basic Economic Problem

Why every economy must make choices: scarcity, the three basic questions, the factors of production, opportunity cost and the production possibility curve. These ideas are the vocabulary for the whole course and turn up in almost every Paper 1 and Paper 2 question.

The nature of the basic economic problem

The basic economic problem is that resources are finite (limited) while wants are infinite (unlimited). Because of this, there is scarcity, and choices have to be made.

  • Economic goods are scarce: they take resources to make and therefore have an opportunity cost (e.g. a laptop, a bus journey, a doctor’s time).
  • Free goods are not scarce: there is enough to satisfy everyone without using up resources, so the opportunity cost is zero (e.g. air to breathe, sunlight, sea water in the ocean).
  • Scarcity affects everyone: consumers (limited income), workers (limited time and skills), producers (limited capital and raw materials) and governments (limited tax revenue).
  • Scarcity is also an environmental issue: fresh water, fertile land, fish stocks and a stable climate are finite. Using them up now reduces what is available to future generations.

The three basic economic questions

Because resources are scarce, every economy — whether market, planned or mixed — has to answer three questions:

1
What to produce?Which goods and services, and in what quantities? E.g. more hospitals or more roads; solar panels or coal power stations.
2
How to produce?Which combination of factors of production and which methods? E.g. labour-intensive hand-picking or capital-intensive machinery; fossil-fuel energy or renewables.
3
For whom to produce?Who gets the output? In a market system it goes to those with the income to pay; a government may allocate some goods according to need (e.g. free healthcare).
Examiner Tip
If a question asks you to define the basic economic problem, you need both halves: limited resources AND unlimited wants, leading to scarcity and the need to make choices. Saying only “resources are scarce” usually earns just one of the two marks.

The factors of production

Factors of production are the resources used to produce goods and services. Each has a reward (the income its owner receives).

Common Mistake
Capital in economics is not money. Money is a way of buying factors; capital is the physical, human-made equipment itself (machines, buildings, tools). Also, investment means spending on capital goods, not buying shares.

Mobility of the factors of production

Factor mobility is the ease with which a factor can move between uses. There are two kinds:

  • Geographical mobility: moving from one place to another.
  • Occupational mobility: moving from one job or use to another.
FactorGeographical mobilityOccupational mobility
LandImmobile — land cannot physically moveFairly mobile — a field can switch from wheat to a solar farm or housing (subject to planning rules)

CapitalSome is mobile (laptops, vans); some is fixed (a steel works, an oil rig)General capital (computers) is mobile; specialised capital (a car assembly robot) is not

Low factor mobility matters because resources get stuck in declining industries, causing unemployment and wasted output. Governments can raise mobility with training schemes, relocation grants and job-vacancy information.

An increase in the quantity or quality of resources raises the productive capacity of the economy (see PPC shifts below).

Opportunity cost

Opportunity cost is the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.

  • It exists because of scarcity: using resources one way means they cannot be used another way.
  • It is the single next best alternative, not every alternative given up.

Opportunity cost affects decisions of all economic agents

  • Consumers: spending $60 on a concert ticket means not buying new trainers.
  • Workers: taking a full-time job may mean giving up the chance to go to university (or vice versa).
  • Producers: a farmer who plants maize on a field gives up the soya beans that could have been grown there.
  • Governments: spending tax revenue on a new motorway may mean fewer resources for flood defences or schools.
Model answer
Explain, using an example, why a government decision involves an opportunity cost. [4]
A government has limited tax revenue but unlimited demands on its spending, so it faces scarcity and must choose (1). If it decides to spend $2 billion on building a new airport (1), those same funds and resources cannot be used for other purposes (1). The opportunity cost is the next best alternative forgone, for example the new hospitals that could have been built with that money instead (1).

Production possibility curve (PPC)

A production possibility curve shows the maximum combinations of two goods (or types of goods) that an economy can produce when all its resources are fully and efficiently used, with a given state of technology.

What the PPC illustrates

  • Scarcity: combinations beyond the curve cannot be reached with current resources.
  • Choice: the economy must pick one point on (or inside) the curve.
  • Opportunity cost: moving along the curve to produce more of one good means giving up some of the other.
PositionMeaning
On the curve (A, D)Productively efficient: all resources fully employed; more of one good requires less of the other
Inside the curve (B)Inefficient: unemployed or under-used resources (e.g. during a recession). More of both goods could be produced with no opportunity cost
Outside the curve (C)Unattainable with current resources and technology; could only be reached if the PPC shifts outwards

Why the PPC is usually concave (bowed out) to the origin

Resources are not equally suited to producing both goods. As an economy specialises more in one good, it has to transfer resources that are less and less suited to it, so the opportunity cost of each extra unit rises. A straight-line PPC would mean a constant opportunity cost (resources equally good at both).

Movements along the PPC

  • A movement along the curve (A → D) shows a reallocation of resources from one good to another.
  • From A to D: more consumer goods, fewer capital goods. The fall in capital goods is the opportunity cost of the extra consumer goods.
  • A movement from inside (B) towards the curve shows previously unemployed resources being used — the economy recovers from a recession, and there is no opportunity cost.

Shifts of the PPC

Outward shift (PPC → right)Inward shift (PPC → left)
Increase in quantity of resources: population growth, immigration, discovery of new minerals, investment in capitalLoss of resources: war, natural disaster, emigration of skilled workers, capital wearing out without replacement
Increase in quality of resources: education and training, better healthcare, new technologyFall in quality: disease reducing labour productivity, soil degradation, depletion of fish stocks, climate-related damage to farmland
  • If only the technology for producing one good improves (e.g. a new crop variety), the PPC pivots outwards along that good’s axis only.
  • Choosing more capital goods today (lower current consumption) tends to shift the PPC further outwards in the future — a classic opportunity-cost trade-off between now and later.

Chain of reasoning: government spending on education

1

2

3

Common Mistake
Do not say a fall in unemployment shifts the PPC outwards. Putting unemployed workers to work moves the economy from a point inside the curve towards the curve. The PPC only shifts when the quantity or quality of resources (or technology) changes.

Topic 2 · Allocation of Resources

The Allocation of Resources

How markets use prices to answer the what, how and for whom questions, why markets sometimes fail, and how governments intervene. This is the largest topic on the syllabus: demand and supply diagrams, elasticity and market failure feature in nearly every Paper 2 and a big share of Paper 1.

Microeconomics and macroeconomics

MicroeconomicsMacroeconomics
FocusIndividual markets and decision makersThe economy as a whole
Decision makersHouseholds (consumers), workers, firms (producers)Governments, and the total of all households and firms
ExamplesPrice of coffee, wages of nurses, a firm’s outputInflation, unemployment, economic growth, balance of payments

The role of markets in allocating resources

A market is any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together to trade. The market system allocates scarce resources through the price mechanism: the interaction of demand and supply determines prices, and prices act as signals.

  • What: firms produce the goods consumers are willing and able to buy at a profitable price.
  • How: firms choose the lowest-cost combination of factors to maximise profit.
  • For whom: output goes to those willing and able to pay.

Rising prices signal producers to move resources into a market; falling prices signal them to move resources out.

Demand

Demand is the willingness and ability to buy a good or service at a given price over a given period of time. Individual demand is one consumer’s demand; market demand is the sum of all individual demands at each price.

  • The law of demand: as price falls, quantity demanded rises (and vice versa), ceteris paribus. The demand curve slopes down from left to right.
  • A change in the good’s own price causes a movement along the demand curve: an extension (price falls, quantity demanded rises) or a contraction (price rises, quantity demanded falls).
  • A change in any other factor causes a shift of the demand curve.

Causes of a shift in demand

Population Income Related goods Advertising Tastes Expectations State (taxes, benefits) — “PIRATES”
  • Income: higher income raises demand for most goods; income tax cuts raise disposable income.
  • Price of substitutes: a rise in the price of petrol cars increases demand for electric cars.
  • Price of complements: a rise in the price of games consoles reduces demand for games.
  • Tastes and fashion, advertising: growing environmental awareness raises demand for reusable bottles.
  • Population: size and age structure (an ageing population raises demand for healthcare).
  • Expectations of future prices, and interest rates (cheaper borrowing raises demand for houses and cars).

Supply

Supply is the willingness and ability of producers to sell a good or service at a given price over a given period of time. Market supply is the sum of all individual firms’ supply.

  • As price rises, quantity supplied rises, because higher prices make production more profitable. The supply curve slopes up from left to right.
  • A change in own price: extension or contraction along the curve. Other factors: a shift.

Causes of a shift in supply

  • Costs of production: higher wages, raw material or energy costs shift supply left.
  • Technology: more productive methods shift supply right.
  • Indirect taxes (shift left) and subsidies (shift right).
  • Weather and natural events: droughts and floods linked to climate change shift the supply of crops left.
  • Number of firms in the market, and prices of other goods the firm could produce instead.
Common Mistake
Never say “price rises, so demand falls, so the demand curve shifts left”. A change in the good’s own price causes a movement along the curve (a change in quantity demanded). Only non-price factors shift the curve (a change in demand).

Price determination and price changes

  • Market equilibrium: the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. There is no tendency to change.
  • Disequilibrium: if price is above equilibrium there is excess supply (a surplus), which pushes price down; if price is below equilibrium there is excess demand (a shortage), which pushes price up.

Price elasticity of demand (PED)

Price elasticity of demand is the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price.

PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price   (% change = change ÷ original × 100)

Value (ignoring the minus sign)DescriptionMeaning
Greater than 1Price elasticQuantity demanded changes by a larger % than price
Less than 1Price inelasticQuantity demanded changes by a smaller % than price
Equal to 1UnitarySame % change; total spending unchanged
0Perfectly inelasticVertical demand curve; quantity does not change
InfinityPerfectly elasticHorizontal demand curve; any price rise loses all sales

Determinants of PED

  • Number and closeness of substitutes: more substitutes → more elastic (one brand of bottled water is elastic; tap water in general is inelastic).
  • Proportion of income spent on the good: a large share (cars, holidays) → more elastic.
  • Necessity or luxury; habit-forming goods (e.g. cigarettes) → inelastic.
  • Time period: demand is more elastic in the long run as consumers find alternatives.

PED, total revenue and decisions

Total revenue (consumer expenditure) = price × quantity sold.

  • Inelastic demand: raising price increases revenue; cutting price reduces it.
  • Elastic demand: cutting price increases revenue; raising price reduces it.
  • Firms use PED to set prices; governments tax goods with inelastic demand (fuel, tobacco) because revenue is high and reliable, but the tax will reduce consumption only a little.
Worked calculation
The price of a bus pass rises from $40 to $50 and monthly sales fall from 2000 to 1800. % change in price = 10 ÷ 40 × 100 = +25%. % change in quantity = −200 ÷ 2000 × 100 = −10%. PED = −10 ÷ 25 = −0.4, so demand is price inelastic. Revenue rises from $80 000 to $90 000.

Price elasticity of supply (PES)

Price elasticity of supply is the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price.

PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price (always positive). PES > 1 is elastic, < 1 inelastic, 0 perfectly inelastic (vertical), infinity perfectly elastic (horizontal).

Determinants of PES

  • Time period: supply is more elastic in the long run (new factories, new crops planted).
  • Spare capacity and stocks: firms with unused machinery or stored goods can raise output quickly.
  • Mobility of factors: if resources can switch easily between uses, supply is elastic.
  • Production time: agricultural goods (months to grow) have inelastic supply in the short run.

Why it matters: when supply is inelastic, a rise in demand mainly raises price rather than output (e.g. city-centre housing).

Examiner Tip
For PED and PES, always interpret your number: state whether it is elastic or inelastic and what that means (“quantity demanded changes proportionately less than price”). Many candidates calculate correctly but lose the interpretation mark. Remember PED is usually negative; PES is positive.

The market economic system

A market economy allocates resources through the price mechanism, with private ownership of resources and minimal government involvement.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Consumer choice and responsiveness to consumer wantsInequality of income and wealth; goods go to those who can pay
Profit motive encourages efficiency and innovationPublic goods may not be provided at all
Resources allocated quickly through price signals, without costly planningMerit goods under-consumed, demerit goods over-consumed
Competition can keep prices low and quality highExternal costs such as pollution ignored; risk of monopoly power

Market failure

Market failure occurs when the market system leads to an inefficient allocation of resources, i.e. too much or too little of a good is produced and consumed.

Public good
Non-excludable and non-rival, e.g. street lighting, flood defences, national defence. Free-rider problem → not provided by market
Merit good
More beneficial than consumers realise; under-consumed, e.g. education, vaccinations
Demerit good
More harmful than consumers realise; over-consumed, e.g. cigarettes, sugary drinks
Private goods
Excludable and rival, e.g. a sandwich

Private costs, external costs and social costs

  • Private costs: costs paid by the consumer or producer involved (e.g. a factory’s fuel bill).
  • External costs: costs imposed on third parties not involved in the transaction (e.g. air pollution, CO2 emissions causing climate change, noise from an airport).
  • Social cost = private cost + external cost.
  • Private benefits: benefits to the consumer or producer. External benefits: benefits to third parties (e.g. vaccination protects others; a new park improves neighbours’ wellbeing).
  • Social benefit = private benefit + external benefit.
  • Markets ignore externalities, so goods with external costs are over-produced and goods with external benefits are under-produced.

Other causes of market failure: information failure, monopoly power (higher prices, restricted output), factor immobility, and the depletion of non-renewable resources (e.g. over-fishing, deforestation) which threatens environmental sustainability.

Common Mistake
A public good is not simply “a good provided by the government”. Education and healthcare are provided by governments but are merit goods (people can be excluded and they are rival). A public good must be non-excludable and non-rival.

The mixed economic system

A mixed economy combines the market system with government intervention: both the private and public sectors own resources and make allocation decisions. Governments intervene to correct market failure and reduce inequality.

Indirect taxes

  • A tax on spending, e.g. VAT/GST, excise duties on fuel, tobacco, sugary drinks; carbon taxes on emissions.
  • It raises firms’ costs, shifting supply to the left: price rises and quantity falls.
  • Aims: reduce consumption of demerit goods, make polluters pay for external costs and promote environmental sustainability, and raise government revenue.
  • Effectiveness depends on PED: with inelastic demand, consumers bear most of the tax and consumption falls only a little. Risks: smuggling, regressive effect on low-income households.

Subsidies

  • A payment from the government to producers (or consumers) to reduce costs, e.g. subsidies for solar panels, public transport, electric vehicles.
  • Supply shifts to the right: price falls and quantity rises.
  • Aims: increase consumption of merit goods and goods with external benefits, support incomes in key industries, and speed the switch to low-carbon energy.
  • Drawbacks: opportunity cost of government spending; firms may become inefficient and dependent on support.

Other forms of intervention

  • Regulation: laws and rules, e.g. bans on smoking in public places, emissions limits for cars, compulsory schooling, banning single-use plastics. Can be effective but needs costly monitoring; fines may be too low to deter.
  • State provision: the government directly provides public goods (defence, street lighting) and merit goods (free schools, hospitals), financed by taxation. Opportunity cost and possible inefficiency.
  • Nationalisation: transferring a private firm or industry into public (government) ownership, e.g. to protect jobs, run natural monopolies such as water or rail in the public interest, or secure key services.
  • Privatisation: transferring a firm or industry from the public sector to the private sector, aiming for greater efficiency through the profit motive and competition, and raising revenue for the government. Risks: private monopoly, job losses, neglect of unprofitable but socially valuable services.
Model answer
Explain how an indirect tax on petrol could reduce an external cost. [4]
Burning petrol creates an external cost, such as air pollution and CO2 emissions that harm third parties (1). An indirect tax raises the costs of sellers, shifting the supply curve to the left (1). This increases the price of petrol, causing a contraction in quantity demanded (1). With less petrol consumed, fewer emissions are produced, so the external cost falls and resources are allocated closer to the socially efficient level (1). However, as demand for petrol is price inelastic, the fall in consumption may be small.
Examiner Tip
For 8-mark “discuss whether” questions on intervention, give a developed argument for, a developed argument against, and a justified conclusion that depends on something specific: the PED of the product, the size of the tax or subsidy, the cost of enforcement, or whether the government has better information than the market.

Topic 3 · Microeconomic Decision-Makers

Microeconomic Decision-Makers

This topic looks at the individual players in an economy: banks, households, workers, trade unions and firms, and how each makes choices. It is the largest topic on the syllabus and supplies many Paper 2 Section B questions, especially on wages, growth of firms and cost/revenue calculations.

Money and banking

Money is anything that is generally accepted as a means of payment for goods and services and settling debts. Barter (swapping goods directly) needs a double coincidence of wants, which money removes.

Functions of money

  • Medium of exchange — used to buy and sell, removing the need for barter.
  • Unit of account (measure of value) — allows prices of different goods to be compared.
  • Store of value — can be saved and used later (weakened by inflation).
  • Standard of deferred payment — allows borrowing now and repaying later.

Characteristics of good money

Acceptable, durable, portable, divisible, homogeneous (uniform), limited in supply and difficult to forge.

Workers

Factors affecting choice of occupation

  • Wage factors: basic pay, overtime, bonuses, commission.
  • Non-wage factors: job security, working hours and holidays, promotion prospects, training, working conditions, fringe benefits (company car, pension, health insurance), job satisfaction, location, flexibility such as remote working, and the employer’s environmental or ethical record.

Wage determination

In a free labour market the wage rate is set where the demand for labour (from firms) equals the supply of labour (from workers).

  • Demand for labour is a derived demand: it depends on demand for the product the workers make. It also depends on labour productivity and the cost of capital that could replace workers.
  • Supply of labour to an occupation depends on the wage, non-wage factors, qualifications and training needed, and the size of the working population.
  • Wages can also be influenced by trade unions, governments (national minimum wage, public-sector pay) and employer bargaining power.

Reasons for differences in earnings

  • Skills, qualifications and training: long training restricts supply (surgeons vs cleaners).
  • Productivity: more productive workers are worth more to firms.
  • Demand for the product: workers in growing industries (e.g. renewable energy engineers) earn more.
  • Unpleasant or dangerous work may earn compensating extra pay.
  • Trade union strength and government policy (minimum wages, public-sector pay scales).
  • Public vs private sector, skilled vs unskilled, agricultural vs manufacturing vs services.
  • Male vs female: discrimination, career breaks, concentration in lower-paid occupations, fewer hours worked.
  • Non-wage benefits: some jobs pay less because they offer high job satisfaction or security.

Division of labour / specialisation

Division of labour is when a production process is split into separate tasks, with each worker specialising in one task.

Trade unions

A trade union is an organisation of workers that aims to protect and improve the interests of its members.

  • Role: negotiate higher wages (collective bargaining), better working conditions, job security, training, and represent members in disputes; lobby government on issues such as the minimum wage.
  • Industrial action: strikes, work-to-rule, overtime bans, go-slows.

Factors that make a union strong

High membership; members hard to replace (skilled); product demand is price inelastic so higher wages can be passed on in prices; strike fund; public support; favourable laws; firm is profitable.

Firms

Classification of firms

  • By sector: primary (extracting natural resources: farming, fishing, mining); secondary (manufacturing and construction); tertiary (services: banking, retail, tourism). As economies develop, the relative importance usually shifts from primary to secondary to tertiary.
  • Private sector firms are owned by individuals/shareholders and usually aim for profit. Public sector organisations are owned by the government and often aim to provide a service.
  • By size: measured by number of employees, capital employed, sales revenue or market share.

Small firms

  • Why they survive: small market size or niche markets; personal service; flexibility; local knowledge; low start-up costs; government support; they may supply larger firms; owners may not want to grow.
  • Challenges: difficulty raising finance, higher average costs (no economies of scale), competition from large firms, reliance on few customers.

Firms and production

  • Production is the process of converting factors of production (inputs) into goods and services (output).
  • Demand for factors of production is derived demand. It depends on demand for the product, the price of each factor, the productivity of each factor, and the availability and cost of substitutes (e.g. machines for workers).
  • Labour-intensive production uses a high proportion of labour relative to capital (hairdressing, handmade crafts, tea picking). Capital-intensive production uses a high proportion of capital (car assembly, oil refining).
  • Firms choose capital-intensive methods when labour is expensive, output is large and standardised, and technology is available; labour-intensive methods suit personal services, small outputs and low-wage economies.

Worked example
A bakery pays rent of $600 a week. Each loaf costs $0.80 in ingredients and energy and sells for $2.00. It sells 1,000 loaves.
  • VC = 1,000 × $0.80 = $800; TC = $600 + $800 = $1,400

Objectives of firms

  • Survival — especially for new firms or in a recession.
  • Profit maximisation — the traditional aim of private firms; rewards owners and funds investment.
  • Growth — larger size brings economies of scale and security.
  • Sales/revenue maximisation or market share — may be preferred by managers.
  • Social welfare / corporate social responsibility — e.g. cutting carbon emissions, ethical sourcing; public-sector firms often aim to provide an affordable service.

Explain why a surgeon usually earns more than a shop assistant. [4]
A surgeon needs many years of training and high qualifications, so few people are able to enter the occupation and the supply of surgeons is low. Topic 4 · Government and the Macroeconomy

Government and the Macroeconomy

This topic covers what governments try to achieve for the whole economy, the policies they use (fiscal, monetary and supply-side), and the three big macroeconomic problems: slow growth, unemployment and inflation. Expect data-response questions using GDP, unemployment and CPI figures, and 8-mark “discuss” questions on which policy works best.

The role of government

  • At a local level: providing services such as refuse collection, local roads and schools.
  • At a national level: providing public goods (defence, justice), merit goods (education, healthcare), welfare benefits, regulation of markets, and managing the macroeconomy.
  • At an international level: trade agreements, membership of international bodies, cooperation on climate change and financial stability.
  • The government is also a producer (state-owned enterprises) and an employer.

Macroeconomic aims of government

Economic growth
Sustained rise in real GDP
Full employment / low unemployment
Everyone willing and able to work has a job
Stable prices / low inflation
Often a target of around 2% a year
Balance of payments stability
Avoid large, persistent current account deficits or surpluses
Redistribution of income
Reduce inequality between rich and poor
Environmental sustainability
Meet present needs without damaging future generations’ ability to meet theirs

Environmental sustainability as an aim includes cutting carbon emissions, protecting natural resources, reducing pollution and encouraging renewable energy, so that growth can continue in the long run.

Possible conflicts between aims

  • Growth vs stable prices: rapid growth in demand can cause demand-pull inflation.
  • Full employment vs stable prices: as unemployment falls, workers can bid up wages, causing cost-push inflation.
  • Growth vs balance of payments: higher incomes raise spending on imports, worsening the current account.
  • Growth vs environmental sustainability: more output often means more pollution and resource depletion.
  • Redistribution vs growth: very high taxes on the rich may reduce incentives to work and invest.
Examiner Tip
Conflicts are not inevitable. Supply-side policy can raise growth and reduce inflation together, and investment in green technology can support both growth and sustainability. Mentioning this earns evaluation marks.

Fiscal policy

Fiscal policy is the use of government spending and taxation to influence aggregate demand and the economy.

The government budget

  • Budget deficit: government spending > tax revenue (financed by borrowing, adding to national debt).
  • Budget surplus: tax revenue > government spending. Balanced budget: the two are equal.
  • Areas of government spending: healthcare, education, defence, infrastructure, welfare benefits and pensions, debt interest.
  • Reasons for spending: provide public and merit goods, support the poor, improve infrastructure, influence the economy.

Reasons for taxation

  • Raise revenue to finance government spending.
  • Redistribute income (progressive taxes).
  • Discourage demerit goods (tobacco, sugary drinks, alcohol).
  • Promote environmental sustainability: carbon taxes, fuel duty and landfill taxes make polluters pay for external costs.
  • Protect domestic industries (tariffs) and manage aggregate demand.

Defining a progressive tax as one where “the rich pay more”. Under a proportional tax the rich also pay a larger amount. A progressive tax takes a higher proportion (percentage) of income as income rises.

Qualities of a good tax (canons of taxation)

  • Equitable: based on ability to pay.
  • Certain: taxpayers know how much, when and how to pay.
  • Convenient: easy and timely to pay.
  • Economical / efficient: cheap to collect relative to revenue raised.
  • Flexible: can be changed easily when conditions change.
  • Does not distort incentives to work, save or invest unnecessarily.

Impact of taxation

Taxes affect consumers (lower disposable income, higher prices), producers (higher costs, lower profits), the government (revenue) and the economy (output, employment, price level, inequality, and pollution in the case of environmental taxes).

Monetary policy is the use of interest rates, the money supply and the exchange rate, usually by the central bank, to influence aggregate demand.

  • Contractionary monetary policy: raise interest rates, reduce the money supply, allow the currency to appreciate.

Supply-side policy

Supply-side policies aim to increase the productive capacity of the economy (aggregate supply) by improving the quantity and quality of factors of production and making markets work more efficiently.

  • Education and training — raise labour productivity and occupational mobility.
  • Improving healthcare — a healthier, more productive workforce.
  • Infrastructure investment — transport, broadband, renewable energy grids cut firms’ costs.
  • Lower direct taxes — stronger incentives to work, invest and start businesses.
  • Deregulation and privatisation — increase competition and efficiency.
  • Labour market reforms — e.g. reducing trade union power or reforming benefits to encourage work.
  • Subsidies for research and development, including green technology.

Evaluation: supply-side policies can boost growth without inflation and improve competitiveness, but they are costly, take a long time to work, and some (deregulation, benefit cuts) can increase inequality or harm the environment.

Economic growth

  • Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the total value of output produced in a country in a year.
  • Economic growth is an increase in real GDP. Real GDP is adjusted for inflation; nominal GDP is at current prices.
  • GDP per head = real GDP ÷ population; a better guide to average living standards.

Causes of recession

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level over time.

Causes of inflation

  • Demand-pull inflation: aggregate demand grows faster than the economy’s ability to supply (“too much money chasing too few goods”). Caused by tax cuts, low interest rates, rising confidence, export booms.
  • Cost-push inflation: rising costs of production are passed on as higher prices. Caused by higher wages, rising raw material or energy prices, a fall in the exchange rate raising import costs, or higher indirect taxes.

Consequences of inflation

  • Falls in real incomes for those on fixed incomes (pensioners, benefit recipients) whose incomes do not keep pace.
  • Savers lose if the interest rate is below inflation; borrowers gain as the real value of debt falls.
  • Menu costs (changing price lists) and shoe-leather costs (time spent searching for best prices or moving money).
  • Uncertainty discourages investment; exports become less price competitive, worsening the current account.
  • Fiscal drag: if tax bands are not raised, people drift into higher tax bands.

Causes and consequences of deflation

  • Causes: falling aggregate demand (bad deflation) or falling costs from productivity gains and new technology (good deflation).
  • Consequences: consumers delay purchases expecting lower prices → demand falls → firms cut output and jobs → further falls in demand (a deflationary spiral); the real burden of debt rises; however, exports may become more competitive.

Policies to control inflation

  • Demand-pull: contractionary fiscal policy (higher taxes, lower spending) and contractionary monetary policy (higher interest rates).
  • Cost-push: supply-side policies to raise productivity; a stronger exchange rate to cut import costs; cutting indirect taxes.
  • Deflation: expansionary fiscal and monetary policy to boost demand.
Model answer

A cut in income tax increases households’ disposable income. With more disposable income, consumers spend more on goods and services, increasing aggregate demand. Firms respond to higher demand by increasing output, and because labour is a derived demand they need to hire more workers. This reduces cyclical unemployment. A lower tax rate may also increase the incentive to work, encouraging people to take up jobs.
Examiner Tip
Always match the policy to the cause. Higher interest rates suit demand-pull inflation but can make cost-push inflation worse for output and jobs. Retraining solves structural, not cyclical, unemployment. Naming the type or cause first makes your evaluation precise.
Common Mistake
Thinking that a fall in the inflation rate means prices are falling. If inflation falls from 6% to 3%, prices are still rising, just more slowly. Only negative inflation (deflation) means the price level is falling.

Topic 5 · Economic Development

Economic Development

How we measure and compare living standards, why poverty exists and how it can be reduced, how populations change, and why some countries are far more developed than others. Paper 2 often asks you to use data on GDP per head, HDI or population to compare two countries, so precise definitions and clear chains of reasoning are essential.

1. Living standards

Living standards describe the quality of life and material well-being of people in a country. Economic development is a broader idea than economic growth: it means an improvement in people’s welfare, including income, health, education and freedom of choice.

Real GDP per head

  • Real GDP per head (per capita) = real GDP ÷ population. It shows the average output (and income) per person, adjusted for inflation.
  • “Real” removes the effect of price changes, so a rise shows more goods and services, not just higher prices.
  • “Per head” allows for population size, so a large country is not automatically ranked above a small one.

Limitations of real GDP per head as a measure of living standards

  • It is an average — it hides the distribution of income. A few very rich people can raise the average while most people remain poor.
  • Informal (hidden) economy — unrecorded activity (cash-in-hand work, illegal trade) is excluded, so GDP may understate true output, especially in low-income countries.
  • Non-marketed output — subsistence farming, housework and voluntary work are not counted.
  • Quality of life factors are ignored — working hours, leisure time, pollution, crime, political freedom and stress.
  • Environmental damage — output from deforestation or heavy CO2 emissions raises GDP but reduces sustainability and future welfare.
  • Composition of output — high spending on defence adds to GDP but may not improve living standards as much as spending on schools or hospitals.
  • International comparisons — figures must be converted into one currency; exchange rates may not reflect differences in the cost of living.

The HDI is a composite measure of living standards published by the United Nations. It combines three dimensions into a single value between 0 and 1 (closer to 1 = more developed).

Examiner Tip

2. Poverty

  • Absolute poverty: a situation where people do not have enough income to meet their basic needs — food, clean water, shelter, clothing and basic healthcare. It is often measured using an international poverty line (an income per day, in purchasing-power terms).
  • Relative poverty: a situation where people are poor compared with others in the same society, often defined as having an income below a certain percentage (e.g. 50% or 60%) of the median income. They can meet basic needs but cannot afford the standard of living most people enjoy.
Common Mistake
Saying relative poverty only exists in poor countries. Relative poverty exists in every country, including the richest, because it is measured against the country’s own average. Absolute poverty can be eliminated in theory; relative poverty is much harder to remove entirely while incomes are unequal.

Causes of poverty

  • Unemployment — no earned income; may depend on low state benefits.
  • Low wages — low-skilled, low-productivity jobs; workers in the informal sector with no protection.
  • Illness, disability and old age — limited ability to work; high medical costs.
  • Lack of education and training — limits access to better-paid jobs.
  • Large family size / high dependency — income shared among more people.
  • Poor infrastructure and lack of access to finance — hard to start businesses or reach markets.
  • Natural disasters, climate change and conflict — destroy crops, homes and capital.
  • Inflation — reduces the real value of low, fixed incomes.
  • Unequal distribution of wealth and land; corruption diverting public funds.

Optimum population

  • The optimum population is the size of population that, combined with the country’s other resources and current technology, maximises output (real GDP) per head.

  • The optimum is not fixed: improvements in technology, capital and education raise it over time.
Model answer
Explain two reasons why an ageing population may reduce a country’s economic growth. (4 marks)
First, an ageing population means a smaller proportion of people are of working age. The labour force shrinks, so the country has fewer workers to produce goods and services, reducing its productive capacity and real GDP. Second, the government has to spend more on state pensions and healthcare for the elderly. This may require higher taxes on workers and firms, reducing incentives to work and invest, or it may mean less government spending on infrastructure and education, which lowers future growth.

International Trade and Globalisation

Why countries specialise and trade, the role of multinational companies, the arguments for free trade and for protection, how exchange rates are set and why they change, and what the current account of the balance of payments shows. This topic is a favourite for data-response questions using trade and exchange rate figures.

1. International specialisation

International specialisation occurs when countries concentrate on producing the goods and services they are relatively best at producing (lowest opportunity cost), and trade to obtain others. It is based on differences in factor endowments: climate, natural resources, labour skills, capital and technology.

  • Examples: Saudi Arabia (oil), Bangladesh (garments), Switzerland (financial services, pharmaceuticals), New Zealand (dairy).
  • Specialisation also happens at regional level within a country (e.g. a region known for car manufacturing, wine, tourism or technology firms).

2. Globalisation and multinational companies

Globalisation is the process by which the world’s economies become increasingly integrated and interdependent through trade in goods and services, flows of capital, movement of labour, and the spread of technology and ideas.

Causes of globalisation

  • Cheaper and faster transport (containerisation, air freight).
  • Improvements in communication technology and the internet.
  • Reduction in trade barriers (trade agreements, the World Trade Organization).
  • Growth of multinational companies and freer movement of capital.

3. Free trade and protection

Free trade is international trade without government restrictions (no tariffs, quotas or other barriers). Protectionism is the use of trade restrictions to protect domestic industries from foreign competition.

Benefits of free trade

  • Lower prices for consumers as the most efficient producers supply the market.
  • Greater choice and variety of products.
  • Larger markets for domestic firms, allowing economies of scale.
  • Increased competition encourages efficiency, innovation and quality.
  • Access to raw materials and capital goods not available at home.
  • Higher world output and living standards through specialisation.

Reasons for protection

  • Protect infant industries — new firms need time to grow and gain economies of scale before facing established foreign rivals.
  • Protect declining industries / jobs — to avoid sudden structural unemployment and give time to adjust.
  • Prevent dumping — foreign firms selling at a price below cost to drive out domestic competitors.
  • Protect strategic industries — food, energy and defence, for national security.
  • Improve the current account — reduce spending on imports.
  • Raise government revenue from tariffs.
  • Promote environmental sustainability — e.g. tariffs on goods made with high carbon emissions, bans on imports of illegally logged timber or endangered species products, standards requiring energy efficiency; this protects the environment and prevents firms in countries with strict rules being undercut by polluters.
  • Protect consumers from unsafe or low-quality products.

Consequences of protection

  • Higher prices and less choice for consumers.
  • Domestic firms face less competition, so may become inefficient and less innovative.
  • Retaliation — other countries impose their own barriers, hurting exporters (a trade war).
  • Higher costs for firms that use imported raw materials and components.
  • World output falls as countries lose the gains from specialisation.
  • On the positive side: jobs may be saved in the short run and infant industries given a chance to grow.

Confusing a tariff with a quota. A tariff is a tax (it raises price and earns government revenue); a quota is a quantity limit. Also, a subsidy protects by lowering domestic firms’ costs — it does not directly make imports more expensive.

4. Foreign exchange rates

The exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another (e.g. £1 = US$1.30). The foreign exchange market is where currencies are bought and sold.

Determination in the foreign exchange market (floating rate)

  • Demand for a currency comes from foreigners buying the country’s exports, foreign tourists visiting, foreign investors buying assets or saving in its banks, and speculators expecting it to rise.
  • Supply of a currency comes from residents buying imports, travelling abroad, investing overseas, and speculators expecting it to fall.
  • The equilibrium exchange rate is where demand for the currency equals supply.

Causes of exchange rate fluctuations

  • Changes in demand for exports / imports — e.g. higher export sales raise demand for the currency (appreciation).
  • Changes in interest rates — higher interest rates attract foreign savings (“hot money”), raising demand for the currency.
  • Relative inflation rates — higher inflation makes exports less competitive, reducing demand for the currency and increasing supply (as residents buy more imports) → depreciation.
  • Foreign direct investment flowing in or out.
  • Speculation about future movements.
  • Government / central bank intervention — buying or selling the currency using foreign currency reserves.

Model answer
Explain how a rise in a country’s inflation rate could cause a current account deficit. (4 marks)
If the country’s inflation rate rises above that of its trading partners, its goods and services become relatively more expensive. Foreign buyers switch to cheaper products from other countries, so the value of the country’s exports falls. At the same time, domestic consumers find imports relatively cheaper than domestically produced goods, so spending on imports rises. With export revenue falling and import spending rising, debits exceed credits, causing a deficit on the trade in goods and services and so on the current account.

Define [2]

0455/22/F/M/22 (a) · [2 marks]

Define the economic problem.

The economic problem is the situation in which there are unlimited wants but only limited (finite) resources to satisfy them, which creates scarcity and means choices have to be made.

0455/22/O/N/23 (a) · [2 marks]

Define, with an example, a capital good.

A capital good is a man-made good that is used to produce other goods and services, rather than being consumed for its own sake. For example, a delivery van used by a bakery or a tractor used on a farm.

Why this scores
One mark for a precise definition (man-made aid to production) and one for a valid example. Avoid money or shares as examples: they are financial capital, not capital goods.

Explain [4]

0455/22/O/N/22 (c) · [4 marks]

Explain the economic problem and why it is always likely to exist.

The economic problem is that people’s wants are unlimited, while the resources available to satisfy them (land, labour, capital and enterprise) are limited. This creates scarcity, so societies must decide what to produce, how to produce it and for whom.

It is always likely to exist because human wants keep growing: as incomes rise and new products are invented, people want more and better goods and services, such as newer phones or foreign holidays. At the same time, resources remain finite, and some, such as oil and minerals, are non-renewable and are being used up. Even if an economy grows, wants grow too, so there will never be enough resources to satisfy all wants and choices will always have to be made.

Why this scores
Two marks for explaining the problem (unlimited wants, limited resources, scarcity) and two for explaining why it persists (wants continually increase; resources remain finite or run out).
0455/21/M/J/25 (b) · [4 marks]

Explain two causes of a decrease in enterprise in an economy.

First, higher taxes on profits (corporation tax) reduce the reward for risk-taking. If entrepreneurs keep a smaller share of any profit they make, fewer people will think it worth risking their savings to start or expand a business, so the amount of enterprise falls.

Second, a recession or economic uncertainty makes it more likely that new businesses will fail. When demand is falling and the future is unclear, potential entrepreneurs expect lower sales and profits, and banks become less willing to lend to them, so fewer new firms are set up. Emigration of skilled, ambitious people is another cause, since it removes potential entrepreneurs from the economy.

Why this scores
Each cause is identified (1) and then developed by linking it to the reward from, or risk of, enterprise (1). Two developed causes give 4 marks.
0455/22/M/J/23 (c) · [4 marks]

Explain one reason why the quantity of land may increase and one reason why the quality of land may increase.

Quantity: land reclamation, such as draining marshland or reclaiming land from the sea, creates new usable land. Similarly, the discovery of new natural resources, such as a new oil or gas field, increases the quantity of land (natural resources) available for production.

Quality: the use of fertilisers, irrigation and better farming techniques can make existing soil more productive, so each hectare of land yields a larger output. Reducing pollution or reversing soil erosion, for example by replanting trees, would also improve the quality of land over time.

Why this scores
One mark for each reason identified and one for each explanation of how it increases the amount or productivity of land. Keeping quantity and quality clearly separate avoids confusion.
0455/22/F/M/20 (b) · [4 marks]

Explain how opportunity cost is different for economic goods and free goods.

Opportunity cost is the next best alternative given up when a choice is made. An economic good is produced using scarce resources, so producing or buying it has an opportunity cost: the resources used to make a car could have been used to make something else, and the money a consumer spends on it could have bought another product.

A free good, such as air or sunlight, is not scarce: it is available in unlimited quantity and needs no resources to produce. Using more of it does not mean giving anything else up, so a free good has zero opportunity cost.

Why this scores
Marks for defining or applying opportunity cost, explaining that economic goods use scarce resources so have an opportunity cost, and explaining that free goods are not scarce so have none.
0455/22/F/M/21 (b) · [4 marks]

Explain the difference between a point inside a PPC and a point outside a PPC.

A point inside a PPC shows that the economy is not using all of its resources, or not using them efficiently. There is spare capacity, such as unemployed workers or idle machinery, so output could be increased without giving up any of the other good.

A point outside a PPC shows a combination of goods that the economy cannot currently produce with its existing resources and technology. It is unattainable at present and could only be reached if the PPC shifted outwards, through more or better-quality resources, or if the country obtained the goods through trade.

Why this scores
Two marks for inside (unemployed or inefficiently used resources) and two for outside (unattainable with current resources; needs economic growth).

Analyse [6]

0455/22/O/N/23 (c) · [6 marks]

Analyse, using a production possibility curve (PPC) diagram, the effect of a decrease in the size of a country’s labour force on its economy.

Labour is a factor of production, so a fall in the size of the labour force, for example because of emigration or an ageing population, reduces the quantity of resources available to the economy. With fewer workers, the maximum output the country can produce of both capital goods and consumer goods falls. This is shown by the PPC shifting inwards from PPC to PPC1, and the economy moving from a point such as A to B.

This means the country’s productive capacity has fallen, so potential economic growth is negative. Combinations that were previously attainable, such as A, are now outside the new curve. Fewer goods and services can be produced, which may reduce living standards unless output per worker rises. Firms may face labour shortages, which push up wage costs and prices, and the government may receive less income tax revenue while facing continued spending demands. The economy may also have to import more to meet domestic demand, worsening its current account.

Why this scores
A correctly labelled diagram with an inward shift, a clear link from fewer workers to fewer factors of production to lower maximum output, and developed consequences (lower productive capacity, living standards, costs) reach the top level.
0455/22/F/M/20 (c) · [6 marks]

Analyse, using a production possibility curve (PPC) diagram, the effect of an increase in enterprise on an economy.

Enterprise is the factor of production that organises land, labour and capital and takes the risks of production. An increase in enterprise means more people are willing to set up and run businesses. New firms bring previously idle resources into use and combine factors in new, more efficient ways, and entrepreneurs often introduce new products and technology. This increases both the quantity and the productivity of resources used, so the economy’s productive capacity rises. The PPC shifts outwards from PPC to PPC1, allowing a move from A to B, where more of both capital and consumer goods can be produced.

As a result, the economy experiences economic growth. Higher output creates more jobs, so unemployment may fall, and higher incomes can raise living standards. Competition from new firms can also lower prices and improve quality for consumers, and the government gains more tax revenue from higher profits and incomes, which it can spend on education or healthcare. Innovative firms may also sell abroad, increasing exports.

Why this scores
The answer defines the role of enterprise, links it to more and better use of resources, shows an outward PPC shift that is labelled and referred to, and develops outcomes (growth, jobs, living standards, tax revenue).
0455/23/M/J/20 (c) · [6 marks]

Analyse the influences on the mobility of two factors of production.

Labour can be geographically and occupationally mobile. Its geographical mobility (moving between places) is influenced by differences in the cost of housing, family and social ties, and access to information about jobs elsewhere. If housing in the area with vacancies is much more expensive, a worker may be unable to move there, so unemployment in one region can exist alongside labour shortages in another. Its occupational mobility (moving between jobs) depends on skills, qualifications and training. A coal miner who loses his job may lack the skills to become a software engineer, so he stays unemployed unless he retrains. Government training schemes and better education therefore increase labour mobility and help resources move to where they are most needed.

Capital mobility depends on the type of capital. Some capital is occupationally mobile: a computer or a delivery van can be used in many different industries. Other capital is specialised, such as a car-assembly robot or an oil rig, and cannot easily be switched to other uses, so it has low occupational mobility. Geographical mobility is also influenced by size and weight: laptops can be moved easily, while factories, dams and railway lines are fixed in one place. When capital is immobile, it may stand idle if demand for what it produces falls, leaving the economy producing inside its PPC.

Why this scores
Two factors are clearly identified, each with influences on both geographical and occupational mobility, and the answer analyses the consequence (unemployment, idle resources, inefficiency) rather than just listing influences.

Discuss whether or not an ageing population is a benefit to an economy.

Why it may be a benefit. An ageing population means older people form a rising proportion of the population. Older workers often have valuable experience and skills. If they stay in work for longer, their experience can raise productivity and help train younger workers, increasing the quality of the labour force. Also, many older people have built up savings and pensions and spend them on goods and services such as healthcare, travel and leisure. This creates demand for new industries (sometimes called the “silver economy”), which encourages firms to invest and creates jobs. In addition, retired people often care for grandchildren or volunteer, freeing younger family members to work.

Why it may not be a benefit. An ageing population usually means a smaller share of people are of working age, so the dependency ratio rises. Fewer workers must support more dependants, so the labour force may shrink, causing labour shortages that push up wage costs and reduce the economy’s productive capacity, shifting the PPC inwards. Furthermore, the government must spend more on state pensions and healthcare while receiving less income tax from a smaller workforce. This creates pressure to raise taxes, cut other spending such as education or infrastructure, or borrow more, which may reduce incentives to work and harm future economic growth. Older workers may also be less occupationally mobile and less willing to retrain in new technologies.

Conclusion. An ageing population is more likely to be a burden than a benefit if it causes the labour force to shrink quickly while pension and health costs rise. However, it depends on how the economy responds: if the retirement age is raised, older people stay healthy and productive, immigration adds working-age people, and firms adopt labour-saving technology, the costs can be managed and the spending of older people can even boost demand. It also depends on the speed of ageing, since a gradual change gives the government time to plan.

Why this scores
Two developed arguments on each side (experience, new demand; dependency ratio, government finances) and a conclusion that makes a judgement and states what it depends on (retirement age, immigration, technology, speed of change).

Explain [4]

Past paper (b) · [4 marks]

Explain two reasons why the price of sugar may fall.

Increase in supply. A good harvest, caused by favourable weather or better fertilisers, raises the quantity of sugar cane produced at every price. The supply curve shifts to the right, so at the old price there is a surplus of sugar. Sellers cut the price to clear the excess stock, so the equilibrium price falls.

Decrease in demand. Health campaigns warning about obesity and diabetes, or a sugar tax on soft drinks, change tastes away from sugary products. Demand for sugar falls at every price, shifting the demand curve to the left. This creates a surplus at the original price, and competition between sellers pushes the price down.

Why this scores
Two separate causes (one supply-side, one demand-side), each identified and then developed by explaining the shift and how the resulting surplus lowers price.
Past paper (b) · [4 marks]

Explain two causes of a shift to the right in the demand curve for fish.

A rise in the price of a substitute such as meat. If beef and chicken become more expensive, fish becomes relatively cheaper. Some consumers switch from meat to fish, so more fish is demanded at every price and the demand curve shifts right.

A change in tastes in favour of fish. If reports show that eating oily fish is good for heart health, or consumers switch away from meat for environmental reasons, fish becomes more desirable. People are willing and able to buy more fish at each price, so demand increases and the curve shifts right.

(Other creditworthy causes: a rise in incomes, as fish is a normal good; a fall in the price of a complement such as rice or chips; a rise in population; successful advertising.)

Why this scores
Each cause is a non-price determinant of demand (a change in price would cause a movement along the curve, not a shift) and each is explained in terms of more being demanded at every price.
Past paper (b) · [4 marks]

Explain what effect more firms producing tyres would have on the PED of individual firms’ tyres.

More firms producing tyres would make the demand for each individual firm’s tyres more price elastic. With more producers, consumers have a greater number of close substitutes to choose from. If one firm raises its price, buyers can easily switch to a rival’s similar tyres, so the firm would lose a large proportion of its sales. The percentage change in quantity demanded would be greater than the percentage change in price, so PED would rise (in absolute value) above 1. As a result, each firm has less power to raise prices and would be under pressure to keep prices low or to differentiate its tyres through branding or quality.

Why this scores
States the direction of change (more elastic), gives the reason (more substitutes), develops it (consumers switch, large fall in quantity demanded) and links it to the PED value and the firm’s pricing power.
Past paper (b) · [4 marks]

Explain how market forces will eliminate a surplus and a shortage.

Surplus. A surplus occurs when the price is above equilibrium, so quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded. Sellers are left with unsold stock, so they lower their price. As the price falls, quantity demanded extends and quantity supplied contracts until the two are equal at the equilibrium price and the surplus disappears.

Shortage. A shortage occurs when the price is below equilibrium, so quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied. Consumers compete for the limited goods and sellers realise they can charge more, so the price rises. The higher price causes quantity demanded to contract and quantity supplied to extend until the market clears at equilibrium.

Why this scores
Defines each disequilibrium, explains the price movement and uses “extension” and “contraction” correctly to show how equilibrium is restored without government intervention.
Past paper (b) · [4 marks]

Explain two ways a firm could increase the productivity of its workers.

Training. The firm could provide on-the-job or off-the-job training. This improves workers’ skills and knowledge, so they work faster, make fewer mistakes and can operate more advanced equipment. Output per worker per hour therefore rises.

Investing in better capital. The firm could buy more advanced machinery or technology, such as automated production lines or faster computers. Each worker then has more and better capital to work with, which allows them to produce more output in the same time, raising labour productivity.

(Other valid ways: incentive schemes such as piece rates or bonuses, improving working conditions and motivation, better management and organisation of tasks through division of labour.)

Why this scores
Two distinct methods, each linked explicitly to higher output per worker, which is the definition of labour productivity.

Past paper (c) · [6 marks]

Analyse why an actor may decide to become a teacher.

Workers choose an occupation by weighing up its wage and non-wage factors.

Wage factors. Most actors earn low and irregular incomes: they are paid per role and may spend long periods with no work. There is a large supply of people wanting to act relative to demand, which keeps the typical wage low. Teachers, by contrast, usually receive a regular salary on a pay scale, often with annual increments and a pension. An actor who wants a higher and more predictable income may therefore switch to teaching, because the expected lifetime earnings are greater and allow them to plan borrowing, such as a mortgage.

Non-wage factors. Teaching offers greater job security, since there is continuous demand for teachers and posts are often permanent contracts, whereas acting jobs are temporary. Teachers usually have long holidays and regular hours rather than evening and weekend performances and touring away from home, which can suit someone with a family. Teaching can also offer job satisfaction from helping young people and clear promotion prospects, for example to head of department.

Transferable skills. An actor already has skills in communication, confidence and presenting to an audience, so the cost of retraining (for example, a teaching qualification) may be relatively low, making the move easier. Occupational mobility is higher when skills overlap. Overall, if the combined wage and non-wage benefits of teaching exceed those of acting, the actor will change occupation.

Why this scores
Uses the economic framework of wage and non-wage factors, compares both jobs rather than describing one, and develops each point into why it would change the actor’s choice, with a link to occupational mobility.

Past paper (d) · [8 marks]

Discuss whether or not demand for coffee is likely to rise in the future.

Why demand may rise.

Incomes are rising in many developing and emerging economies, such as China and India. Coffee, especially café coffee, is a normal good, so as real incomes grow consumers can afford to buy more of it at every price. The demand curve shifts to the right.

Tastes are also changing. Coffee-shop culture is spreading, and advertising by large chains and social media promotes coffee as a lifestyle product. Population growth in countries with a young, urban population also adds more potential consumers. Both increase the number of people willing and able to buy coffee.

Why demand may not rise.

Health concerns about too much caffeine could shift tastes towards substitutes such as herbal tea, fruit juice or water, reducing demand for coffee. If the price of a close substitute such as tea falls, some consumers will switch away from coffee.

Climate change may reduce the supply of coffee beans, as coffee plants are sensitive to rising temperatures and droughts. This would raise the price of coffee, causing a contraction in quantity demanded (a movement along the curve). A recession would also reduce incomes and cut spending on non-essential purchases such as café coffee.

Conclusion. Demand for coffee is likely to rise overall, because the growth in incomes and population in emerging economies is a long-term trend that outweighs health concerns in most markets. However, it depends on the country: in high-income countries where consumption is already high, demand may be stable or fall as tastes shift, and it also depends on whether a future recession cuts incomes.

Why this scores
Two developed arguments on each side using determinants of demand, a clear distinction between a shift in demand and a contraction caused by a price rise, and a conclusion that makes a judgement and says what it depends on.
Past paper (d) · [8 marks]

Discuss whether or not skilled workers are always paid more than unskilled workers.

Why skilled workers are usually paid more.

The supply of skilled workers is limited. Skills such as those of surgeons or engineers take years of education and training to acquire and require particular abilities, so few people can do the job. The supply of labour is low and inelastic, which pushes the wage up.

Skilled workers also tend to have high productivity: they produce more output, or output of greater value, per hour. Firms’ demand for labour is a derived demand, so they are willing to pay a high wage to workers who add a lot of revenue. High demand combined with low supply gives a high equilibrium wage.

Why skilled workers are not always paid more.

Demand for some skills can be low or falling. A skilled craftworker in a declining industry, or a worker whose skill has been replaced by automation, may earn less than an unskilled worker in a booming sector, because the derived demand for their labour has fallen.

Wages also depend on bargaining power and government. Unskilled workers in an industry with a strong trade union may win higher pay than non-unionised skilled workers, and a national minimum wage can raise unskilled pay. In some public-sector jobs, such as nursing or teaching, skilled workers are paid by government pay scales that may be lower than the wages of unskilled workers doing dangerous or unpleasant work, such as offshore oil labourers who receive compensation for poor conditions. Some skilled workers also accept lower pay for non-wage benefits, such as job satisfaction.

Conclusion. Skilled workers are usually, but not always, paid more. It depends on the demand for the skill relative to its supply: a skill that is in short supply and highly valued by employers earns a high wage, but where demand for a skill is low, or unskilled workers have strong unions, dangerous conditions or minimum wage protection, the gap can narrow or reverse.

Why this scores
Uses demand and supply of labour on both sides, brings in trade unions, government, compensating differentials and non-wage factors, and the word “always” is directly challenged in a reasoned conclusion.
Past paper (d) · [8 marks]

Discuss whether all monopolies have low costs of production.

Why monopolies may have low costs.

A monopoly is the single or dominant supplier in its market, so it usually produces on a very large scale. This allows it to benefit from economies of scale: for example, bulk-buying discounts on raw materials (purchasing economies), cheaper borrowing because lenders see it as low risk (financial economies) and the use of specialist, efficient machinery (technical economies). Average costs fall as output rises.

A monopoly can also earn supernormal profits in the long run because barriers to entry stop new firms entering. It can use these profits to fund research and development, leading to new, more efficient production methods that cut costs further. Natural monopolies, such as water supply networks, have very high fixed costs, so one firm supplying the whole market has the lowest average cost.

Why monopolies may not have low costs.

Without competition, a monopoly has little incentive to keep costs down. It can survive while being inefficient, for example by overstaffing, paying managers high salaries or using out-of-date equipment, because customers have no alternative supplier. Rising costs can simply be passed on as higher prices.

A monopoly may also grow so large that it suffers diseconomies of scale: communication becomes slower, management becomes harder to coordinate and workers feel less motivated, so average costs rise. In addition, a monopoly in a small local market (such as the only shop on an island) may not be large enough to gain significant economies of scale at all.

Conclusion. Not all monopolies have low costs. It depends on their size and on whether they face pressure to be efficient: a large monopoly that reinvests profits and faces the threat of regulation or potential competitors is likely to have low costs, whereas a protected monopoly with no competitive pressure, or one that has grown too large, may have high costs.

Why this scores
Names specific economies and diseconomies of scale, uses monopoly concepts (barriers to entry, supernormal profit, lack of competition) and gives a conclusion that directly answers “all”.
Past paper (d) · [8 marks]

Discuss whether or not a firm should have growth as its main objective.

Why growth should be the main objective.

Growth allows a firm to benefit from economies of scale. As output increases, average costs fall through bulk buying, specialist machinery and cheaper finance. Lower costs allow the firm to cut prices, compete more strongly and earn higher profits in the long run.

A larger firm also gains market share and becomes more secure. It is less likely to be taken over or forced out of business, it can spread risk by diversifying into different products and markets, and it has more power when negotiating with suppliers. Managers may also gain status and higher salaries from running a larger firm.

Why growth should not be the main objective.

Chasing growth can reduce profits in the short run. Expanding requires heavy spending on new premises, machinery and marketing, and the firm may cut prices to win sales. Shareholders usually want profit maximisation, so they may be unhappy if dividends fall. If the firm grows too large, it may suffer diseconomies of scale, such as poor communication and low worker motivation, pushing average costs up.

Other objectives may be more important depending on the situation. A new firm or one in a recession may need survival as its main aim, keeping costs low and cash available. Some firms aim for social welfare or environmental sustainability, for example reducing carbon emissions, which rapid expansion could undermine. Small firms may deliberately stay small to keep personal control and offer a specialised service.

Conclusion. Growth is a sensible main objective for an established, financially strong firm in a growing market where economies of scale are significant. However, it depends on the firm’s stage and circumstances: for a new firm or one facing a downturn, survival or profit should come first, and growth is only worthwhile if it does not lead to diseconomies of scale or conflict with owners’ aims.

Why this scores
Covers the main alternative objectives from the syllabus (survival, growth, profit maximisation, social welfare), develops each point with a consequence, and reaches a conditional judgement.

Past paper (d) · [4 marks]

Explain how government spending on training may increase tax revenue in the long run.

Government spending on training improves the skills of the labour force, which raises labour productivity and occupational mobility. Better-trained workers are more attractive to employers, so unemployed people are more likely to find jobs, and existing workers can move into higher-skilled, better-paid occupations.

More people in work and higher wages mean higher incomes, so the government collects more income tax. Past paper (b) · [4 marks]

Explain two reasons why governments redistribute income.

To reduce poverty. In a market economy some people, such as the unemployed, the sick and the elderly, earn little or no income and cannot afford necessities such as food, housing and healthcare. By taxing high earners progressively and paying benefits to low-income households, the government raises the living standards of the poorest.

A large gap between rich and poor can cause social tension and crime and may reduce equality of opportunity, since children in poor households may receive less education. Analyse [6]

Past paper (c) · [6 marks]

Analyse how a central bank could reduce inflation.

A central bank can use contractionary monetary policy to reduce inflation, mainly by raising the interest rate (and by reducing the money supply).

A higher interest rate increases the cost of borrowing. Households take out fewer loans and mortgages to buy houses, cars and other consumer goods, and people with existing variable-rate mortgages have to make higher repayments, leaving them with less disposable income. A higher interest rate also increases the reward for saving, so households save more and spend less. As a result, consumer expenditure falls.

Firms also face higher borrowing costs, so some investment projects, such as buying new machinery, become unprofitable and investment falls. In addition, higher interest rates may attract foreign financial capital, raising the exchange rate. This makes imports cheaper, reducing imported cost-push inflation, and makes exports dearer, reducing net exports.

With lower consumption, investment and net exports, total (aggregate) demand in the economy falls. Firms face less demand for their products, so they are less able to raise prices and may offer discounts to sell stock. Workers also have less bargaining power for wage rises. This reduces demand-pull inflation, so the general price level rises more slowly and the inflation rate falls.

Why this scores
Names the tool, then links it logically through borrowing, saving, consumption, investment and the exchange rate to lower aggregate demand and a lower inflation rate, using precise terms throughout.
Past paper (c) · [6 marks]

Analyse how a central bank could avoid deflation.

Deflation is a sustained fall in the general price level, often caused by weak aggregate demand. A central bank can avoid it by using expansionary monetary policy: cutting the interest rate and increasing the money supply.

A lower interest rate reduces the cost of borrowing. Households are more willing to take out loans and mortgages to buy houses, cars and furniture, and those with existing mortgages pay less each month, leaving more disposable income. Saving earns a lower return, so people are encouraged to spend rather than save. Consumer expenditure therefore increases.

Firms can borrow more cheaply, so more investment projects become profitable and investment rises. Lower interest rates can also cause financial capital to leave the country, lowering the exchange rate. Exports become cheaper abroad and imports become dearer, so net exports rise and imported goods push up the price level.

If the central bank increases the money supply, for example by buying government bonds, banks have more funds to lend, reinforcing the rise in spending. Higher consumption, investment and net exports increase aggregate demand. Firms face stronger demand and can raise prices rather than cut them, so the general price level stops falling and inflation returns to a low positive rate, avoiding deflation.

Why this scores
Defines deflation, identifies expansionary tools and traces them through each component of aggregate demand to a stable or rising price level.
Past paper (c) · [6 marks]

Analyse the advantages of a low rate of inflation.

International competitiveness. If a country’s inflation rate is low compared with its trading partners, its products become relatively cheaper. Demand for its exports rises and domestic consumers buy fewer imports. This improves the current account of the balance of payments and increases output and employment in export industries.

Certainty and investment. Low and stable inflation makes it easier for firms to predict future costs, prices and profits. With less uncertainty, firms are more confident to invest in capital goods, which increases productive capacity and leads to economic growth in the long run.

Protecting purchasing power. With low inflation, the real value of incomes and savings is not eroded quickly. People on fixed incomes, such as pensioners, keep their standard of living, and savers are not penalised, which encourages saving that banks can lend for investment. There are also fewer menu costs and shoe-leather costs, as firms do not need to change prices frequently.

Advantage over zero or negative inflation. A low positive rate, rather than deflation, means consumers do not delay purchases in expectation of lower prices, and firms’ revenues grow steadily. A small rise in prices can encourage firms to produce more, supporting employment.

Discuss whether or not a fall in a country’s unemployment rate will reduce poverty in that country.

Past paper (c) · [6 marks]

Analyse the economic effects of a reduction in a country’s export of services.

Exports of services, such as tourism, banking, insurance, transport and education sold to foreigners, bring money into the country and are recorded as credits in the trade in services part of the current account. If these exports fall, for example because foreign tourists stop visiting after a rise in the exchange rate or a global recession, less money flows in. If imports stay the same, the current account moves towards a deficit (or a larger deficit).

Exports are also an injection of spending into the economy, so lower exports reduce aggregate (total) demand. Hotels, airlines and banks sell less, so they cut output and need fewer workers. This leads to higher unemployment and slower, or even negative, economic growth. The workers who lose their jobs have lower incomes, so they spend less in other local businesses, spreading the fall in demand further through the economy.

With less demand, pressure on prices eases, so inflation may fall. Lower incomes and profits also mean the government collects less tax revenue while paying more in unemployment benefits, which can worsen its budget position. Less demand for the currency from foreigners buying the country’s services may cause the exchange rate to depreciate, which could eventually make exports cheaper and help them recover.

Why this scores
A clear chain from fewer service exports to the current account, then to aggregate demand, output, jobs and growth, with further effects on inflation, government finances and the exchange rate, all in correct economic terms.
Past paper (c) · [6 marks]

Analyse how cleaning up pollution could benefit an economy.

Pollution is an external cost of production and consumption: it harms third parties who are not part of the transaction. Cleaning it up reduces these external costs and moves the economy towards the socially optimum use of resources, supporting the government aim of environmental sustainability.

Cleaner air and water improve people’s health. Fewer people suffer from breathing problems or water-borne diseases, so workers take fewer days off sick and the productivity of the labour force rises. Healthier people also live and work longer. This increases the quality of labour, shifts the economy’s PPC outwards and raises potential economic growth. At the same time, the government and households spend less on treating pollution-related illness, so the government can use the money saved on other services, such as education, and families have more income to spend on other goods.

Cleaning up polluted land and rivers also improves the quality of land as a factor of production. Farmland becomes more fertile and fish stocks recover, raising output in agriculture and fishing. A cleaner environment attracts more tourists and can encourage multinational firms and skilled workers to locate in the country, increasing exports of services, employment and incomes. The clean-up work itself creates jobs. Overall, living standards rise, not just through higher real GDP but through a better quality of life.

Why this scores
Uses the concept of external costs, then develops two full chains (health → productivity → growth; better land and environment → tourism, investment, output) linked to living standards and sustainability.

Discuss [6]

Past paper (h) · [6 marks]

Discuss whether or not an increase in a country’s import tariff on rice would increase the output of its domestic rice farmers.

Why output may increase. A tariff is a tax on imports, so a higher tariff raises the price of imported rice in the domestic market. Imported rice becomes less competitive compared with rice grown by domestic farmers, so consumers switch to domestic rice. Higher demand for domestic rice and a higher market price increase the profitability of rice farming, so domestic farmers have an incentive to expand production, for example by planting more land or using more fertiliser, and output rises. New farmers may also be attracted into the industry.

Why output may not increase. Domestic farmers may be unable to expand quickly because supply of rice is price inelastic in the short run: it takes a growing season to plant and harvest, and suitable land and water may be limited. Output may also be held back by poor weather or a lack of capital. In addition, other countries may retaliate by putting tariffs on this country’s exports; if the farmers export some of their rice, they lose foreign sales. If demand for rice is price inelastic and the tariff is small, consumers may carry on buying imported rice they prefer, such as a particular variety, so demand for domestic rice rises only slightly.

Conclusion. The tariff is likely to increase domestic output over time, but by how much depends on the size of the tariff, how easily domestic farmers can increase supply, and whether imported and domestic rice are close substitutes. Output will rise most in the long run, when farmers have had time to expand.

Why this scores
A developed argument for (higher import price → switch to domestic rice → higher profits → more output) and developed arguments against (inelastic supply, retaliation, weak substitution), with a conclusion naming what it depends on.
Past paper (g) · [6 marks]

Discuss whether or not an increase in a country’s population will benefit its economy.

Why it may benefit the economy. A larger population, especially one with more people of working age, increases the size of the labour force. More workers raise the economy’s productive capacity, shifting the PPC outwards, and help fill skill shortages. A larger population also means a bigger domestic market, so demand for goods and services rises. Firms can produce on a larger scale and benefit from economies of scale, lowering average costs, and the government collects more tax revenue.

Why it may not benefit the economy. If the population grows faster than the economy’s output, real GDP per head falls and living standards decline. The increase may be mainly children, raising the dependency ratio and requiring more government spending on schools and healthcare. Pressure on scarce resources such as housing, water and farmland can cause shortages, higher prices, congestion and pollution, harming environmental sustainability. If there are not enough jobs, unemployment rises.

Conclusion. Population growth is more likely to benefit an economy that has spare resources, a shortage of workers and the capital to employ extra people productively, as in some high-income countries with ageing populations. It is less likely to benefit a low-income country where output is already growing slowly. It depends on the age structure and skills of the extra people and on whether output grows faster than population.

Why this scores
Balanced development of labour force and market-size benefits against lower GDP per head, dependency and resource pressure, with a conclusion that depends on the country’s circumstances and age structure.

Past paper (d) · [8 marks]

Discuss whether or not an increase in government subsidies will reduce a deficit on the current account of the balance of payments.

Why it may reduce the deficit. A current account deficit occurs when spending on imports of goods and services (plus outflows of income and transfers) exceeds receipts from exports. A subsidy is a payment from the government to producers, which lowers their costs of production. If subsidies are given to exporting firms, they can lower the prices of their products abroad. This makes exports more price competitive, so foreign demand rises and export revenue increases, as long as demand is price elastic. Higher export earnings reduce the current account deficit.

Subsidies to domestic firms that compete with imports, such as local food producers, allow them to cut their prices. Domestic consumers then switch from imported goods to cheaper domestically produced goods, so spending on imports falls, again reducing the deficit. In the longer run, subsidies for training, research or new technology can raise productivity and quality, making the country’s firms more internationally competitive.

Why it may not reduce the deficit. If demand for the country’s exports is price inelastic, for example because foreign buyers value brand or quality more than price, a small price cut will raise the quantity sold only slightly, and export revenue may even fall. Firms may also keep the subsidy as extra profit rather than lowering prices. Moreover, subsidies must be financed. If the government pays for them by raising taxes or cutting other spending, or by borrowing, other parts of the economy may be harmed. Other countries may regard export subsidies as unfair and retaliate with tariffs on this country’s goods, reducing exports.

Also, if subsidised firms expand production, they may need more imported raw materials and components, so imports rise. And if the subsidy increases incomes in the economy, households may spend more on imported goods. The deficit may also be caused by factors subsidies do not address, such as large outflows of profits to foreign-owned firms or a high exchange rate.

Conclusion. Subsidies can reduce a current account deficit if they are targeted at exporters and import-competing firms with price elastic demand, and if the price reductions are passed on to buyers. They are less likely to work when demand is inelastic, production relies on imported inputs, or trading partners retaliate. On balance, subsidies are more effective as a long-term supply-side measure that raises productivity than as a quick fix, and the result depends on how they are targeted and financed.

Why this scores
Two developed arguments for (cheaper exports; switching from imports) and at least two against (inelastic demand, retaliation, imported inputs, opportunity cost of financing), finished with a judgement about targeting, elasticity and time.
Past paper (d) · [8 marks]

Discuss whether or not countries specialising benefits consumers.

Why specialisation may benefit consumers. When countries specialise, they concentrate on producing the goods and services they are best at, for example because of their climate, natural resources or skilled workforce, and trade for the rest. Producing on a larger scale allows firms to gain economies of scale and to become more efficient, so average costs fall. These lower costs can be passed on to consumers as lower prices, raising their real incomes and allowing them to buy more goods and services.

Specialisation and trade also give consumers greater choice and quality. Consumers can buy products their own country cannot make, or cannot make well, such as tropical fruit in a cold country or advanced electronics in a country without the technology. Competition from specialist foreign producers pressures domestic firms to improve quality and innovate, which also benefits consumers.

Why specialisation may not benefit consumers. A country that relies on others for key products becomes dependent on imports. If supply is disrupted, for example by war, a natural disaster, a pandemic or export restrictions in the supplying country, consumers may face shortages and sharp price rises, as happened with some food and energy products in recent years. Transport costs and tariffs may also offset the cost advantage. In addition, over-specialisation can harm consumers as workers: if world demand for the country’s main export falls, or a cheaper producer appears abroad, workers in that industry lose their jobs and incomes. Their occupational immobility can lead to long-term unemployment, reducing their ability to buy goods and services.

Specialisation may also lead to a few large global firms dominating markets. If they gain monopoly power, they may raise prices and restrict choice rather than passing lower costs on. Transporting goods over long distances also increases pollution and CO2 emissions, an external cost that consumers bear as members of society.

Conclusion. Specialisation generally benefits consumers through lower prices and wider choice, and this is the main reason countries trade. However, the benefit depends on whether cost savings are passed on through competition, how reliable supply chains are, and how diversified the economy is. A country that specialises but keeps several sources of supply and several export industries is most likely to gain without exposing its consumers to excessive risk.

Why this scores
Developed benefits (economies of scale → lower prices; more choice and quality) balanced against developed drawbacks (dependence and supply disruption, unemployment of consumers as workers, monopoly power), with a conclusion stating the conditions for benefit.
