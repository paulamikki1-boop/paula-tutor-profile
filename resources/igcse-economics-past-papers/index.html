1.1 The nature of the economic problem

These questions test whether you can explain scarcity precisely (unlimited wants, finite resources) and the difference between economic and free goods; examiners reward exact economic definitions rather than everyday meanings.

What examiners keep seeing
The examiner reports say weaker candidates use the everyday meaning of a ‘free good’ (something given away at no charge) instead of the economic one: a good that is not scarce, uses no resources and has no opportunity cost. Others explain the economic problem in general terms but never apply it to the group the question names, such as consumers.
0455/22/F/M/25 Q2(a) · February/March 2025 · [2 marks]

Source material: South Korea makes use of both economic goods and free goods.

Identify a difference between economic goods and free goods.

Economic goods are scarce because resources (factors of production) are used up to produce them, so they have an opportunity cost. Free goods are not scarce: they are in unlimited supply, need no resources to produce and so have no opportunity cost.

Why this scores
One mark for the economic-good half and one for the free-good half of the same difference, so the answer states both. Examples earn nothing here, and the examiner report warns against the everyday meaning of ‘free’: a free newspaper is still an economic good, because resources were used to make it.

0455/23/O/N/22 Q1(c) · October/November 2022 · [2 marks]

Source material: Tourism relies on natural resources including sunshine and beaches.

State why sunshine has no opportunity cost.

Sunshine is a free good: it occurs naturally and is in unlimited supply, so no resources have to be used to produce it. Using sunshine in Ecuador’s tourism industry therefore means nothing has to be given up, so there is no opportunity cost.

Why this scores
One mark for identifying sunshine as a free good and one for a reason (unlimited supply, not scarce, needs no resources so nothing is sacrificed). The examiner report says vague answers such as ‘sunshine is everywhere’, or answers that only defined opportunity cost, did not score.

Source material: Consumers experience the economic problem when buying raspberries.

Explain how the economic problem results in consumers having to make choices.

The economic problem is that people’s wants are unlimited but the resources available to satisfy them are limited (finite). This creates scarcity: there is not enough to produce everything that everyone would like.

For consumers, the limited resource is their income. A household in Serbia has only a certain amount of money to spend each week, so it cannot buy everything it wants and has to choose, for example between buying raspberries and buying another fruit or a snack. Choosing one product means giving up the next best alternative, which is the opportunity cost of that choice. Scarcity therefore forces consumers to rank their wants and decide which ones to satisfy. 1.2 The factors of production

These questions test the four factors of production, their rewards, and how mobile each factor is; examiners reward precise terms such as ‘resource used to produce goods and services’ and the distinction between occupational and geographical mobility.

What examiners keep seeing
The examiner report says a high proportion of candidates asked to define a factor of production simply listed land, labour, capital and enterprise, which is not a definition. Rewards are another trap: some candidates matched rewards to the wrong factor or named the factor itself (machinery, farmland) instead of its reward.
0455/22/F/M/25 Q3(a) · February/March 2025 · [2 marks]

Define factor of production.

A factor of production is a resource, or input, that is used to produce goods and services.

Why this scores
One mark for ‘resource’ or ‘input’ and one for ‘used to produce goods and services’. The examiner report says many candidates just named the four factors, which is not a definition, and the mark scheme gives no marks for examples.

0455/22/F/M/22 Q5(b) · February/March 2022 · [4 marks]

Source material: Ghana uses much of its agricultural land to grow cocoa.

Explain whether land is mobile.

Most land is geographically immobile. Land is fixed in one place: a cocoa farm in Ghana cannot be moved to another region or country where it might be more productive or where demand is higher. Some natural resources counted as land, such as fish or wild animals, can move from place to place, but they are the exception.

Land can be occupationally mobile. The same piece of land can usually be switched to a different use. Ghana’s farmland could change from growing cocoa to growing other crops, or be cleared for housing, factories or roads if those uses become more profitable. How occupationally mobile it is depends on the type of land: flat, fertile land has many possible uses, whereas a mountainside has very few.

Why this scores
Two marks for each type of mobility: identifying that most land is geographically immobile (1) and explaining that it is fixed in one place (1), then identifying that it is occupationally mobile (1) with an example of a change of use (1). The examiner report credited answers that separated the two kinds of mobility, as this one does.

0455/22/M/J/25 Q2(c) · May/June 2025 · [6 marks]

Source material: Three of the accountancy firms reduced the number of new workers they hired and the other accountancy firm announced job losses. The ability of workers to gain new jobs is influenced by their mobility.

Analyse the advantages of an increase in labour mobility.

Labour mobility is the ability of workers to change jobs. Occupational mobility is the ability to move from one type of job to another, and geographical mobility is the ability to move from one area to another to work.

Lower unemployment. If accountants who lose their jobs are occupationally mobile, for example because their numeracy and IT skills are transferable, they can move quickly into banking, finance or teaching. If they are geographically mobile, they can move to regions or countries where jobs are available. This reduces structural and frictional unemployment, which means the government spends less on unemployment benefits, receives more income tax and fewer households fall into poverty.

Higher incomes for workers. Mobile workers can move to the jobs that pay best and make the most of their skills, and changing jobs gives them new experience. As a result their productivity and earning power rise, so their standard of living improves.

Fewer labour shortages, so more output. Firms in expanding industries can fill vacancies more easily when workers are able to move to them. This stops shortages of workers pushing up wages and costs, so there is less cost-push inflation, and it allows these firms to expand. The economy’s resources are used more fully, so real GDP and economic growth increase.

A more flexible economy. When consumer demand changes, as it did for accountancy services in 2023, workers can move out of declining industries and into growing ones. Resources are reallocated more quickly to the products consumers want, which reduces surpluses and shortages.

Why this scores
Defines both types of mobility, then builds complete chains for workers (jobs, wages), the government (benefits, tax), firms (shortages, costs) and the economy (output, growth), matching the mark scheme’s points. The examiner report praised answers that distinguished geographical from occupational mobility and analysed the effects on unemployment, firms’ flexibility and output.

0455/22/M/J/22 Q1(g) · May/June 2022 · [6 marks]

Source material: To achieve this, it announced increased spending on some areas including mental health, child poverty and pollution. … It was, however, difficult to predict firms’ future profits.

Discuss whether or not enterprise is likely to have increased in New Zealand after 2019.

Enterprise is the willingness and ability of entrepreneurs to take risks and organise the other factors of production to set up and run businesses, in return for profit.

Why enterprise might have increased. New Zealand has high productivity, partly because of high investment in capital goods and education. A well-educated population has more people with the skills and confidence to start firms, and high productivity keeps costs down, raising the profits that reward entrepreneurs. The government’s extra spending on mental health, child poverty and pollution also raises total demand. Higher demand increases firms’ expected revenue and profits, giving people a stronger incentive to set up or expand businesses.

New Zealand’s high GDP per head also attracts net immigration. Some immigrants are likely to be entrepreneurs who set up new firms, and a larger population means more customers. If the government also followed the economists’ advice and cut income tax rates, households would have more disposable income, raising demand and profits further.

Why enterprise might not have increased. The source says it was difficult to predict firms’ future profits because of uncertainty about GDP and corporation tax rates. Entrepreneurs bear risk, so if they fear a recession could cut demand, the expected reward for taking that risk falls and fewer people start businesses.

The extra government spending also has to be paid for. If the government raised corporation tax, firms would keep less of their profit, reducing the incentive to be an entrepreneur. Measures to reduce pollution may also raise firms’ costs of production, for example by forcing them to use cleaner but more expensive methods, which cuts profits further.

Conclusion. Enterprise is likely to have increased in New Zealand after 2019, because an educated, productive labour force and rising demand give entrepreneurs good opportunities. This is most likely if corporation tax was not raised to fund the extra spending and confidence about future GDP stayed high; if taxes on profits rose during a period of uncertainty, enterprise may have stayed the same or fallen.

Why this scores
Up to 4 marks are available for each side, so the answer gives developed reasons both ways, each linked to the reward for enterprise (profit) or to risk, and uses the source material: education, government spending, immigration, uncertainty and corporation tax. The examiner report picked out exactly these points in strong answers, and the conclusion names the conditions the outcome depends on.

1.3 Opportunity cost

These questions test whether you can identify the next best alternative given up in a real choice and explain how it shapes decisions; examiners reward a specific, relevant alternative rather than a general mention of ‘cost’.

What examiners keep seeing
The examiner reports note that some candidates describe opportunity cost as the option that is chosen rather than the next best alternative that is given up, and others pick an alternative that does not fit the context, such as saying the opportunity cost of exporting is importing (it is selling the products in the home market).
0455/22/F/M/24 Q1(b) · February/March 2024 · [2 marks]

Source material: They spend this time in a variety of ways including cycling, jogging and playing golf.

Identify two possible opportunity costs of a Danish person using their leisure time to cycle.

1. Jogging.

2. Playing golf.

Why this scores
One mark each for the two other leisure activities named in the source, because these are the alternative uses of the same leisure time that are given up. “Identify” needs no explanation; the key is to pick alternatives from the source rather than a money cost.

0455/22/F/M/25 Q2(b) · February/March 2025 · [4 marks]

Source material: The country is building the world’s first floating city, estimated to cost $180m.

Explain how opportunity cost will influence a government’s decision on whether to spend tax revenue on a large infrastructure project.

Opportunity cost is the next best alternative given up when a choice is made. A government’s tax revenue is limited, so if it spends $180m on a large infrastructure project such as South Korea’s floating city, that money cannot be used for something else, for example building new hospitals or schools. On the diagram, moving from A to B gains more infrastructure but means fewer hospitals and schools.

The government therefore has to compare the benefits of the project with the benefits of the best alternative it would give up. It will go ahead only if it judges that the project gives the greater benefit, for example because protecting people and property from rising sea levels is a higher priority than extra healthcare. If the forgone hospitals would bring greater benefits, the opportunity cost is too high and the government may reject or delay the project.

Why this scores
Marks for defining opportunity cost (best alternative forgone), for explaining that limited tax revenue spent on the project cannot be spent elsewhere, with an example, and for showing how the government weighs its priorities to decide. The examiner report said strong answers explained that tax revenue is limited and identified which choice would be given up.

0455/23/O/N/25 Q2(c) · October/November 2025 · [6 marks]

Source material: Over this period, its decisions have always been influenced by opportunity cost.

Analyse how opportunity cost influences the decisions made by consumers, workers and producers.

Opportunity cost is the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made. Consumers, workers and producers all have limited resources, so every decision involves giving something up, and they compare what they would gain with what they would sacrifice.

Consumers. Consumers have limited incomes, so they cannot buy everything they want. If a family spends $50 on a meal out, it cannot spend that $50 on new clothes. A rational consumer chooses the option that gives more satisfaction than the alternative given up. If the price of a product rises, such as the 8% rise in the MNC’s food prices, its opportunity cost rises because buying it now means giving up more of other goods, so consumers may switch to cheaper brands.

Workers. Workers have limited time. An hour spent at leisure means the wage for that hour is given up, so if wages rise, leisure becomes more expensive and a worker may choose to work more hours. Workers also choose between jobs: accepting one job means giving up the pay and conditions of the next best job, so they compare wages and non-wage factors before deciding.

Producers. Firms have limited finance, land, labour and capital, so they must decide what to produce, how to produce it and where to locate. If a food manufacturer uses its factory to make more chocolate, the opportunity cost is the cereal or coffee it could have made instead. The firm therefore puts its resources into the products that earn the highest profit, and switches them when another product becomes more profitable.

Why this scores
Opens with a definition, then analyses all three groups, each with its limited resource (income, time, finance), a specific choice and the alternative forgone. The mark scheme caps answers at 4 marks if they cover fewer than two of consumers, workers and producers, so covering all three, each with a chain to the decision made, secures full marks.

0455/21/M/J/24 Q1(h) · May/June 2024 · [6 marks]

Source material: Nicaragua is the world’s twelfth largest producer of coffee. Global coffee consumption continues to increase.

Discuss whether or not Nicaragua should devote more of its resources to coffee production.

Devoting more resources to coffee means using more of Nicaragua’s land, labour, capital and enterprise to grow and process coffee. Every extra resource used this way has an opportunity cost: the other goods those resources could have produced. The diagram shows this as a movement along the PPC from A to B.

Why Nicaragua should. Global coffee consumption continues to increase, so demand for Nicaragua’s coffee is likely to rise. Demand for coffee is price-inelastic (PED of 0.3), so as rising world demand pushes up the price, farmers’ revenue rises. More coffee exports would also improve the current account of the balance of payments, and as agricultural products already make up 60% of exports, Nicaragua has the land, skills and experience to produce coffee efficiently.

Expanding coffee production would also create jobs in rural areas, where wages are particularly low and unemployment rose in 2020. More employment would raise incomes, reduce poverty and increase tax revenue. Larger coffee firms could also gain economies of scale, lowering average costs and making Nicaragua more competitive against other producers.

Why Nicaragua should not. Coffee output is often affected by droughts and earthquakes. Putting even more resources into one crop makes the economy more vulnerable, because a single natural disaster could wipe out much of its output and export earnings. If world coffee prices fall, for example because other countries expand production, Nicaragua’s income would also fall sharply.

There is also a significant opportunity cost. The land and workers used for extra coffee could instead produce food for the domestic market or develop manufacturing and tourism, which may pay higher wages than low-paid coffee farming. Table 1.1 suggests that relying on agriculture keeps GDP per head low: only 3% of the Bahamas’ labour force works in agriculture and its GDP per head is $25 194, compared with 31% and $1905 in Nicaragua.

Conclusion. Nicaragua should devote somewhat more resources to coffee, because world demand is growing and it already produces coffee well. The case is strongest if the extra resources come from unemployed workers and are used for drought-resistant farming and high-quality coffee; it is weakest if they are taken from manufacturing or tourism, which would do more to raise GDP per head in the long run.

Why this scores
Up to 4 marks are available for each side, so the answer develops reasons both ways and uses the source data (rising world demand, PED of 0.3, natural disasters, low rural wages, Table 1.1). The opportunity-cost argument is credited directly by the mark scheme, and the conclusion makes a judgement that depends on where the extra resources come from.

1.4 Production possibility curve (PPC) diagrams

These questions test what a PPC shows and the difference between a point on it, a point inside it and a shift of the whole curve; examiners reward fully labelled diagrams and linking every shift to a change in the quantity or quality of resources.

What examiners keep seeing
The 2022 examiner report says weaker answers treated a fall in actual output, such as a poor grape harvest, as a shift of the PPC. A PPC shows productive capacity, not actual output: only a change in the quantity or quality of resources shifts it, while unemployed resources put the economy inside the curve. Diagram marks are also lost when the curves do not touch both axes.
0455/22/M/J/22 Q3(a) · May/June 2022 · [2 marks]

Define a production possibility curve.

A production possibility curve is a diagram showing the maximum combinations of two types of products that an economy can produce with its given resources and technology.

Why this scores
One mark for ‘maximum output’ or ‘possible combinations’ and one for ‘two products’ or ‘given resources’. The examiner report notes that some candidates just drew a diagram: a diagram is not a definition and earns nothing here.

0455/22/O/N/25 Q2(b) · October/November 2025 · [4 marks]

Source material: The Belgian Government wants the economy to move to a production possibility point beyond its current production possibility curve (PPC).

Explain two ways an economy could reach a point outside its current PPC.

Increase investment in capital goods. If firms and the government devote more resources to building new machinery, factories and infrastructure, the quantity of capital increases. With more capital the economy can produce more of both types of goods, so the PPC shifts outwards and a point that was outside the old curve becomes attainable.

Improve the quality of labour through education and training. Spending on education and training makes workers more skilled, like the highly specialised workers in Belgium’s scientific industry. Their productivity rises, so the same number of workers can produce more output. Productive capacity increases and the PPC shifts outwards. The diagram shows the result of either change: point X, outside the original PPC, lies on the new curve PPC1.

Why this scores
One mark for each way identified and one for each explanation of how it raises the quantity or quality of resources and so shifts the PPC outwards. Other creditworthy ways include immigration, land reclamation, new technology and discovering new natural resources.

0455/23/O/N/23 Q2(c) · October/November 2023 · [6 marks]

Source material: In 2019, Mozambique was hit by a serious storm which destroyed bridges, factories, roads and electricity lines.

Analyse, using a production possibility curve (PPC) diagram, the effect of a serious storm on an economy.

A production possibility curve (PPC) shows the maximum combinations of two types of goods, here capital goods and consumer goods, that an economy can produce with its existing resources and technology.

A serious storm, such as the one that hit Mozambique in 2019, destroys factors of production. Bridges, factories, roads and electricity lines are all capital, so the quantity of capital falls. Farmland may be flooded and workers may be injured or forced to leave the area, which reduces the quantity of land and labour as well.

With fewer resources, the economy’s productive capacity falls: it can no longer produce as much of either type of good as before. As shown in the diagram, the PPC shifts inwards from PPC to PPC1. A combination on the old curve, such as point A, is no longer attainable, and the most the economy can now produce is a combination on PPC1, such as B.

In the short run, damaged roads and power lines may also stop firms that still have their machinery from getting raw materials or electricity, so some resources become unemployed and the economy may produce inside even its new PPC. The economy can only move back out as the damaged capital is rebuilt.

Why this scores
Up to 4 marks are for the diagram: axes labelled with two types of output, both curves touching both axes, and the inward shift shown with an arrow and labels. The other 2 marks are for explaining that the storm destroys resources and so reduces productive capacity. A new curve that does not reach both axes loses the diagram marks.

0455/21/M/J/22 Q1(g) · May/June 2022 · [6 marks]

Source material: The interest rate in Cyprus was very low in 2019. … In 2019, two concerns for the future of Cyprus’s economy were that the quality of some of its agricultural land may decline and that some of its top entrepreneurs may emigrate.

Discuss whether or not, after 2019, Cyprus’s production possibility curve (PPC) is likely to shift to the right.

A PPC shows the maximum output of two types of goods that an economy can produce with its resources. It shifts to the right (outwards) when the quantity or quality of those resources increases, raising productive capacity. The diagram below shows the two possibilities discussed: a shift to the right (PPC1) or a shift to the left (PPC2).

Why it might shift to the right. The interest rate in Cyprus was very low in 2019. Cheaper borrowing encourages firms to invest in capital goods, and some firms have already become more capital-intensive. More and better machinery increases the quantity and quality of capital, so productive capacity rises and the PPC shifts outwards.

The labour force may also grow. The population rose from 1.1 million in 2015 to 1.2 million in 2019, life expectancy is rising and more workers are working past retirement age. If the retirement age is raised, people will stay in the labour force for longer, increasing the quantity of labour and shifting the PPC to the right.

Why it might not shift to the right. The quality of some agricultural land may decline, which would reduce the productivity of land, and some top entrepreneurs may emigrate. Enterprise organises the other factors and takes risks, so losing entrepreneurs means fewer new firms and less efficient use of resources. Both would reduce productive capacity and could shift the PPC to the left.

In the longer term, the birth rate fell from 11.0 to 10.2 per 1000, so fewer young people will enter the labour force in future. Unless immigration increases, the quantity of labour may eventually fall, which would also stop the PPC shifting outwards.

Conclusion. Cyprus’s PPC is likely to shift to the right in the next few years, because low interest rates and longer working lives should increase the quantity of capital and labour. This depends on how many entrepreneurs actually emigrate and how quickly land quality declines: if those losses are large, they could cancel out the gains and leave the PPC unchanged or shift it to the left.

Why this scores
Each side links evidence from the source to a change in the quantity or quality of a factor of production, and so to productive capacity, which is what the mark scheme rewards (up to 4 marks per side). The examiner report says weaker answers wrongly used the bad grape harvest, a fall in actual output, as a reason for a shift; this answer avoids that and ends with a conditional judgement.

2.1 Microeconomics and macroeconomics Background for 2027–29

These short questions test whether you can say precisely what microeconomics and macroeconomics study and classify examples of each; examiners reward the key phrases ‘individual markets and decision makers’ and ‘the economy as a whole’.

Source material: Its output includes products with both elastic and inelastic supply.

Price elasticity of supply (PES) measures how responsive quantity supplied is to a change in price. Supply is elastic when quantity supplied changes by a greater percentage than price (PES greater than 1) and inelastic when it changes by a smaller percentage (PES less than 1). The diagrams below compare the two.

0455/22/M/J/23 Q2(d) · May/June 2023 · [8 marks]

Source material: A growing private sector can move an economy towards a market economic system.

Discuss whether or not a market economy allocates resources in the best possible way.

A market economy is one in which resources are allocated by the price mechanism: private firms decide what to produce in response to consumer demand, with little government intervention.

Why it might allocate resources in the best possible way. The price mechanism responds automatically to consumers’ wishes. If demand for electric bikes rises and demand for petrol cars falls, the price of electric bikes rises and the price of petrol cars falls. Making electric bikes becomes more profitable, so firms move land, labour and capital out of car production and into bikes. Resources are therefore used to make what consumers value most, without a government needing to plan it. The diagrams below show the two markets.

Competition between private firms also encourages efficiency. Firms that keep costs low and make good-quality products win customers, while inefficient firms make losses and close, releasing their resources to better uses. Wages work in the same way: higher wages attract workers into the jobs where labour is most in demand. As a result, output is produced at low cost and sold at low prices.

Why it might not. The market ignores external costs and benefits. A factory that pollutes a river does not pay for the damage, so its product is over-produced, while goods with external benefits, such as vaccinations, are under-produced. In the same way, merit goods such as education are under-consumed because people do not realise their full benefit, demerit goods such as cigarettes are over-consumed, and public goods such as street lighting are not produced at all because firms cannot stop non-payers from benefiting.

Resources may be slow to move too: if workers are occupationally or geographically immobile, there can be unemployment in declining industries while growing industries are short of labour.

Conclusion. A market economy is good at allocating resources to private goods that consumers want, such as clothing or smartphones, when markets are competitive. It does not allocate resources in the best possible way where there are external costs or benefits, merit, demerit or public goods, or monopoly power. How close it comes to the best allocation depends on how competitive its markets are and how important these market failures are, which is why most countries choose a mixed economy.

Source material: However, Madagascar has a largely market economic system. Nearly 70% of Madagascar’s population live in poverty.

Discuss whether or not a high level of poverty is likely to exist in a market economic system.

A market economic system allocates resources through the price mechanism, with little government intervention. Absolute poverty is when people do not have enough income to meet their basic needs; relative poverty is when people have much less income than the average in their country.

Why a high level of poverty is likely. In a market system, people’s incomes depend on the factors of production they own. Workers with few skills earn low wages, while owners of capital and land earn profit and rent, so income and wealth are very unequally distributed. With little or no government welfare, such as unemployment benefits or pensions, people who cannot work or who lose their jobs have no income to fall back on, so they fall into poverty.

Merit goods such as education and healthcare are available only to those who can pay for them. Poor families cannot afford them, so their children stay unskilled and unhealthy and earn low wages as adults, which keeps poverty going from one generation to the next. Firms may also grow into monopolies and raise the prices of necessities, reducing what low-income households can buy. Madagascar, with a largely market system, has nearly 70% of its population living in poverty.

Why a high level of poverty is not likely. Competition and the profit motive encourage firms to be efficient, which keeps prices low, so people’s incomes buy more. Efficient firms also expand and create jobs; higher employment means more households earn an income and fewer are in absolute poverty.

Market economies respond quickly to changes in consumer demand and give entrepreneurs an incentive to invest and innovate, which can lead to high economic growth: Madagascar grew by 6% in 2020. Over time, faster growth raises average incomes, so even if relative poverty remains because incomes are unequal, absolute poverty can fall, as it has in many high-income economies with large private sectors.

Conclusion. A market economic system is likely to have a high level of relative poverty because it creates income inequality and provides no safety net. Whether absolute poverty is high depends mainly on how productive and competitive its firms are and how fast the economy grows: a low-income, labour-intensive economy like Madagascar is likely to have high poverty, while a high-income market economy with efficient firms is likely to have low absolute poverty but still some relative poverty.

This meets Level 3: it defines the key terms, develops two arguments on each side, uses the Madagascar data, and the conclusion separates absolute from relative poverty, which the mark scheme credits as an evaluative point.

0455/21/O/N/25 Q3(c) · October/November 2025 · [6 marks]

Source material: Japan has faced significant periods of deflation in recent decades.

Analyse reasons why central banks are important for an economy.

A central bank is the government-owned bank that controls the money supply, runs monetary policy and acts as the banker to the government and to commercial banks.

Keeping prices stable. The central bank changes the rate of interest to influence total demand. In a period of deflation, as Japan has experienced, it can cut the interest rate, which makes borrowing cheaper and saving less rewarding. Households spend more on credit and firms invest more, so total demand rises and prices stop falling. This matters because deflation encourages people to delay purchases, which cuts output and jobs. When inflation is too high it does the opposite, raising interest rates to protect people’s purchasing power. The diagram below shows the chain for a cut in the rate of interest.

Maintaining financial stability. The central bank supervises commercial banks, for example by checking they keep enough liquid assets. If a bank runs short of cash it acts as lender of last resort, lending to it so that it does not collapse. This protects savers’ deposits, keeps public confidence in the banking system and makes sure households and firms can still borrow, so spending and investment are not suddenly cut.

Managing the exchange rate. The central bank holds the country’s foreign currency reserves and can buy or sell its own currency to stop sharp swings in the exchange rate. A more stable exchange rate reduces uncertainty for exporters and importers, so they can plan and invest with more confidence.

Why this scores
Each function is linked through to why it matters for the economy, which is what “coherent analysis” needs. The mark scheme credits controlling inflation and deflation through monetary policy, financial stability, lender of last resort and exchange-rate management, and the Japan context shows application.

0455/23/O/N/22 Q5(d) · October/November 2022 · [8 marks]

Source material: In 2019, commercial bank lending to firms and households in Palau increased.

Discuss whether or not an increase in commercial bank lending will increase economic growth.

Economic growth is an increase in a country’s real GDP, that is, in the output of goods and services. Commercial bank lending is the loans that banks make to households and firms.

Why it might increase economic growth. More loans allow households to buy expensive items such as houses, cars and furniture that they could not pay for out of current income. Consumer spending rises, so total demand rises. Firms respond by producing more and employing more workers, so real GDP increases, especially if the economy has spare capacity.

Firms can also borrow to invest in capital goods such as machinery, buildings and technology, and new firms can borrow to start up. Investment adds to total demand straight away, but it also increases the economy’s productive capacity, because more and better capital raises productivity. This means output can keep rising in the long run.

Why it might not increase economic growth. If the economy is already at or near full employment, firms cannot easily produce more. Higher total demand then pulls up prices rather than output, causing demand-pull inflation instead of growth.

The extra spending may also go on imports. Palau is a small island economy that has to import many goods, so loans spent on imported cars or electronics raise output in other countries, not in Palau. In addition, some households and firms may borrow simply to repay old debts, and those who take on more debt must use future income for repayments. If interest rates later rise, their spending and investment will fall, which can reverse any growth.

Conclusion. Higher commercial bank lending is likely to give at least a short-term boost to growth. It is most likely to raise growth in the long run if the loans finance investment by firms rather than consumer spending, if the economy has unemployed resources, and if the money is spent on domestically produced goods. In a small, import-dependent economy like Palau, much of the effect of extra household borrowing may leak abroad, so the benefit depends mainly on how much of the lending goes to productive investment.

Why this scores
Level 3: definitions first, two developed chains on each side (consumption and investment versus inflation, imports and debt), Palau’s context used, and a conclusion that states the conditions. The examiner report noted that the “might not” side was usually weak and limited to a mention of debt or imports, so develop it as fully as the first side.

0455/22/M/J/22 Q5(c) · May/June 2022 · [6 marks]

Source material: Nicaragua experienced a 4% decrease in GDP in 2019, although household spending rose.

Analyse how the level and pattern of household spending may change when GDP decreases.

GDP is the total value of output produced in a country in a year, so a fall in GDP usually means falling incomes. The level of household spending is the total amount spent; the pattern is what it is spent on.

The level is likely to fall. When output falls, firms cut jobs, hours and pay, so household incomes fall. Households have less purchasing power and buy fewer goods and services. Lower GDP also reduces confidence: people worried about losing their jobs save more for emergencies and are less willing to borrow in case they cannot repay. If they expect prices to fall, they may also delay buying expensive items such as cars. All of this cuts spending further.

But the level may not fall. As in Nicaragua in 2019, spending can rise even when GDP falls. If households believe the fall is only temporary, they may use their past savings or borrow, perhaps helped by lower interest rates during the downturn, to maintain their living standards. Spending then falls by less than income, or not at all, so households spend a higher proportion of their income.

The pattern is likely to change. With lower incomes, households spend a larger share on necessities such as food, rent and electricity, and cut back on luxuries such as holidays, eating out and new clothes. They may also switch to cheaper brands and buy fewer imported goods.

Why this scores
It covers both the level and the pattern, which the question asks for, and develops each chain: lower GDP → lower income or confidence → less spending or more saving. The examiner report said weaker answers stopped at “income falls so they spend less”, while strong ones analysed necessities versus luxuries and the motives for saving and borrowing. The “may not fall” paragraph uses the stimulus and is credited by the mark scheme.

Source material: Smartphones have made it easier for people to borrow and save their money.

Discuss whether or not an increase in the level of savings is beneficial for an economy.

Saving is the part of disposable income that is not spent on consumption. An increase in the level of savings means households are putting aside more of their income.

Why it may be beneficial. Savings deposited in banks provide funds that banks can lend to firms. Firms borrow to invest in machinery, technology and training, which raises productivity and the economy’s productive capacity. This leads to economic growth and higher living standards in the future. Discuss whether or not division of labour will benefit workers.

Division of labour is when production is broken down into separate tasks, with each worker specialising in one task, such as processing loans, handling online customer queries or managing IT security in a bank.

Why it might benefit workers. Repeating the same task makes workers highly skilled at it: practice makes perfect. They become more confident and find the work less stressful because they know exactly what to do. Their higher productivity can lead to higher wages or bonuses, and makes the firm more competitive, which improves their job security.

Their work can also be disrupted if colleagues doing earlier tasks are absent.

Division of labour is most likely to benefit workers when their tasks are skilled and well paid, as with specialist technology roles in banking, and when employers rotate jobs and retrain staff as technology changes. It is least likely to benefit them when the tasks are simple and repetitive, because those jobs are both boring and the first to be automated, which is exactly what the shift to online banking is doing.

0455/22/O/N/25 Q3(c) · October/November 2025 · [6 marks]

Source material: It is expected the merger will lower average total cost as the new firm is likely to benefit from greater internal economies of scale.

Analyse the internal economies of scale that a cosmetics firm could experience.

Internal economies of scale are the reductions in average total cost (cost per unit) that a firm gains as a result of its own growth in size.

Purchasing economies. A large cosmetics firm buys ingredients such as oils, fragrances and packaging in very large quantities. Suppliers give it bulk-buying discounts, so the cost of the raw materials in each bottle of perfume or tube of lipstick falls, which lowers average total cost.

Technical economies. A larger firm can afford advanced, automated machinery for mixing, filling and packaging its products. This equipment is expensive, but when it is used to produce millions of units its cost is spread over a much larger output. Output per worker also rises, so the cost of producing each unit falls.

Marketing economies. Advertising a cosmetics brand, for example through a global social media campaign, costs much the same whether the firm sells one million or ten million units. A large firm spreads this cost over far more sales, so its advertising cost per unit is much lower than a small rival’s.

Financial and research and development economies. Banks see a large firm as less risky, so it can borrow more cheaply, at lower interest rates, which reduces its costs. It can also afford its own research laboratory to develop new products, such as perfumes made from the renewable raw materials it plans to use, and the cost of that research is spread across a large output.

Why this scores
Opens with a definition and then fully develops four named internal economies, each ending with the effect on average (unit) cost. The mark scheme caps an undeveloped list of economies at 3 marks, so it is the explanation of how each one lowers cost per unit that earns the top marks.

0455/21/M/J/25 Q1(h) · May/June 2025 · [6 marks]

Source material: The Gabonese Government wants to develop its textile industry, encouraging firms to grow in size.

Discuss whether or not a growth in the size of firms in Gabon’s textile industry will reduce their average cost of production.

Average cost is total cost divided by output. It will fall if firms gain economies of scale as they grow, and rise if they suffer diseconomies of scale.

Why average cost may fall. Larger textile firms can buy cotton, dyes and thread in bulk and receive discounts, so the material cost of each metre of cloth falls (purchasing economies). They can also afford advanced weaving machines and spread their cost over a much bigger output (technical economies). In addition, with more workers each one can specialise in one task, such as cutting or sewing, which the source mentions; this raises labour productivity so the labour cost per item falls.

Why average cost may not fall. If a firm grows too large it may suffer diseconomies of scale. Communication becomes harder as there are more layers of management, so decisions are slower and mistakes more frequent. Workers may feel less valued in a very large firm, so motivation and productivity fall. In both cases output rises by a smaller proportion than costs, so average cost rises. Simply employing more workers or buying more machines also raises total cost and will only lower average cost if it makes production more efficient. The diagram shows both stages: average total cost falls as the firm grows, then rises if it grows too large.

Conclusion. Gabon’s textile firms are likely to see average cost fall as they grow, because they are starting from a small size where economies of scale are large. However, this depends on how well the growth is managed: if firms grow so large that communication and worker motivation suffer, diseconomies of scale could push average cost back up.

Why this scores
The mark scheme gives up to 4 marks for each side, and full marks for two explained economies and one explained diseconomy of scale. The examiner report says weaker answers just listed economies of scale or wrote about factors that raise total cost; this answer links every point to cost per unit and uses the source’s reference to specialisation.

0455/22/M/J/23 Q4(d) · May/June 2023 · [8 marks]

Source material: Some dairy farms and some small firms went out of business in 2020.

Discuss whether or not small firms are more likely to go out of business than large firms.

A small firm is one with few employees, a low output and a small share of its market. A firm goes out of business when its revenue does not cover its costs for long enough that it can no longer pay its bills and has to close.

Why small firms might be more likely to go out of business. Small firms produce a low output, so they cannot gain economies of scale such as bulk-buying discounts or the use of advanced machinery. Their average cost is therefore higher than that of large rivals. Large firms can then charge lower prices, attract customers away from small firms and leave them with too little revenue to cover their costs.

Small firms also find it harder to raise finance. Commercial banks see them as risky, so loans are more difficult to obtain and charge higher interest rates, and small firms usually have little retained profit to fall back on. Many are new and run by inexperienced entrepreneurs who are still testing whether demand exists. If there is a sudden fall in demand, as in Australia in 2020, a small firm has nothing to cover its losses, while a large firm can survive using its reserves.

Why small firms might not be more likely to go out of business. Large firms can suffer diseconomies of scale. Poor communication and low worker motivation can raise their average cost, so a large firm can become inefficient and make losses. Small firms are easier to manage and more flexible: the owner can quickly change the product or cut costs when demand changes.

Many small firms also serve a niche market, such as a specialist cheese made on one dairy farm, where demand is too small for a large firm to bother entering. In that market the small firm may be the only supplier and have loyal customers. Governments in many countries also give small firms grants, cheap loans or tax relief, which helps them survive.

Conclusion. Small firms are generally more likely to go out of business than large firms, mainly because they have higher average costs and less access to finance to survive a downturn. However, a small firm with a niche market, loyal customers and government support may be very secure, while a large firm that has grown too big and suffers diseconomies of scale can still fail. The risk depends most on whether the small firm competes head-on with large firms on price or serves a specialist market.

Why this scores
This fits Level 3: definitions first, two developed arguments each side (economies of scale and finance against diseconomies, flexibility, niche markets and government support) and a conclusion naming the key condition. The examiner report gave a Level 1 example that stated points such as “small firms are not as developed” without explaining them, and noted that weaker answers did not distinguish average from total cost.

Source material: Recently, US farmers have switched to keeping other livestock and growing crops that changed their demand for labour.

Demand for the food produced. If demand for the crops or meat the farmer produces rises, the price and the profit from extra output rise. 0455/22/M/J/24 Q4(d) · May/June 2024 · [8 marks]

Source material: Despite this possibility, some Tajik firms bought new capital equipment.

Discuss whether or not consumers would benefit from firms buying new capital equipment.

Capital equipment is the man-made machinery, tools and technology that firms use to produce goods and services. Buying new capital equipment is a form of investment.

Why consumers might benefit. New machinery usually embodies the latest technology, so it raises productivity: more output is produced with the same workers and materials. This lowers the firm’s average cost of production. If the firm passes these lower costs on, consumers pay lower prices, which raises their purchasing power and allows them to buy more goods and services.

Modern equipment also tends to be more accurate, so it reduces mistakes and improves the quality and reliability of products. It may allow the firm to produce entirely new products, and because output increases, goods are more widely available and shortages are less likely. Consumers therefore gain more choice and better products.

Why consumers might not benefit. New capital equipment is expensive. In the short run the firm may raise its prices to cover the cost of the purchase or the interest on a loan, so consumers pay more. While workers learn to use the new machines there may be disruption, so output and quality could fall for a time.

In addition, the firm may not pass lower costs on at all. A firm with little competition, such as a monopoly, may keep prices the same and take the cost savings as higher profit. Machine-made products can also become more standardised, so consumers who valued handmade or personalised goods lose out. If workers are replaced by machines, some consumers may lose their jobs and their income.

Conclusion. Consumers are likely to benefit in the long run, mainly through lower prices and better quality, once the new equipment is working fully. The benefit is smaller, or may not appear, in the short run, or when the firm faces little competition and so has no need to cut its prices. Whether consumers gain therefore depends mainly on how competitive the market is and on the time period being considered.

Why this scores
Level 3 needs a reasoned discussion of both sides: the answer defines capital, develops two arguments each way (cost and price; quality and choice; short-run price rises; lack of competition and standardisation) and ends with a judgement that depends on competition and time. Each chain goes all the way to the effect on consumers, which is the focus of the question.

0455/22/F/M/22 Q3(c) · February/March 2022 · [6 marks]

Analyse, using a diagram, the effect of an increase in output on average fixed cost (AFC) and total fixed cost (TFC).

Fixed costs are costs that do not change with the level of output in the short run, such as rent, insurance and loan repayments. They have to be paid even if the firm produces nothing.

Total fixed cost. Because fixed costs do not depend on output, an increase in output has no effect on total fixed cost. If a firm pays $10 000 a month in rent, it pays $10 000 whether it makes 100 units or 1 000 units. TFC is therefore shown as a horizontal line in the diagram.

Average fixed cost. AFC is total fixed cost divided by output. As output rises, the same total fixed cost is spread over more units, so AFC falls continuously. With rent of $10 000, AFC is $100 per unit at an output of 100 but only $10 per unit at an output of 1 000. In the diagram, an increase in output from Q1 to Q2 causes AFC to fall from AFC1 to AFC2.

This is why firms with high fixed costs try to increase output: spreading fixed costs more thinly lowers their average total cost and allows them to charge lower prices or earn more profit on each unit. In the long run, however, all costs can change, for example if the firm moves to bigger premises, which would shift TFC upwards.

Why this scores
The mark scheme gives up to 4 marks for the diagram (axes labelled costs and output, a horizontal TFC, a downward-sloping AFC, both curves labelled) and 2 for the written analysis that TFC does not change and AFC falls because TFC is divided by a larger output. The examiner report says some candidates drew TFC rising, or confused AFC with average variable cost.

0455/22/F/M/23 Q2(d) · February/March 2023 · [8 marks]

Source material: Some of their firms have grown and now compete with foreign multinational companies (MNCs) and public sector firms.

Discuss whether or not private sector firms are likely to charge lower prices than public sector firms.

Private sector firms are owned by individuals or shareholders and usually aim to maximise profit. Public sector firms are owned by the government and often aim to provide an affordable service and to increase social welfare.

Why private sector firms might charge lower prices. Most private sector firms face competition from rivals. To attract customers and avoid losing market share, they must keep their prices low. They also have a strong incentive to be efficient: if they waste resources their costs rise, they make losses and they may go out of business. Lower costs allow them to charge lower prices than a public sector firm that has no fear of closing down.

Large private sector firms, such as MNCs, may also produce on a very large scale and gain economies of scale, such as bulk buying and advanced technology. They may have more funds to invest in new machinery than a government-owned firm, which lowers their average cost and allows them to cut prices.

Why private sector firms might not charge lower prices. The main objective of a private sector firm is usually profit, so if it can charge a high price without losing many customers, it will. This is most likely if it is a monopoly with no close rivals, or if demand for its product is price-inelastic, for example water or electricity.

Public sector firms, by contrast, may deliberately charge low prices to make essential services affordable for poorer households, for example public transport. They may be subsidised by the government, so they can charge a price below their cost of production. A public sector firm may also be very large, such as a national electricity company, and gain its own economies of scale.

Conclusion. Private sector firms are likely to charge lower prices than public sector firms when they face strong competition, because competition forces them to keep costs and prices down. However, where a private firm is a monopoly selling an essential product, it is likely to charge more than a subsidised public sector firm whose objective is affordability rather than profit. The answer therefore depends mainly on the level of competition in the market and on whether the public sector firm is subsidised.

Why this scores
This reaches Level 3 by explaining how each firm’s objective and situation affects its price, rather than only stating the objectives. The examiner report said many candidates recognised the different motives but did not go on to explain the effect on prices, and that stronger answers explored competition, funds available and the size of firms, as this answer does.

0455/22/O/N/24 Q5(c) · October/November 2024 · [6 marks]

Source material: There are many firms in the Peruvian tourism industry.

Analyse the benefits consumers may gain from a competitive market.

A competitive market is one with many firms selling similar products and low barriers to entry, so consumers have many close substitutes to choose from.

Lower prices. Because consumers can easily switch to a rival, each firm must keep its prices low to attract customers and win market share. If one Peruvian tour operator raised its prices, its customers would go to another. Lower prices increase consumers’ purchasing power, so they can buy more with the same income.

Higher quality and innovation. To stand out from rivals, firms try to improve their products, for example better hotels, guides or booking websites. Source material: There are several firms in the industry, but the number is declining. There is a possibility that soon there may be just one firm in the industry.

Discuss whether or not consumers would benefit from only one firm producing shampoo.

If only one firm produced shampoo, it would be a monopoly: the single supplier of a product, protected by barriers to entry.

Why consumers might benefit. A single firm would supply the whole market, so it would produce on a very large scale. It could buy ingredients and bottles in bulk at a discount and use the most advanced, automated production lines. These economies of scale would lower its average cost. If the firm passed some of these savings on, consumers could pay a lower price than when several smaller firms each had higher costs.

A monopoly may also earn high profits and feel secure that rivals cannot copy its ideas. It can therefore afford to spend heavily on research and development, producing better shampoos, for example for different hair types, and maintaining a high quality. Consumers would also save time, as they would not need to compare many brands.

A profit-maximising monopoly can restrict supply and raise its price without losing many sales, so consumers pay more and lose purchasing power.

Consumers would also have less choice: the firm may offer only a few types of shampoo. With no rivals to fear, it has less incentive to keep quality high or to be efficient, and if its quality falls, consumers have no alternative. 0455/23/O/N/20 Q3(d) · October/November 2020 · [8 marks]

Source material: Privatisation can reduce monopoly power in a market.

Discuss whether or not a government should allow monopolies.

A monopoly is a single firm that supplies the whole of a market, or has a dominant share of it, and is protected by barriers to entry.

Why a government should allow monopolies. A monopoly produces on a very large scale, so it can gain economies of scale, such as bulk-buying discounts and specialised machinery. Its average cost may therefore be lower than if many small firms shared the market, and it can charge lower prices. In industries such as water supply or railways, having several firms each building their own pipes or tracks would waste resources, so one firm is more efficient.

Monopolies often earn high profits, which they can reinvest in research and development. This can lead to new, better-quality products and more efficient methods of production. A large monopoly may also be able to compete with big foreign firms in international markets, increasing the country’s exports. A state-owned monopoly may even have social welfare objectives and keep its prices low.

Why a government should not allow monopolies. A private monopoly with no rivals can abuse its market power. Since consumers have no alternative, it can restrict output and raise its price to maximise profit, so consumers pay more and buy less. This is a form of market failure because resources are not allocated in the way consumers would choose.

Without the pressure of competition, a monopoly may also become complacent. It has little incentive to cut costs, improve quality or offer choice, so costs may rise and products may be poorer. The high profits go to the owners at the expense of consumers, which can widen income inequality, especially if the product is a necessity such as electricity.

Conclusion. A government should allow monopolies in industries where large-scale production gives major economies of scale or where competition would waste resources, such as water supply, but only if it regulates their prices and quality. Where a monopoly is likely to raise prices and become inefficient, the government should prevent it or open the market to competition, for example through privatisation and allowing new firms to enter. The decision depends on whether the cost savings from size are large enough to outweigh the risk of abuse of market power.

Why this scores
Level 3 requires both sides to be developed; the examiner report noted that weaker answers were stronger on not allowing monopolies, while strong answers also evaluated the benefits (better quality, funds for research and development, competing with foreign firms). This answer gives both sides equal depth and ends with a conditional judgement about regulation and economies of scale.

0455/21/O/N/24 Q4(c) · October/November 2024 · [6 marks]

Source material: For example, in 2018 India imposed a tariff of approximately 90% on imports of coffee to protect its coffee farmers.

Analyse possible reasons why a government may want to intervene in a market.

A government may intervene in a market when the market mechanism leads to market failure, an inefficient allocation of resources, so that the price system alone produces the wrong goods, in the wrong quantities, for the wrong people.

To provide public goods. Goods such as street lighting and defence are non-excludable and non-rival, so people can enjoy them without paying. This free rider problem means private firms cannot make a profit from them, so the market will not supply them at all. The government therefore provides them itself, financed by taxation.

To limit monopoly power. A firm with monopoly power can restrict output and charge high prices, reducing consumer choice and quality. Competition policy and regulation can stop harmful mergers or cap prices, so that consumers pay lower prices.

To protect producers and workers. A government may impose tariffs, as India did with a 90% tariff on coffee imports, to protect domestic farmers from cheaper foreign competition. This keeps domestic firms in business and protects jobs, and it may give an infant industry time to grow until it can compete. Similarly, a national minimum wage raises the pay of low-paid workers above the market wage.

0455/22/F/M/21 Q2(d) · February/March 2021 · [8 marks]

Source material: The government is concerned that trying to reduce unemployment may increase India’s inflation rate.

Discuss whether or not a government can reduce unemployment without increasing inflation.

Unemployment is when people in the labour force who are willing and able to work cannot find a job. Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level over time.

Why it may be able to. If the government uses supply-side policy, such as spending more on education and training, workers become more skilled and productive. This makes it easier for unemployed people to find jobs, and higher productivity lowers firms’ costs per unit, so they can expand output without raising prices. Total (aggregate) supply rises alongside demand, so unemployment falls without inflation.

The state of the economy also matters. If there is a recession with many unemployed workers and spare capacity, firms can increase output by taking on unemployed workers who are willing to accept the current wage. A rise in total demand caused by more government spending then mainly raises output and employment rather than prices. Subsidies to firms can also help: they lower costs, so firms can take on more workers and keep prices down.

Why it may not be able to. Expansionary fiscal or monetary policy, such as cutting income tax or lowering interest rates, raises disposable income and spending, so total demand rises. If the economy is already close to full employment, firms cannot easily increase output, so the extra demand pulls up prices: demand-pull inflation.

As unemployment falls, firms also compete for a smaller pool of workers, and trade unions are in a stronger position to bargain for higher wages. Higher wages raise firms’ costs of production, and firms pass these on as higher prices, causing cost-push inflation. In addition, supply-side policies take a long time to work, so in the short run the government may have to rely on demand-side policies that are more likely to be inflationary.

Conclusion. A government can reduce unemployment without increasing inflation if the economy has a lot of spare capacity, as in a recession, or if it uses supply-side measures that raise total supply as well as demand. It is much harder when the economy is close to full employment and the government uses expansionary demand-side policy, since rising demand and wages are then likely to push up prices. For India, with growth already high, success depends on whether the government focuses on training and productivity rather than simply boosting spending.

Why this scores
This is a Level 3 answer: both terms defined, two developed arguments on each side, and a conclusion that depends on the level of spare capacity and the type of policy. The examiner report singled out answers that contrasted demand-side policy (risk of demand-pull and cost-push inflation) with supply-side policy, and warned that weaker answers skipped stages in the chain.

Economic growth is an increase in a country’s real GDP, that is, a rise in the output of goods and services over time.

Why a government should aim for high growth. Higher output means higher incomes on average. Households can afford more and better goods and services, such as better housing, food and healthcare, so material living standards rise. As firms produce more, they also need more workers, so unemployment falls, and fewer people live in poverty.

Growth also increases tax revenue without the government having to raise tax rates, because incomes, spending and profits all rise. Source material: Japan has faced significant periods of deflation in recent decades. Despite efforts to stimulate the economy through fiscal policy measures and monetary policy measures, economic growth remains low.

Discuss whether or not deflation is a problem for an economy.

Deflation is a sustained fall in the general price level, so the inflation rate is negative.

Why deflation may be a problem. If consumers expect prices to keep falling, they delay buying items such as cars, furniture and electronics, waiting for them to become cheaper. Total (aggregate) demand falls, so firms sell less, cut their prices further and reduce output. They need fewer workers, so unemployment rises and incomes fall, which reduces spending even more. This deflationary spiral can lead to a long period of low growth, as Japan has experienced. The diagram below shows this spiral.

Deflation also increases the real value of debt. Households and firms must repay a fixed amount that is now worth more in real terms, so they cut spending to pay off their debts. Firms’ revenues and profits fall with lower prices, so they invest less and confidence in the economy falls. Monetary policy also becomes less effective, because interest rates cannot easily be cut much below zero to encourage borrowing.

Why deflation may not be a problem. If prices are falling because costs of production are falling, for example because of new technology or higher productivity, firms can cut prices and still make a profit. Consumers’ real incomes rise, so they can afford more goods and services and living standards improve.

Lower prices can also make a country’s exports more internationally competitive, if its prices are falling faster than other countries’. Export sales rise, which can increase output and employment. A mild, short period of deflation is also unlikely to change consumers’ spending habits very much.

Conclusion. Deflation is a serious problem when it is caused by falling total demand and lasts a long time, because expectations of falling prices lead to a spiral of lower spending, output and employment, as Japan’s low growth suggests. It is much less of a problem, and can even be beneficial, when it is caused by falling costs and is mild and short-lived. The key factors are its cause, how long it lasts and whether people expect prices to keep falling.

Why this scores
Level 3: a definition, two developed arguments on each side (the deflationary spiral and debt against cost-driven deflation and competitiveness), and a conclusion that turns on the cause and length of the deflation, which the mark scheme names as the key evaluative point.

0455/21/M/J/24 Q2(d) · May/June 2024 · [8 marks]

Source material: The country’s economy experienced the world’s seventh highest economic growth rate, with an increase in GDP of 7.4%.

Discuss whether or not people living in countries with a high GDP enjoy higher living standards than people in countries with a low GDP.

GDP is the total value of output produced in a country in a year. Living standards are people’s quality of life, including both their material income and non-material factors such as health, education and the environment.

Why people in high-GDP countries might enjoy higher living standards. A high GDP usually means firms are producing a large amount of goods and services and paying out a large amount of income as wages and profits. People with higher incomes can afford better housing, a more varied diet and more consumer goods, so their material living standards are higher.

A high GDP also generates high tax revenue for the government. It can spend this on hospitals, schools, clean water and pensions. Better healthcare raises life expectancy, and better education gives people the skills for more interesting, better-paid jobs with better working conditions, so non-material living standards rise too.

Why they might not. A high GDP does not mean a high GDP per head. A country with a very large population, such as Bangladesh with over 170 million people, can have a high total GDP while the average income of each person is low. A small country such as Luxembourg has a much lower GDP but a far higher income per head and higher living standards.

Even where GDP per head is high, income may be very unevenly distributed. If a small group of rich people receives most of the income, many others may still live in poverty. In addition, high-GDP countries often have a higher cost of living, so the same income buys less, and high output can come with long working hours, stress, pollution and traffic congestion, which reduce quality of life. GDP in low-income countries may also under-record people’s real incomes, because a lot of activity takes place in the informal economy.

Conclusion. People in countries with a high GDP do not necessarily enjoy higher living standards. They are likely to be better off only if the high GDP is also a high real GDP per head once the cost of living is taken into account, if income is shared fairly evenly, and if the government uses its tax revenue on health and education rather than the gains being offset by pollution and long working hours. For Bangladesh, 7.4% growth in GDP will raise living standards most if it is faster than population growth and reaches low-income households.

Why this scores
This meets the Level 3 description: definitions first, two developed arguments on each side, and a conclusion that gives a clear judgement with the specific conditions (GDP per head, cost of living, distribution, use of tax revenue). The key evaluative point the mark scheme looks for is that high GDP is not the same as high GDP per head.

Source material: As cities experience economic growth, the buildings in the city become taller.

Discuss whether or not economic growth will improve living standards in a city.

Economic growth is an increase in a country’s or region’s real GDP, the output of goods and services. Living standards are the quality of life of people living in the city, including their income, health, housing and environment.

Why it might improve living standards in a city. When a city grows, existing firms expand and new offices, shops and construction projects appear, such as the taller buildings in the stimulus. This increases the demand for labour, so unemployment in the city falls and wages tend to rise. City residents then have more income to spend on better food, housing and leisure.

Growth also raises the tax revenue of the city and national government, because more people pay income tax and more is spent on goods subject to VAT. This revenue can fund better public transport, street lighting, hospitals and schools in the city, improving residents’ health and education. With more firms competing, residents also have a wider choice of shops, restaurants and entertainment.

Why it might not. Growth brings negative externalities. More production and more car ownership increase air pollution, noise and traffic congestion. City residents may suffer more breathing problems and spend hours a day commuting, which reduces their quality of life even though their incomes are higher.

A growing city also attracts migrants from rural areas and other countries looking for work. The demand for housing rises faster than new homes can be built, so rents and house prices rise and some people end up living in overcrowded or poor-quality housing. The benefits of growth may also go mainly to skilled workers and business owners, while low-skilled workers face higher prices without higher wages, so inequality and relative poverty in the city increase.

Source material: It is estimated that 9% of Hawaii’s population were living in poverty in 2022.

Discuss whether or not fiscal policy is effective in reducing poverty.

Fiscal policy is the use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy. Poverty is a lack of income to reach an acceptable standard of living, either absolute (unable to afford necessities) or relative (poor compared with others in the country).

Why fiscal policy might be effective. The government can raise the incomes of the poor directly by paying more generous state benefits, such as unemployment benefit and state pensions. With Hawaii’s ageing population, higher pensions would help retired people who cannot work to afford food, heating and medicine, reducing absolute poverty quickly.

Fiscal policy can also tackle the causes of poverty. Spending more on state education and training raises the skills of children from poor families, so they can get better-paid jobs in future. If this is financed by progressive taxation, the rich pay a higher proportion of their income and the money is redistributed to the poor, which narrows the income gap and reduces relative poverty. Cutting indirect taxes such as VAT on food also lowers the price of necessities, so low-income households can buy more.

Why it might not be effective. Extra spending has to be paid for through higher taxes or borrowing. Higher income taxes may discourage people from working or investing, and skilled workers may leave. In Hawaii, high labour mobility between US states means young workers can easily move away, so the tax base shrinks and there is less revenue to spend on the poor.

Expansionary fiscal policy can also be inflationary. Hawaii already has rising prices because of growth in tourism, and if extra government spending increases total demand further, the prices of necessities such as housing and food rise faster. The poor then lose purchasing power, which can increase poverty. In addition, generous benefits may reduce the incentive to find work, and education spending takes many years to affect incomes.

Conclusion. Fiscal policy can be effective in reducing poverty, especially absolute poverty, if spending is targeted at those who need it most, for example pensions for the elderly and free education and healthcare for low-income families, and is financed by progressive taxes. It is less effective when inflation is already high, as in Hawaii, when taxes push mobile workers to leave, or when benefits are designed in a way that discourages work.

Why this scores
Level 3: definitions of both key terms, two developed arguments on each side using Hawaii’s ageing population, inflation and labour mobility, and a conclusion that judges which fiscal measures work and under what conditions. Life expectancy is one of the three components of the Human Development Index, so a country with better healthcare will have a higher HDI value and a higher level of development. This creates more jobs, reduces unemployment and poverty, and raises tax revenue that the government can spend on education. In a country with poor healthcare, the opposite cycle keeps incomes and development low.

The examiner report praised answers that focused on how healthcare affects productivity, incomes and living standards; this answer does that and adds life expectancy and HDI. 0455/23/O/N/24 Q4(c) · October/November 2024 · [6 marks]

Analyse the causes of differences in economic development between countries.

Economic development is an improvement in the living standards of a country’s people. It is not the same as economic growth: a country’s output can rise without most people becoming better off. Countries differ in development for several reasons.

Saving and investment. In low-income countries, most income is spent on necessities, so people can save very little. With little saving, banks have few funds to lend for investment, so firms cannot buy modern machinery and technology. Workers’ productivity stays low, which keeps incomes low, and the cycle continues. High-income countries can save and invest more, raising productivity and income per head. The diagram shows this low-income cycle.

The size of each sector. Less developed countries usually have a large primary sector, with many people in subsistence farming. Farming produces low value added, and incomes are low and depend on the weather. More developed countries have large secondary and tertiary sectors, where output per worker and wages are higher, so average incomes and living standards are higher.

Population growth. A country with a rapidly growing population has many young dependants. Income must be shared among more people, so income per head grows slowly, and the government has to stretch spending on schools and healthcare across a rising number of children. A country with slower population growth can raise income per head more easily.

Education. Countries with better education have more skilled workers, who are more productive and can do higher-paid jobs. This raises incomes and attracts foreign firms, creating more jobs, whereas poorly educated workforces are limited to low-skilled, low-paid work.

It defines economic development and states that it is not the same as economic growth, a distinction the mark scheme insists on. It then analyses four separate differences, each ending with income per head or living standards, which provides the logical chains the top marks need.

Discuss whether or not increased government spending on education will lead to economic development.

Economic development is an improvement in the living standards of a country’s people, measured by indicators such as income per head, life expectancy and years of schooling (for example the HDI).

Why it might lead to economic development. More government spending on education, such as building schools, training teachers and funding universities, raises people’s skills and qualifications. A more skilled workforce is more productive, so workers can get better-paid jobs and unemployment falls. Higher incomes mean people can afford more goods and services, raising their living standards. In Guyana, a better-educated workforce could take skilled jobs in the growing oil industry rather than these jobs going to foreign workers.

Education also improves development directly and indirectly. Years of schooling are part of the HDI, so the HDI rises. Educated people tend to make healthier choices and consume fewer demerit goods such as cigarettes, so life expectancy rises. An educated workforce also attracts investment from foreign multinationals, which increases the country’s productive capacity and creates more jobs.

This fits Level 3: definition, two developed points on each side (skills, productivity and incomes; HDI, health and FDI against time lags, opportunity cost, inefficiency and brain drain) and a conclusion that judges the time period and the conditions. 0455/23/M/J/25 Q5(c) · May/June 2025 · [6 marks]

Source material: This agency also aims to coordinate trade between African countries to help technological development.

Analyse the benefits a country may gain from exporting to and importing from other countries.

International trade is the buying and selling of goods and services across national borders: exports are sold abroad and imports are bought from abroad.

Benefits of exporting. Exporting lets a country specialise in what it is best at producing. Its workers build up skills and its firms become more efficient, so output rises. Selling abroad also gives firms a much larger market than the home country alone, so they can grow and gain economies of scale, which lowers their average cost and lets them charge more competitive prices.

Exports are also a component of total (aggregate) demand. When foreign buyers spend more on a country’s products, domestic firms raise output and take on more workers, which causes economic growth and reduces unemployment. The export revenue also brings foreign currency into the country, which helps the current account of the balance of payments.

Benefits of importing. Imports give consumers a wider choice of goods and services, often at lower prices than domestic firms could offer, which raises living standards. Firms can import raw materials and capital goods that the country cannot produce, or cannot produce cheaply, which lowers their costs of production.

Imports also bring competition, which puts pressure on domestic firms to cut costs and improve quality. Trading with other countries spreads ideas and technology, as the African Space Agency hopes, which raises productivity and allows the economy to produce more in the future. On a production possibility curve (PPC) diagram, this appears as an outward shift of the curve.

Why this scores
It defines international trade, then analyses exports and imports separately, as the examiner report advised, linking each to a macroeconomic outcome (growth, employment, living standards, costs). Specialisation at a national level means a country concentrating its resources on producing a narrow range of goods and services, then trading for the rest.

Source material: The intention is to raise economic growth through more international trade.

Discuss whether or not increased international trade can promote economic growth.

Economic growth is an increase in a country’s real GDP, its output of goods and services. International trade is the exchange of goods and services between countries, through exports and imports.

Why it might promote economic growth. Lower trade barriers give domestic firms access to a much larger market. Small and medium-sized firms in ASEAN countries can sell to millions more customers, so exports rise. Exports are an injection into total (aggregate) demand, so firms increase output and employ more workers, and real GDP rises.

Selling to a larger market also lets firms specialise and gain economies of scale, which lowers their average costs. Imports of cheaper raw materials and capital goods reduce costs further, while foreign competition pushes domestic firms to become more efficient. Lower costs and higher productivity increase the economy’s productive capacity, so growth can be sustained in the long run.

Why it might not promote economic growth. More trade also means more imports. If consumers switch from domestic products to cheaper foreign ones, spending leaks out of the economy, total demand for domestic output falls and real GDP may fall rather than rise. The diagram sets this chain beside the export chain above.

An economy that depends more on trade is also more exposed to sudden falls in world demand, such as a recession in its main trading partners, which can reduce its output.

Conclusion. Increased international trade is likely to promote growth when the country’s firms are competitive enough for exports to rise faster than imports and can use the larger market to gain economies of scale. It is less likely to do so in the short run for a country whose industries are inefficient or still developing, where rising imports and closures may outweigh export gains until resources move into the industries where it has an advantage.

Source material: For example, in 2018 India imposed a tariff of approximately 90% on imports of coffee to protect its coffee farmers.

Discuss whether or not high tariffs can reduce unemployment.

A tariff is a tax on imports. Unemployment is when people who are willing and able to work cannot find a job.

Why high tariffs might reduce unemployment. A high tariff, such as India’s 90% tariff on coffee, raises the price of imports. Consumers switch to domestically produced substitutes, so demand for Indian coffee rises. Domestic producers increase output and, since the demand for labour is a derived demand, they employ more workers. The diagram shows the first step: the tariff reduces the supply of imported coffee and raises its price.

High tariffs also raise tax revenue for the government. It could spend this on education and training, which makes workers more skilled and occupationally mobile, or on subsidies to domestic firms. Both could reduce unemployment, especially structural unemployment.

Why high tariffs might not reduce unemployment. Other countries may retaliate with tariffs on the first country’s exports. Its exports become more expensive abroad, foreign demand for them falls and export industries cut jobs, which may cancel out the jobs saved in protected industries.

In addition, protected firms face less competition, so they may become inefficient and raise prices rather than expand output. Source material: Some economists suggest that the Chadian Government should impose import quotas to reduce its trade in goods deficit.

Discuss whether or not imposing import quotas will reduce a trade in goods deficit.

An import quota is a limit on the quantity or value of a product that can be imported over a period. A trade in goods deficit occurs when spending on imports of goods is greater than revenue from exports of goods.

Why quotas might reduce the deficit. A quota directly restricts the quantity of goods coming into the country. With fewer imports allowed in, the amount spent on imports falls, so the gap between import spending and export revenue narrows.

Because imports are scarcer, their price rises. Consumers and firms then switch to domestically produced substitutes. Domestic firms sell more and may expand, which further reduces the need for imports and could, in time, allow them to export.

Why quotas might not reduce the deficit. If the quota is set above the quantity currently imported, it has no effect. If it only covers a few goods, total import spending changes little. A low-income country such as Chad may also have few domestic substitutes for products like machinery and fuel, so firms and households must still buy imports, and when supply is restricted they may pay higher prices for each unit.

Trading partners may retaliate by restricting Chad’s exports, which reduces export revenue and can leave the deficit just as large. Quotas may also encourage smuggling. If imported raw materials and capital goods are restricted, Chad’s own producers face higher costs, which makes its exports less competitive.

Conclusion. Import quotas can reduce a trade in goods deficit in the short run if they cover a wide range of products for which domestic substitutes exist and trading partners do not retaliate. For Chad, which lacks substitutes for many imported goods and would harm its own producers by restricting imported inputs, quotas are unlikely to reduce the deficit by much. Raising the quality and quantity of its exports would be a more lasting solution.

0455/21/M/J/25 Q5(d) · May/June 2025 · [8 marks]

Source material: The Australian Government imposes relatively low import tariffs and the country has a floating exchange rate.

Discuss whether or not having a floating exchange rate benefits an economy.

A floating exchange rate is one whose value is determined by the market forces of demand for and supply of the currency, without the government or central bank fixing it.

Why it might benefit an economy. A floating rate can correct a current account imbalance automatically. If a country has a deficit, it is supplying more of its currency to buy imports than foreigners demand to buy its exports, so the exchange rate falls. Its exports become cheaper and its imports more expensive, so exports rise, imports fall and the deficit shrinks without the government having to act. The diagram shows the fall in the exchange rate that starts this process.

The government and central bank do not have to keep large reserves of foreign currency to defend a fixed value, and they do not need to change interest rates to protect the exchange rate. This leaves interest rates free to be used for other macroeconomic aims, such as controlling inflation or reducing unemployment.

Why it might not benefit an economy. A floating rate can change frequently and unpredictably. Firms do not know how much they will earn from exports or pay for imports, which makes planning difficult and can discourage trade and investment, including investment by foreign MNCs.

Speculation can make the swings larger. If traders expect the currency to fall, they sell it, which makes it fall further than the economy’s trade position would justify. A sharp fall raises import prices and can cause cost-push inflation, while a sharp rise makes exports uncompetitive and can cause unemployment in export industries.

Conclusion. A floating exchange rate is likely to benefit a large, diversified economy with well-developed financial markets, such as Australia, where firms can protect themselves against currency changes and the automatic adjustment of the current account is valuable. It is less beneficial for a small economy that relies on one export or on imported essentials, where large swings in the currency cause damaging uncertainty and inflation.

Why this scores
Level 3: a definition, then developed arguments that are about the floating system itself (automatic correction, no reserves needed, freedom for interest rates; uncertainty, speculation) rather than just a strong or weak currency, which the examiner report says limited many answers. The report singled out automatic correction of the current account as the mark of the best responses.

0455/23/M/J/22 Q4(b) · May/June 2022 · [4 marks]

Source material: Despite having more imports of goods than exports of goods, it still records a surplus on the current account of its balance of payments.

Explain how a country with more imports of goods than exports of goods can still record a surplus on the current account of its balance of payments.

The current account has four parts: trade in goods, trade in services, primary income and secondary income. Importing more goods than it exports gives a country a deficit on trade in goods, but the overall current account can still be in surplus if the other three parts together have a larger surplus.

0455/22/M/J/23 Q5(d) · May/June 2023 · [8 marks]

Source material: In 2019, China’s economic growth rate was 6.1% and Chinese households increased their spending.

Discuss whether or not a country with a high economic growth rate will have a deficit on the current account of its balance of payments.

Economic growth is an increase in real GDP. A deficit on the current account occurs when a country’s spending on imports and other debit items exceeds its earnings from exports and other credit items.

Why it might have a deficit. Higher growth raises incomes, so households are both able and more willing to spend more, including on imported goods and on services such as foreign holidays. As spending on imports rises faster than export earnings, more money flows out than in. Firms expanding production also import more raw materials and capital goods.

A fast-growing home market can also draw goods away from exports, as firms find it easier to sell at home. If total demand grows faster than the economy’s ability to produce, prices rise and the country’s products become less price-competitive abroad, so exports fall and imports rise.

Why it might not have a deficit. The growth may be export-led. If the economy is growing because foreign demand for its products is rising, as with China, export revenue increases along with output, so the current account may move into surplus.

Growth also encourages firms to invest in new capital and technology, which raises productivity, lowers costs and improves quality. Higher tax revenue from growth can fund education and healthcare, raising labour productivity. Both make domestic products more competitive, so exports rise and consumers switch away from imports.

Conclusion. A country with a high growth rate is likely to have a current account deficit when growth is driven by household spending and it relies on imported consumer goods and inputs. It is unlikely to have a deficit when growth is export-led and accompanied by rising productivity, as in China, where strong export earnings outweigh the extra imports that higher incomes bring.

Why this scores
Level 3: definitions, two developed arguments on each side with clear links to imports and exports, and a conclusion that depends on what is driving the growth. The examiner report criticised answers that said exports would rise without explaining why, and those that confused a current account deficit with a budget deficit.

0455/22/O/N/25 Q5(d) · October/November 2025 · [8 marks]

Source material: The country has one of the world’s largest deficits on the current account of its balance of payments (as a percentage of GDP). Some economists suggest cutting taxes would reduce this deficit.

Discuss whether or not cuts in taxes will reduce a deficit on the current account of a country’s balance of payments.

A current account deficit is when a country’s spending on imports and other debit items is greater than its earnings from exports and other credit items. Tax cuts could be cuts in income tax, corporation tax, indirect taxes or tariffs.

Why tax cuts might reduce the deficit. A cut in corporation tax leaves firms with more profit after tax, so they are more able and willing to invest in new machinery and technology. This raises productivity and quality and lowers costs, so their products become more competitive. Exports rise and consumers buy domestic products instead of imports, which reduces the deficit. Lower corporation tax may also attract export-oriented MNCs to the country.

A cut in income tax increases the reward from working, which may motivate workers to work harder, raising productivity and lowering costs. Cutting tariffs on imported raw materials and components lowers firms’ costs too, so they can cut the prices of their exports.

Why tax cuts might not reduce the deficit. A cut in income tax raises disposable income, and some of this extra income is spent on imports, so import spending may rise and the deficit may widen. Higher spending also increases total demand, which may cause demand-pull inflation and make the country’s products less price-competitive abroad.

Tax cuts also reduce government revenue, so the government may have to spend less on education, healthcare and transport, which harms productivity in the long run. In addition, if foreign MNCs are attracted, they send their profits home, which is a debit on primary income and adds to the current account deficit.

Conclusion. Tax cuts are more likely to reduce the deficit if they are targeted at firms, such as lower corporation tax linked to investment or cheaper imported inputs for exporters, because these raise competitiveness. Cuts in income tax are likely to make the deficit worse in the short run, since much of the extra spending will go on imports. The overall effect depends on which taxes are cut and on whether higher investment raises productivity quickly enough to outweigh the extra spending on imports.

Why this scores
Level 3: definitions, developed arguments on both sides that distinguish between types of tax, and a conclusion that judges which tax cuts would work and why. The mark scheme rewards recognising that different taxes have different effects, and the MNC profit point links to primary income.

0455/22/M/J/20 Q2(b) · May/June 2020 · [4 marks]

Explain two influences on whether demand for a product is price-elastic or price-inelastic.

Availability of close substitutes. If a product has many close substitutes, consumers can easily switch when its price rises. A small rise in price therefore causes a large percentage fall in quantity demanded, so demand is price-elastic. A product with few or no close substitutes, such as electricity, has price-inelastic demand.

Proportion of income spent on the product. If a product takes up a large share of a consumer’s income, such as a car, a price rise has a noticeable effect on what they can afford, so they cut their purchases significantly and demand is price-elastic. If the product takes only a small share of income, such as salt or matches, a price rise is hardly noticed, so quantity demanded changes very little and demand is price-inelastic.

Why this scores
Each influence is identified (1) and then explained by showing which way it pushes PED and why (1). Showing both the elastic and the inelastic case makes the explanation secure.

Analyse [6]

0455/22/F/M/24 Q4(c) · February/March 2024 · [6 marks]

Source material: The delivery of luxury chocolate and salt, for example, was delayed. These two goods have differences in their price elasticity of demand.

Analyse the reasons why the price elasticity of demand for one brand of luxury chocolates is likely to be different from that of salt.

Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in price. Demand for one brand of luxury chocolates is likely to be price-elastic, while demand for salt is likely to be price-inelastic.

Substitutes and breadth of definition. One brand of luxury chocolates is narrowly defined, so it has many close substitutes: other luxury chocolate brands and other gifts such as flowers. If its price rises, many consumers switch to a rival brand, so quantity demanded falls by a greater percentage than the rise in price (PED greater than 1). Salt is a widely defined product with almost no substitutes in cooking, so consumers keep buying it and quantity demanded falls by a smaller percentage (PED less than 1).

Necessity or luxury, and postponing the purchase. Luxury chocolates are a want, not a need, and buying them can easily be postponed or skipped if they become more expensive. Salt is seen as a necessity for cooking and preserving food, so households cannot easily cut back. This makes demand for the chocolates more responsive to price than demand for salt.

Proportion of income. A box of luxury chocolates takes up a noticeable proportion of income, so a price rise is felt and consumers react. Salt is very cheap and is bought infrequently, so a price change is hardly noticed and has very little effect on the quantity bought.

However, the difference may be smaller if consumers are very loyal to the chocolate brand, for example because of strong advertising. Loyal customers see few true substitutes, which would make demand for that brand less elastic.

Why this scores
It starts with a definition, then analyses three determinants and applies each one to both products, ending each chain with what happens to quantity demanded and the PED value. The brand-loyalty point is credited by the mark scheme and shows a deeper understanding.

0455/23/M/J/21 Q5(c) · May/June 2021 · [6 marks]

Source material: The demand for smartphones has become more price-inelastic as the range of functions available has increased.

Analyse the advantages of selling a product which is price-inelastic in demand.

A product is price-inelastic in demand when a change in its price causes a smaller percentage change in quantity demanded, so PED is less than 1. Smartphones are an example: as their range of functions has grown, people see them as essential and have few alternatives.

Higher revenue from a price rise. With inelastic demand, price and total revenue move in the same direction. If a firm raises its price from P1 to P2, quantity demanded falls only slightly from Q1 to Q2. The revenue gained on the units still sold is greater than the revenue lost on the units no longer sold, so total revenue rises (see diagram).

Higher profit. Because the firm sells slightly fewer units after the price rise, its total costs of production may fall at the same time as its revenue rises. Profit is the difference between total revenue and total cost, so profit is likely to increase.

Ability to pass on higher costs. If the firm’s costs rise, for example because of higher wages or an indirect tax, it can raise its price without losing many customers. It can protect its profit margins in a way that a firm with elastic demand could not.

Stable revenue and easier planning. Demand for an inelastic product changes little when prices change, so the firm’s sales and revenue are more predictable. This makes it easier to plan output, borrow money and invest in new products.

Why this scores
Defines price-inelastic demand with an example, then builds full chains of analysis: price rise → small fall in quantity → revenue rises → profit rises. 0455/22/O/N/25 Q2(c) · October/November 2025 · [6 marks]

Source material: The country produces a wide range of goods and services with different degrees of price elasticity of demand.

Analyse how price elasticity of demand can influence a firm’s pricing decisions.

Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in price. A firm uses its PED to predict what will happen to its total revenue when it changes its price.

Elastic demand: lower the price. If demand is price-elastic (PED greater than 1), a fall in price causes a greater percentage rise in quantity demanded. Total revenue therefore rises when the price is cut. This is likely when the firm’s product has close substitutes, is not a necessity or takes up a large share of consumers’ incomes. A firm in this position is encouraged to lower its price, or at least avoid raising it, because a price rise would cause many customers to switch to rivals and revenue would fall.

Inelastic demand: raise the price. If demand is price-inelastic (PED less than 1), a rise in price causes a smaller percentage fall in quantity demanded, so total revenue rises. This is likely when the product has few substitutes, is a necessity or is a small share of income. The firm is encouraged to raise its price, because it will lose relatively few sales and its revenue will increase.

Price discrimination. If a firm sells to groups of customers with different PEDs, it can charge different prices for the same product. For example, a train company charges higher fares at peak times, when commuters must travel and demand is inelastic, and lower fares off-peak, when leisure travellers can choose another time or another form of transport and demand is elastic. This raises the firm’s total revenue from both groups.

Why this scores
Opens with a definition, then analyses both cases fully: the PED value, why demand might be that elastic, the pricing decision and the effect on revenue. Price discrimination with a real example is a separate developed point that the mark scheme credits.

0455/22/M/J/25 Q2(d) · May/June 2025 · [8 marks]

Discuss whether or not the demand for chocolate is likely to be price elastic.

Demand is price-elastic when a change in price causes a greater percentage change in quantity demanded, so PED is greater than 1.

Why demand may be price-elastic. There are many close substitutes for chocolate, both other chocolate brands and other snacks such as biscuits or sweets. If one firm raises the price of its chocolate bars, consumers can easily switch to a rival brand, so quantity demanded falls by a larger percentage than the price rise. This applies especially to individual brands, which are much more price-elastic than chocolate as a whole.

Chocolate is also a luxury rather than a necessity. Nobody needs it to live, so if the price rises, especially for premium chocolate, consumers can cut back, buy it less often or postpone the purchase. Over a longer time period, consumers also have more opportunity to find alternatives and change their habits, so demand becomes more elastic over time.

Why demand may be price-inelastic. A chocolate bar is cheap and takes up only a small proportion of most people’s income, particularly in high-income countries. A 10% rise in the price of a bar is hardly noticed, so consumers keep buying roughly the same amount and quantity demanded falls by a smaller percentage than the price.

In addition, chocolate contains sugar and many people buy it out of habit, so for regular consumers it can be habit-forming. Strong brand loyalty, built up by advertising, also means consumers may stick with their favourite brand even when its price rises. Both make demand less responsive to price.

Conclusion. Demand for one particular brand of chocolate is likely to be price-elastic, because so many close substitutes exist. However, demand for chocolate as a whole is likely to be price-inelastic in high-income countries, because it is cheap relative to incomes and habit-forming. The answer depends on how widely the product is defined, the income of consumers, whether it is an everyday bar or an expensive luxury box, and the time period being considered.

Why this scores
This matches the Level 3 description: a definition first, two developed arguments on each side, each linked back to the size of the change in quantity demanded, and a conclusion that makes a judgement and states what it depends on (breadth of definition, income, type of chocolate, time). The examiner report praised answers that kept PED, not “more or less demand”, at the centre.

Source material: It is predicted that the price elasticity of demand (PED) for cars will also change in the future.

Discuss whether or not demand for cars will become more price-elastic in the future.

Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in price. Demand would become more price-elastic if a change in the price of cars caused a bigger percentage change in the quantity of cars demanded than it does now.

Why demand may become more price-elastic. Substitutes for owning a car are improving. Many cities are investing in faster and cheaper public transport, such as metro lines, and ride-hailing and car-sharing apps are spreading. If these alternatives become cheaper and more convenient, consumers can more easily choose not to buy a car when car prices rise, so quantity demanded will fall by a larger percentage.

There may also be more car producers and models to choose from, for example new firms making electric cars. With more close substitutes within the market, a rise in the price of one brand will lead buyers to switch to another. Consumers can also compare prices online easily. In addition, if car prices rise faster than incomes, cars will take up a larger proportion of income, which also makes demand more elastic. On a diagram, more price-elastic demand is a flatter demand curve, as shown below.

Why demand may not become more price-elastic. For many people a car may become more of a necessity. If people move further from where they work, or live in rural areas with poor public transport, they have no real alternative, so they will keep buying cars even if prices rise. Habit also matters: people who are used to the convenience of driving are reluctant to give it up.

Furthermore, as incomes rise over time, in many economies a car takes up a smaller proportion of income than it used to. A price rise is then less noticeable, so the quantity demanded responds less. Strong brand loyalty to car makers may have the same effect.

Conclusion. Demand for cars is likely to become more price-elastic in large cities, where public transport and ride-sharing provide closer substitutes, and for individual brands as competition grows. It may become less elastic in rural areas or where incomes are rising quickly. Overall, it depends mainly on how good and how cheap the alternatives to car ownership become, and on how car prices change relative to incomes.

Why this scores
Starts with a definition, which the examiner report says helped strong candidates stay focused, and keeps every argument about the responsiveness of quantity demanded, not about whether demand rises or falls. It has two developed arguments on each side and a conclusion that depends on specific conditions (location, substitutes, prices relative to incomes).
