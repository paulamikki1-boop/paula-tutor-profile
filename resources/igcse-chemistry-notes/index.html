Topic 1 · Particles & Structure

States of Matter

How the arrangement, movement and energy of particles explain the properties of solids, liquids and gases, and why substances change state or spread out by diffusion. These ideas underpin almost every later topic, and examiners reward precise particle language.

The three states

All matter is made of tiny particles (atoms, molecules or ions). The kinetic particle theory says these particles are always moving, and the amount of movement depends on how much kinetic energy they have.

Changes of state

Changes of state are physical changes: no new substance is made and they are reversible.

Melting
Solid → liquid, at the melting point
Freezing
Liquid → solid, at the same temperature as melting
Boiling
Liquid → gas throughout the liquid, at the boiling point
Evaporation
Liquid → gas at the surface only, below the boiling point
Condensation
Gas → liquid on cooling
Sublimation
Solid → gas directly (e.g. solid CO2, iodine)

Explaining changes of state with particles

  • Melting: heating gives particles more kinetic energy; they vibrate more until they overcome some of the forces holding them in fixed positions and can move past each other.
  • Boiling: particles gain enough energy to overcome the attractive forces between them completely and escape as a gas; bubbles of gas form throughout the liquid.
  • Evaporation: only the most energetic particles at the surface escape, so it can happen at any temperature. It is faster when the liquid is warmer, has a larger surface area, or when air moves over it.
  • Condensing and freezing: particles lose kinetic energy, slow down and the attractive forces pull them closer together.
Common Mistake
Do not say particles themselves “expand”, “melt” or “get bigger” when heated. The particles stay the same size; it is the spacing and movement that change. Also avoid saying bonds break when a simple molecular substance boils — it is the forces between molecules that are overcome.

Supplement Heating and cooling curves

If a pure solid is heated steadily and its temperature is recorded against time, the graph shows sloping sections and flat sections.

1
Solid warming upTemperature rises; particles vibrate faster.
2
Flat section at melting pointTemperature stays constant. Energy supplied is used to overcome forces of attraction between particles, not to raise kinetic energy. Solid and liquid are both present.
3
Liquid warming upTemperature rises again; particles move faster.
4
Flat section at boiling pointTemperature constant while energy overcomes the remaining attractive forces. Liquid and gas both present. This plateau is longer than the melting one because more energy is needed.
5
Gas warming upTemperature rises as gas particles move faster.
  • A cooling curve is the reverse: the flat sections occur at the boiling (condensing) point and freezing point, where energy is released as forces of attraction form, so the temperature stays constant.
  • A pure substance melts and boils at a sharp, fixed temperature. An impure substance melts over a range and at a lower temperature, and boils at a higher temperature.
Examiner Tip
For the flat sections, the credited explanation is that energy is used to overcome the forces of attraction between particles (or is released as these forces form during cooling). Simply writing “the substance is changing state” describes but does not explain.

Supplement Effect of temperature and pressure on the volume of a gas

  • Increasing temperature (at constant pressure): particles gain kinetic energy, move faster and hit the container walls harder and more often, so the gas expands — volume increases.
  • Increasing pressure (at constant temperature): particles are pushed closer together, so the volume decreases. Gases can be compressed because there is lots of space between particles.
  • In a sealed container of fixed volume, raising the temperature increases the pressure, because particles collide with the walls more frequently and with more force.

Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, as a result of their random motion. It happens in gases and liquids (very slowly in liquids) but not noticeably in solids.

Evidence for diffusion

  • A purple crystal of potassium manganate(VII) placed in water: the colour slowly spreads through all the water without stirring.
  • A smell (e.g. perfume) spreads across a room.
  • Bromine gas in a gas jar spreads upwards into a jar of air above it until the brown colour is even throughout.

Supplement Effect of relative molecular mass on rate of diffusion

  • At the same temperature, gas particles with a lower relative molecular mass (Mr) move faster on average, so they diffuse faster.
  • Higher temperature also increases the rate of diffusion because all particles move faster.

The ammonia and hydrogen chloride experiment

1
Set upPlace cotton wool soaked in concentrated ammonia solution at one end of a long glass tube, and cotton wool soaked in concentrated hydrochloric acid at the other. Seal both ends with bungs.
2
ObserveAfter a few minutes a white ring (smoke) of ammonium chloride forms inside the tube: NH3(g) + HCl(g) → NH4Cl(s)
3
ConcludeThe ring forms closer to the HCl end. NH3 (Mr = 17) has a lower Mr than HCl (Mr = 36.5), so ammonia molecules move faster and travel further in the same time.
Model answer
In the experiment above, explain why the white solid forms nearer to the hydrochloric acid end of the tube. [3]
Both gases diffuse along the tube by the random movement of their particles. Ammonia has a lower relative molecular mass (17) than hydrogen chloride (36.5), so ammonia molecules move faster at the same temperature. Ammonia therefore travels further along the tube before the two gases meet and react to form ammonium chloride, so the ring is closer to the hydrochloric acid end.
Common Mistake
Writing that ammonia is “lighter” or “less dense” is too vague. Use the term lower relative molecular mass and link it to the particles moving faster.

Topic 2 · Particles & Structure

Atoms, Elements and Compounds

What atoms are made of, how electrons are arranged, and how atoms join by ionic, covalent and metallic bonding. Structure and bonding questions appear on every paper, and explaining properties from structure is a favourite source of 3–4 mark questions.

Elements, compounds and mixtures

Element
A substance made of only one type of atom
Compound
A substance made of two or more elements chemically combined
Mixture
Two or more substances not chemically combined
  • A compound has different properties from the elements it contains and a fixed composition; it can only be separated by a chemical reaction.
  • A mixture keeps the properties of its components and can be separated by physical methods (filtration, distillation, chromatography).

Atomic structure

An atom has a tiny central nucleus containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons in shells.

ParticleRelative chargeRelative massLocation
Proton+11Nucleus
Neutron01Nucleus
Electron−11/1840 (negligible)Shells around the nucleus
  • Proton number (atomic number, Z): the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.
  • Mass number (nucleon number, A): the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.
  • Number of neutrons = mass number − proton number. Atoms are neutral, so number of electrons = number of protons.
  • Notation: 2311Na has 11 protons, 11 electrons and 12 neutrons.

Electronic configuration (first 20 elements)

  • Shells fill in order: first shell holds up to 2, second up to 8, third up to 8 (for elements 1–20).
  • Examples: C 2,4; O 2,6; Na 2,8,1; Cl 2,8,7; Ca 2,8,8,2.
  • The number of occupied shells = the period number.
  • The number of outer-shell electrons = the group number (Groups I–VII).
  • Noble gases (Group VIII) have a full outer shell, which makes them unreactive. Helium has 2 outer electrons; the others have 8.

Isotopes

Isotopes are different atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

  • Example: 3517Cl (18 neutrons) and 3717Cl (20 neutrons).
  • Isotopes of an element have the same chemical properties.
  • Supplement This is because they have the same number of electrons in their outer shell (the same electronic configuration), and chemical reactions involve electrons.

Supplement Calculating relative atomic mass

Relative atomic mass (Ar) is the weighted average mass of the isotopes of an element, using their relative abundances.

Worked example
Chlorine is 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37. Calculate Ar.
Ar = (35 × 75 + 37 × 25) ÷ 100
= (2625 + 925) ÷ 100
= 3550 ÷ 100
= 35.5

Ions and ionic bonding

  • An ion is a charged particle formed when an atom gains or loses electrons.
  • Cations are positive ions, formed when atoms (usually metals) lose electrons. Anions are negative ions, formed when atoms (usually non-metals) gain electrons.
  • An ionic bond is a strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
  • Ionic bonds form between metals and non-metals. Electrons are transferred from the metal to the non-metal so both gain a full outer shell (noble gas configuration).
1
Sodium atom, Na 2,8,1Loses its one outer electron to become Na+ (2,8).
2
Chlorine atom, Cl 2,8,7Gains that electron to become Cl− (2,8,8).
3
Dot-and-cross diagramDraw the ions in square brackets with the charge outside the top right; show the transferred electron as a cross among the dots of the chloride ion. Usually only outer shells need to be shown.
  • Core: dot-and-cross diagrams for Group I metals with Group VII non-metals (e.g. NaCl, KBr).
  • Supplement Dot-and-cross diagrams for other metal/non-metal combinations, e.g. MgO (Mg2+ and O2−, two electrons transferred) and CaCl2 (one Ca2+ and two Cl−).
  • Supplement An ionic compound has a giant lattice structure: a regular arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions, e.g. sodium chloride.

Properties of ionic compounds

  • High melting and boiling points.
  • Good electrical conductivity when molten or in aqueous solution; poor when solid.
  • Supplement Explanation: many strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions in the giant lattice need a lot of energy to overcome. When solid the ions are held in fixed positions; when molten or dissolved the ions are free to move and carry charge.

Simple molecules and covalent bonds

  • A covalent bond forms when a pair of electrons is shared between two atoms, giving each a noble gas electronic configuration. Covalent bonding occurs between non-metal atoms.
  • Core dot-and-cross diagrams: H2, Cl2, H2O, CH4, NH3, HCl.
  • Supplement Also: CH3OH, C2H4 (one C=C double bond), O2 (double bond), CO2 (two C=O double bonds), N2 (triple bond).

Properties of simple molecular substances

  • Low melting and boiling points (many are gases or liquids at room temperature).
  • Poor electrical conductivity: there are no ions or free electrons to carry charge.
  • Supplement Explanation: the covalent bonds within molecules are strong, but the intermolecular forces (attractive forces between molecules) are weak, so little energy is needed to separate the molecules. Larger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces and higher boiling points.
Common Mistake
Saying water has a low boiling point because “covalent bonds are weak” loses the mark. Covalent bonds are strong; it is the weak forces between molecules that are overcome on boiling.

  • Both have very high melting points because many strong covalent bonds must be broken.
  • Supplement Silicon(IV) oxide, SiO2, has a giant covalent structure similar to diamond: each Si is bonded to 4 O atoms and each O to 2 Si atoms. It is hard, has a very high melting point and does not conduct electricity.

Supplement Metallic bonding

  • Metallic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between a lattice of positive metal ions and a “sea” of delocalised electrons.
  • Good electrical conductivity: the delocalised electrons are free to move through the structure and carry charge.
  • Malleable and ductile: the layers of positive ions can slide over each other without breaking the metallic bonding, because the delocalised electrons still hold them together.
Examiner Tip
When explaining conductivity always name the mobile charge carrier: ions for molten or dissolved ionic compounds, delocalised electrons for metals and graphite. Never say ions move in a metal or electrons move in an electrolyte.
Model answer
Explain why sodium chloride conducts electricity when molten but not when solid. [3]
Sodium chloride is made of Na+ and Cl− ions held in a giant ionic lattice. In the solid, the ions are held in fixed positions by strong electrostatic attractions and cannot move. When molten, the lattice breaks down and the ions are free to move, so they can carry charge through the liquid.

Topic 3 · Quantitative Chemistry

Stoichiometry

Writing formulae and balanced equations, and using relative masses and the mole to calculate amounts of substances. Calculation questions carry many marks on the Extended paper, and method marks are awarded for clear working even if the final answer is wrong.

Formulae

  • The molecular formula is the number and type of different atoms in one molecule, e.g. C2H6 for ethane.
  • Supplement The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of the different atoms or ions in a compound, e.g. CH3 for ethane.
  • You should be able to deduce a formula from a diagram or model by counting each type of atom.
  • Ionic formulae come from ion charges: the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge.
Positive ionsNegative ions
Na+, K+, H+, NH4+, Ag+Cl−, Br−, OH−, NO3−
Mg2+, Ca2+, Cu2+, Zn2+, Fe2+O2−, S2−, SO42−, CO32−
Al3+, Fe3+N3−, PO43−

Examples: Al3+ + O2− gives Al2O3; Ca2+ + NO3− gives Ca(NO3)2. Roman numerals give the charge: iron(III) chloride is FeCl3.

Equations

1
Word equationmagnesium + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride + hydrogen
2
Correct formulaeMg + HCl → MgCl2 + H2 (never change a formula to balance)
3
Balance with big numbers in frontMg + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2
4
Add state symbolsMg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g). (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water.
  • Supplement Ionic equations show only the ions that change; spectator ions are left out. Neutralisation: H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l). Precipitation: Ag+(aq) + Cl−(aq) → AgCl(s).
  • You may be asked to deduce a balanced equation from information given in words, including state symbols.

Relative masses

  • Relative atomic mass, Ar: the average mass of the isotopes of an element compared to 1/12th of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.
  • Relative molecular mass, Mr: the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in a molecule. The same term (relative formula mass) is used for ionic compounds.
  • Example: Mr of CaCO3 = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100. Relative masses have no units.
  • Calculate reacting masses in simple proportions, e.g. if 100 g CaCO3 gives 56 g CaO, then 25 g gives 14 g. Mass is conserved in reactions.

Concentration

  • Concentration can be measured in g/dm3 or mol/dm3.
  • 1 dm3 = 1000 cm3; divide cm3 by 1000 to convert to dm3.

Supplement The mole and the Avogadro constant

  • The mole (mol) is the unit of amount of substance. One mole contains 6.02 × 1023 particles (the Avogadro constant).
  • The mass of one mole of a substance in grams equals its Ar or Mr (the molar mass, in g/mol).

Reacting masses and gas volumes

Worked example
What mass of calcium oxide forms when 20.0 g of calcium carbonate is heated? What volume of CO2 is produced at r.t.p.?
CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g)
Moles CaCO3 = 20.0 ÷ 100 = 0.200 mol
Mole ratio CaCO3 : CaO : CO2 = 1 : 1 : 1
Mass CaO = 0.200 × 56 = 11.2 g
Volume CO2 = 0.200 × 24 = 4.80 dm3

Limiting reactant

The limiting reactant is the one that is completely used up; it controls the amount of product. The other reactant is in excess.

Worked example
2.4 g of magnesium is added to 0.10 mol of hydrochloric acid. Which reactant is limiting, and what mass of MgCl2 forms?
Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)
Moles Mg = 2.4 ÷ 24 = 0.10 mol
0.10 mol Mg needs 0.20 mol HCl, but only 0.10 mol is present
So HCl is limiting; Mg is in excess
Moles MgCl2 = 0.10 ÷ 2 = 0.050 mol
Mass MgCl2 = 0.050 × 95 = 4.75 g

Titration calculations

Worked example
25.0 cm3 of sodium hydroxide solution is neutralised by 20.0 cm3 of 0.100 mol/dm3 sulfuric acid. Find the concentration of the NaOH.
H2SO4(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na2SO4(aq) + 2H2O(l)
Moles H2SO4 = 0.100 × (20.0 ÷ 1000) = 0.00200 mol
Moles NaOH = 2 × 0.00200 = 0.00400 mol
Concentration NaOH = 0.00400 ÷ (25.0 ÷ 1000) = 0.160 mol/dm3

Empirical and molecular formulae

Worked example
A hydrocarbon contains 85.7% carbon and 14.3% hydrogen by mass. Its Mr is 56. Find its empirical and molecular formulae.
C: 85.7 ÷ 12 = 7.14; H: 14.3 ÷ 1 = 14.3
Divide by smallest: C = 7.14 ÷ 7.14 = 1; H = 14.3 ÷ 7.14 = 2
Empirical formula = CH2 (mass 14)
56 ÷ 14 = 4
Molecular formula = C4H8

Percentage yield, composition and purity

  • Percentage yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100
  • Percentage composition by mass of an element = (total Ar of that element in the formula ÷ Mr) × 100
  • Percentage purity = (mass of pure substance ÷ mass of impure sample) × 100
Worked example
In the calcium carbonate example above, only 9.8 g of CaO was collected. Calculate the percentage yield.
Theoretical yield = 11.2 g
Percentage yield = (9.8 ÷ 11.2) × 100
= 87.5%
Examiner Tip
Use the mole-ratio step every time: moles of known → ratio from the balanced equation → moles of unknown → convert. Write units on every line and keep 3 significant figures unless told otherwise; rounding too early is a common way to lose the final mark.
Common Mistake
Forgetting to convert cm3 to dm3 before using concentration or the 24 dm3 molar volume. Another frequent error is ignoring the mole ratio (e.g. treating H2SO4 : NaOH as 1 : 1 instead of 1 : 2).

  • I can use the mole, the Avogadro constant and the molar gas volume in calculations (Supplement).
  • I can identify a limiting reactant and carry out titration calculations (Supplement).
  • I can calculate empirical formula, molecular formula, percentage yield, composition and purity (Supplement).
Topic 4 · Physical Chemistry

Electrochemistry

How electricity decomposes ionic compounds, how to predict the products at each electrode, and how electroplating and hydrogen–oxygen fuel cells work. Examiners regularly ask for products, observations and, on the Extended paper, ionic half-equations.

Electrolysis: key terms

Electrolysis is the decomposition of an ionic compound, when molten or in aqueous solution, by the passage of an electric current.

Electrolyte
The molten or aqueous ionic compound that conducts and is decomposed
Cathode
Negative electrode; attracts cations
Anode
Positive electrode; attracts anions
Inert electrodes
Graphite (carbon) or platinum; do not react
External circuit
Wires and power supply connecting the electrodes
PANIC: Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode. OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain of electrons.

2

Predicting products

  • Molten binary compound (two elements, inert electrodes): the metal forms at the cathode and the non-metal at the anode.
  • In general, metals or hydrogen form at the cathode and non-metals (other than hydrogen) form at the anode.
  • Aqueous solutions also contain H+ and OH− ions from water.
  • At the cathode: hydrogen forms if the metal is more reactive than hydrogen; the metal forms if it is less reactive (e.g. copper, silver).
  • At the anode: with a concentrated halide solution the halogen forms; with a dilute halide solution, or a sulfate, oxygen forms.

Writing the chlorine anode equation as Cl− → Cl + e−. Chlorine is diatomic, so it must be 2Cl− → Cl2 + 2e−. Also check the charges balance on both sides, and that electrons appear on the left at the cathode and on the right at the anode.

Electroplating

Electroplating uses electrolysis to coat one metal with a thin layer of another metal.

1
CathodeThe object to be plated (e.g. a steel spoon).
2
AnodeA piece of the plating metal (e.g. silver).
3
ElectrolyteAn aqueous solution containing ions of the plating metal (e.g. silver nitrate).
4
ResultMetal ions are reduced and deposited on the object; the anode dissolves to replace them, so the electrolyte concentration stays constant.
  • Uses: to improve appearance (e.g. silver or gold plating on jewellery) and to resist corrosion (e.g. chromium on steel taps, tin on steel cans).
  • Supplement Copper is purified the same way: impure copper as the anode, pure copper as the cathode, copper(II) sulfate solution as electrolyte. Impurities fall below the anode.
Model answer
Aqueous copper(II) sulfate is electrolysed using carbon electrodes. Explain what is seen at each electrode and why the colour of the solution changes. [4]
At the cathode a pink-brown solid forms, because Cu2+ ions gain electrons and are deposited as copper: Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu. Copper is less reactive than hydrogen, so copper is discharged instead of hydrogen. At the anode bubbles of oxygen form, from hydroxide ions losing electrons. The blue colour fades because Cu2+ ions, which cause the blue colour, are removed from the solution and not replaced.

Hydrogen–oxygen fuel cells

  • A fuel cell uses hydrogen and oxygen to produce electricity, with water as the only chemical product.
  • Overall reaction: 2H2(g) + O2(g) → 2H2O(l)

Topic 5 · Physical Chemistry

Chemical Energetics

Every reaction either releases thermal energy to the surroundings or takes it in. This topic covers exothermic and endothermic reactions, reaction pathway diagrams and (for Supplement) calculating enthalpy changes from bond energies — a reliable source of calculation marks on Paper 4.

Exothermic and endothermic reactions

Energy is conserved in every reaction. What changes is where it is stored: some energy is held in the chemical bonds (the system), and some is thermal energy in everything around the reaction (the surroundings — the solvent, test tube, air and thermometer).

  • Exothermic reaction: a reaction that transfers thermal energy to the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings increases.
  • Endothermic reaction: a reaction that takes in thermal energy from the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings decreases.
Exothermic examplesEndothermic examples
Combustion of fuels, e.g. CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)Thermal decomposition, e.g. CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g)
Neutralisation, e.g. HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)Photosynthesis (energy taken in as light)
Reactive metals with water or acid, e.g. Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)Dissolving ammonium nitrate or ammonium chloride in water

Measuring the temperature change in the lab

  • Carry out the reaction in an insulated container (a polystyrene cup with a lid) to reduce thermal energy loss to the air.
  • Record the starting temperature, mix, stir, and record the highest (or lowest) temperature reached.
  • A rise in temperature shows an exothermic reaction; a fall shows an endothermic reaction.
Common Mistake
Saying “an endothermic reaction gets hotter because it takes in heat”. The reaction mixture and the container actually feel colder, because thermal energy is being drawn out of the surroundings into the chemical bonds of the products.

Reaction pathway diagrams

A reaction pathway diagram (energy level diagram) plots the energy of the chemicals on the vertical axis against the progress of the reaction on the horizontal axis.

Exothermic

Supplement Enthalpy change and activation energy

  • Enthalpy change, ΔH: the transfer of thermal energy during a reaction. Units: kJ/mol.
  • For an exothermic reaction ΔH is negative (the system loses energy).
  • For an endothermic reaction ΔH is positive (the system gains energy).
  • Activation energy, Ea: the minimum energy that colliding particles must have in order to react.

Supplement Bond breaking and bond making

  • Bond breaking is endothermic — energy must be supplied to pull atoms apart.
  • Bond making is exothermic — energy is released when new bonds form.
  • If more energy is released making bonds than is taken in breaking bonds, the reaction is exothermic overall.
  • If more energy is taken in breaking bonds than is released making bonds, the reaction is endothermic overall.
Breaking In (energy in), Making Out (energy out) — “BIMO”

Calculating ΔH from bond energies

ΔH = total energy needed to break bonds in reactants − total energy released making bonds in products.

Worked example
Calculate ΔH for H2(g) + Cl2(g) → 2HCl(g). Bond energies (kJ/mol): H–H 436, Cl–Cl 242, H–Cl 431.
Bonds broken: 1 × H–H + 1 × Cl–Cl = 436 + 242 = 678 kJ
Bonds made: 2 × H–Cl = 2 × 431 = 862 kJ
ΔH = 678 − 862
ΔH = −184 kJ/mol (exothermic, because more energy is released than taken in)
Worked example
Calculate ΔH for the combustion of methane: CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O. Bond energies (kJ/mol): C–H 412, O=O 496, C=O 743, O–H 463.
Bonds broken: 4 × C–H = 1648; 2 × O=O = 992; total = 2640 kJ
Bonds made: 2 × C=O = 1486; 4 × O–H (two in each H2O) = 1852; total = 3338 kJ
ΔH = 2640 − 3338
ΔH = −698 kJ/mol
Common Mistake
Forgetting to count every bond. Draw out the displayed formula of each molecule first, then multiply by the balancing number: 2H2O contains four O–H bonds, and CO2 contains two C=O double bonds. Also always include the sign in the final answer.
Model answer
The combustion of hydrogen is exothermic. Explain why, in terms of bond breaking and bond making. [3]
Energy is taken in to break the bonds in the reactants (H–H and O=O), so bond breaking is endothermic. Energy is released when the new O–H bonds in water form, so bond making is exothermic. More energy is released in making the new bonds than is taken in breaking the old ones, so overall thermal energy is given out and ΔH is negative.
Examiner Tip
When asked whether a reaction is exothermic or endothermic from data, always give the comparison (“more energy released making bonds than absorbed breaking bonds”), not just the name. The comparison carries the mark.

Topic 6 · Physical Chemistry

Chemical Reactions

This topic covers how to tell physical and chemical changes apart, what controls the rate of a reaction, reversible reactions and equilibrium (including the Haber and Contact processes), and redox. Rate graphs, collision theory explanations and equilibrium predictions appear on almost every paper.

Physical and chemical changes

  • Physical change: no new substance is made and the change is usually easy to reverse, e.g. melting ice, dissolving salt, boiling water.
  • Chemical change: one or more new substances are formed; often accompanied by an energy change, a colour change, fizzing or a precipitate, and usually hard to reverse, e.g. burning magnesium, rusting.

Rate of reaction

Rate measures how quickly reactants are used up or products are formed. The five factors on the syllabus:

Change madeEffect on rateCollision theory reason
Increase concentration of a solutionIncreasesMore particles per unit volume, so more frequent collisions
Increase pressure of a gasIncreasesParticles are closer together, so more particles per unit volume and more frequent collisions
Increase surface area (smaller pieces or powder)IncreasesMore particles exposed at the surface, so more frequent collisions
Increase temperatureIncreasesParticles have more kinetic energy, move faster so collide more often, and a greater proportion of collisions have energy ≥ activation energy
Add a catalystIncreasesProvides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy
  • Catalyst: a substance that increases the rate of a reaction and is unchanged in mass and chemical composition at the end of the reaction.
  • Enzymes are proteins that act as biological catalysts. They work best at an optimum temperature and pH; high temperatures denature them.
  • Fine powders (e.g. flour, coal dust) suspended in air can burn so fast that they cause explosions — a hazard in mills and mines.

Supplement Collision theory in detail

  • Particles react only when they collide with energy equal to or greater than the activation energy. These are successful collisions.
  • Rate depends on the frequency of successful collisions (number per second), not just the number of collisions.
  • Temperature has two effects: collisions become more frequent, and a larger proportion of particles have energy ≥ Ea. The second effect is the more important one.
  • A catalyst lowers the activation energy, so a larger proportion of collisions are successful at the same temperature.
Common Mistake
Writing “more collisions”. Examiners want more frequent collisions or more collisions per second. A reaction eventually has the same total number of collisions either way — what changes is how often they happen.

Practical methods for following rate

1
Volume of gas producedCollect the gas in a gas syringe (or an inverted measuring cylinder over water) and record the volume at regular time intervals. Suits e.g. Mg + HCl producing H2.
2
Loss of massPlace the flask on a balance with a cotton wool plug (lets gas out, keeps acid spray in) and record the mass at regular intervals. Suits reactions giving off a dense gas such as CO2 from CaCO3 + HCl.
3
Time for a precipitate to obscure a markSodium thiosulfate + hydrochloric acid produces a sulfur precipitate; time how long until a cross under the flask disappears. Rate is proportional to 1/time.
4
Control variablesChange one factor only; keep volumes, concentrations, mass and particle size of solid and temperature the same in every other respect.

Interpreting rate graphs

  • On a graph of volume of gas (or loss of mass) against time, the gradient shows the rate.
  • The curve is steepest at the start (highest concentration of reactants) and levels off when one reactant is used up.
  • A faster reaction gives a steeper initial curve. If the amounts of reactants are the same, it levels off at the same final volume, just sooner.
  • Halving the amount of the limiting reactant halves the final volume of gas.
  • Supplement Calculate average rate = change in volume ÷ time taken; find the rate at a point by drawing a tangent and working out its gradient.
Model answer
Explain why magnesium powder reacts faster with hydrochloric acid than the same mass of magnesium ribbon. [3]
The powder has a larger surface area. More magnesium atoms are exposed to the acid particles, so there are more frequent collisions between acid particles and magnesium atoms. This gives more frequent successful collisions, so the rate is higher.

Reversible reactions

  • Some reactions can go in both directions; this is shown by the symbol ⇌.
  • Heating blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate drives off water and leaves white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate. Adding water turns it blue again:
    CuSO4·5H2O(s) ⇌ CuSO4(s) + 5H2O(l)
  • Pink hydrated cobalt(II) chloride becomes blue when heated (anhydrous) and turns pink again when water is added:
    CoCl2·6H2O(s) ⇌ CoCl2(s) + 6H2O(l)
  • These colour changes are used as chemical tests for water (anhydrous CuSO4 white → blue; anhydrous CoCl2 blue → pink). To test whether water is pure, check that it boils at exactly 100 °C and freezes at 0 °C.

Supplement Equilibrium

  • A reversible reaction in a closed system reaches equilibrium when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction and the concentrations of reactants and products no longer change.
  • Equilibrium is dynamic: both reactions keep happening.

  • Temperature: a low temperature would give a higher equilibrium yield (forward reaction is exothermic) but the rate would be too slow. 450 °C is a compromise between rate and yield.

Redox

  • Oxidation is gain of oxygen; reduction is loss of oxygen.
  • Redox reactions involve simultaneous oxidation and reduction, e.g. in CuO + H2 → Cu + H2O, copper(II) oxide is reduced and hydrogen is oxidised.
  • Roman numerals show the oxidation number of an element in a compound, e.g. iron(II) sulfate contains Fe2+, iron(III) chloride contains Fe3+.

Supplement Electrons and oxidation numbers

  • Oxidation is loss of electrons and an increase in oxidation number. Reduction is gain of electrons and a decrease in oxidation number.
  • Rules: an uncombined element has oxidation number 0; a simple ion has oxidation number equal to its charge; in a compound the oxidation numbers add up to 0; in an ion they add up to the charge. Oxygen is usually −2 and hydrogen usually +1.
  • An oxidising agent oxidises another substance and is itself reduced. A reducing agent reduces another substance and is itself oxidised.
  • Example: Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Cu(s)
    Zn → Zn2+ + 2e− (oxidation; Zn is the reducing agent)
    Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu (reduction; Cu2+ is the oxidising agent)
  • Tests using colour changes: acidified potassium manganate(VII) is an oxidising agent and turns from purple to colourless when it reacts with a reducing agent. Aqueous potassium iodide is a reducing agent and turns from colourless to brown (iodine forms) when it reacts with an oxidising agent.

Acids, Bases and Salts

This topic covers the typical reactions of acids and bases, the pH scale and indicators, the classification of oxides, and how to prepare pure, dry samples of salts. Salt preparation methods are a favourite extended-writing question on both core and extended papers.

Properties of acids

  • Acids contain hydrogen ions, H+(aq), when dissolved in water.
  • Acids have a pH below 7, turn blue litmus red, turn methyl orange red and keep thymolphthalein colourless.

The pH scale and neutralisation

  • pH runs from 1 to 14. Below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral, above 7 is alkaline. Measure it with universal indicator (compare to a colour chart) or a pH meter.
  • Neutralisation is the reaction between an acid and an alkali: H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l)

Supplement Proton transfer and strength of acids

  • An acid is a proton (H+) donor; a base is a proton acceptor.
  • A strong acid is completely dissociated in aqueous solution, e.g. hydrochloric acid: HCl(aq) → H+(aq) + Cl−(aq)
  • A weak acid is only partially dissociated in aqueous solution, e.g. ethanoic acid: CH3COOH(aq) ⇌ H+(aq) + CH3COO−(aq)
  • At the same concentration, a strong acid has a higher H+ concentration, so a lower pH and reacts faster (e.g. with magnesium).
  • The lower the pH, the higher the H+ concentration. Each drop of 1 pH unit means a tenfold increase in H+ concentration.
Common Mistake
Confusing strong with concentrated. Strength is about how completely the acid dissociates; concentration is about how much acid is dissolved per dm3. You can have a dilute strong acid or a concentrated weak acid.

Oxides

  • Acidic oxides are usually oxides of non-metals, e.g. CO2, SO2. They react with bases to form a salt and water.
  • Basic oxides are usually oxides of metals, e.g. CuO, CaO. They react with acids to form a salt and water.
  • Supplement Amphoteric oxides react with both acids and bases to produce a salt and water, e.g. aluminium oxide, Al2O3, and zinc oxide, ZnO.

The Periodic Table

The Periodic Table organises the elements so that their properties can be predicted from their position. You need to explain trends in Group I, Group VII, the transition elements and the noble gases, and predict the properties of elements you have never met — a common source of “suggest” marks.

Arrangement of elements

  • Elements are arranged in order of increasing proton number (atomic number).
  • Periods are horizontal rows; groups are vertical columns.
  • Across a period, elements change from metallic to non-metallic character (e.g. period 3: Na, Mg, Al are metals; Si is a metalloid; P, S, Cl, Ar are non-metals).
  • The group number equals the number of outer-shell electrons (Group I has 1, Group VII has 7). Group VIII (Group 0) noble gases have a full outer shell.
  • The period number equals the number of occupied electron shells, e.g. chlorine (2,8,7) is in period 3, Group VII.
  • Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of outer-shell electrons.
  • The Periodic Table can be used to predict the charge on an ion: Group I forms 1+, Group II 2+, Group III 3+, Group VI 2−, Group VII 1−.

Alloys

  • An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements (metals or non-metals such as carbon).
  • Brass is a mixture of copper and zinc.
  • Stainless steel is a mixture of iron with other elements such as chromium, nickel and carbon.

Displacement reactions

  • A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a solution of its salt or from its oxide.
  • Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s): blue solution fades, pink-brown copper forms, temperature rises.
  • Thermite: 2Al(s) + Fe2O3(s) → Al2O3(s) + 2Fe(l)
  • You can use displacement results (and reactions with water and acid) to put unfamiliar metals into order of reactivity.
  • Supplement Reactivity is the tendency of a metal to form positive ions. A more reactive metal loses electrons more readily. Ionic equation: Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Cu(s). Zinc is oxidised (loses electrons); copper ions are reduced (gain electrons).
  • Supplement Aluminium seems unreactive (e.g. no reaction with water, slow start with acid) because it is covered by a thin, tough, unreactive layer of aluminium oxide that prevents contact with water or acid.
Common Mistake
Saying "copper reacts with dilute acid slowly". Copper is below hydrogen, so it does not react with dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid at all. Also do not say aluminium is unreactive: it is reactive, but protected by its oxide layer.

Corrosion of metals

  • Rusting is the corrosion of iron and steel. It needs both water and oxygen. Rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide.
  • Classic experiment: iron nails in three tubes: (1) air and water → rusts; (2) boiled water with an oil layer (no oxygen) → no rust; (3) dry air with anhydrous calcium chloride (no water) → no rust.
  • Salt water speeds up rusting.

Preventing rusting

  • Barrier methods keep out water and oxygen: painting, greasing or oiling, coating with plastic.
  • Galvanising: coating iron with a layer of zinc.
  • Supplement Barrier methods work only while the coating is unbroken.
  • Supplement Sacrificial protection: a more reactive metal (zinc or magnesium) is attached to the iron. The more reactive metal loses electrons (is oxidised) in preference to iron, so iron does not rust. Used on ship hulls and pipelines. Zn(s) → Zn2+(aq) + 2e−
  • Supplement Galvanising protects in two ways: the zinc acts as a barrier, and if scratched the zinc still corrodes sacrificially instead of the iron.
Model answer
Explain why blocks of zinc attached to a steel ship hull stop the hull rusting, even where the paint is scratched. [3]
Zinc is more reactive than iron.
Zinc loses electrons / is oxidised more readily than iron, so the zinc corrodes instead of the iron (sacrificial protection).
Electrons from the zinc flow to the iron, so iron does not form ions / is not oxidised.

Extraction of metals

  • The ease of extracting a metal depends on its position in the reactivity series.
  • Metals above carbon (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al) are extracted by electrolysis of the molten compound.
  • Metals below carbon (Zn, Fe, Cu) are extracted by reduction with carbon (or carbon monoxide).
  • Very unreactive metals (silver, gold) are found uncombined (native).

Aluminium from bauxite

  • Aluminium is too reactive to be reduced by carbon, so it is extracted by electrolysis.
  • Supplement Aluminium oxide is dissolved in molten cryolite. This lowers the operating temperature (saving energy and cost) and makes a better conductor than molten alumina alone.

Supplement The greenhouse effect

  • The Sun's radiation passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth's surface.
  • The Earth's surface emits thermal (infrared) energy.
  • Greenhouse gases (carbon dioxide, methane) absorb some of this thermal energy and re-emit it in all directions, including back towards the Earth, while some is reflected back into space.
  • Higher concentrations of greenhouse gases mean more thermal energy is kept in the atmosphere, so the average temperature rises (global warming) and climate changes.

Supplement Strategies to reduce environmental effects

Climate change

  • Planting trees (they remove CO2 by photosynthesis).
  • Reducing livestock farming (less methane).
  • Decreasing use of fossil fuels.
  • Increasing use of hydrogen as a fuel and of renewable energy such as wind and solar.

Acid rain

  • Using catalytic converters in vehicles.
  • Using low-sulfur fuels.
  • Flue gas desulfurisation using calcium oxide in power stations: CaO(s) + SO2(g) → CaSO3(s)

Catalytic converters

Catalytic converters remove oxides of nitrogen and carbon monoxide (and unburnt hydrocarbons) from exhaust gases using a platinum or rhodium catalyst:

  • 2CO(g) + 2NO(g) → 2CO2(g) + N2(g)
  • 2NO2(g) + 4CO(g) → N2(g) + 4CO2(g)
  • 2CO(g) + O2(g) → 2CO2(g)

Photosynthesis

  • Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, in the presence of light and chlorophyll.
  • Supplement Symbol equation: 6CO2(g) + 6H2O(l) → C6H12O6(aq) + 6O2(g)
  • Photosynthesis removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, which is why deforestation adds to climate change.
Examiner Tip
Keep your pollutant stories separate. Carbon dioxide and methane cause global warming; sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen cause acid rain. Carbon monoxide is toxic but is not a greenhouse gas answer and does not cause acid rain. Never link CO2 to the "hole in the ozone layer".

  • A homologous series is a family of similar compounds with similar chemical properties due to the presence of the same functional group.

Alkanes

  • Saturated hydrocarbons; generally unreactive apart from combustion.

Carboxylic acids

  • Ethanoic acid is a weak acid and shows typical acid reactions, forming ethanoate salts:
  • With metals: 2CH3COOH + Mg → (CH3COO)2Mg + H2 (magnesium ethanoate)
  • With bases: CH3COOH + NaOH → CH3COONa + H2O (sodium ethanoate)
  • With carbonates: 2CH3COOH + Na2CO3 → 2CH3COONa + H2O + CO2
  • Supplement Ethanoic acid forms by oxidation of ethanol: (a) by bacterial oxidation during fermentation (wine turning to vinegar), or (b) with acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII), which turns from purple to colourless.

Common Mistake
Naming esters the wrong way round. The product of ethanol and propanoic acid is ethyl propanoate, not propyl ethanoate. Also, in formulae remember the C of –COOH is counted in the chain: ethanoic acid is CH3COOH (two carbons).

Polymers

  • Polymers are large molecules built up from many small units called monomers. Plastics are made from polymers.
  • Addition polymerisation: many alkene monomers join; the C=C opens. Ethene forms poly(ethene).
  • Disposal problems of plastics (they are non-biodegradable): they take up space and persist in landfill; they accumulate in oceans, harming marine life; burning them forms toxic gases.
  • Supplement Identify the repeat unit and linkages in a polymer; deduce the polymer from a monomer and vice versa. Draw the repeat unit in brackets with continuation bonds through them and "n" outside, e.g. propene gives poly(propene) with repeat unit –[CH2–CH(CH3)]–.
  • Supplement Addition polymerisation gives only the polymer; condensation polymerisation joins monomers with two functional groups and also releases a small molecule such as water.
  • Supplement Nylon is a polyamide (amide linkage –CONH–) from a dicarboxylic acid and a diamine. PET is a polyester (ester linkage –COO–) from a dicarboxylic acid and a diol. PET can be converted back into monomers and re-polymerised, which aids recycling.
  • Supplement Proteins are natural polyamides formed by condensation polymerisation of amino acid monomers, joined by amide linkages.
Examiner Tip
When drawing any displayed formula, check every carbon has exactly four bonds, every hydrogen one and every oxygen two. In the –COOH group draw the C=O and the O–H separately. Missing hydrogens or a five-bonded carbon lose the mark.

Cracking breaks these long-chain alkanes into shorter-chain alkanes, such as petrol, for which demand is high.

Acid–base titrations

1

2

Chromatography

  • Paper chromatography separates mixtures of soluble coloured substances using a suitable solvent.
  • Method: draw a start line in pencil (graphite does not dissolve); spot the samples on it; stand the paper in solvent with the solvent level below the start line (so the spots do not dissolve into the bulk solvent); cover and allow the solvent to rise; remove and mark the solvent front.
  • A pure substance gives one spot; an impure substance (mixture) gives two or more spots.
  • Identify unknowns by comparing their spots with those of known substances run on the same paper: spots at the same height match.
  • Supplement Colourless substances are made visible with a locating agent, sprayed on the dried chromatogram.
  • Supplement Rf = distance travelled by substance ÷ distance travelled by solvent. Rf has no units and is always less than 1.
