Exam Guide: Papers, Timing and Command Words

What the three papers look like for the 2026–2028 exams, how long you get per mark, what each command word is asking for, the data you are given in the exam, and the calculations you must know by heart. Read this once before you start revising and again in the week before the exam.

Answering “describe” with an explanation, or “explain” with a description. For “describe the change in rate” you say what happens (it decreases); for “explain” you say why (fewer particles per unit volume, so collisions are less frequent).

What you are given in the exam

  • The Periodic Table is printed at the back of every paper, with relative atomic masses (e.g. H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Na = 23, Cl = 35.5, Cu = 64). Always use these values.
  • The statement that one mole of any gas occupies 24 dm3 at room temperature and pressure (r.t.p.).
  • Notes for use in qualitative analysis (tests for cations, anions and gases, and flame test colours) in Papers 5 and 6. For Papers 3 and 4 you must know the tests from memory.
  • You are not given the equations below or any definitions; learn them.

Practical papers: skills tested

Paper 5 and Paper 6 test the same experimental skills (assessment objective AO3), so revise them the same way. Expect questions on:

  • Contexts: measuring volumes, masses, temperatures, times and lengths; rates of reaction; salt preparation; filtration, crystallisation, simple and fractional distillation, chromatography; electrolysis; tests for ions, gases and water; reactions of dilute acids (including ethanoic acid); oxidising and reducing agents; heating and cooling curves; titrations; solubility; melting and boiling points; displacement reactions; temperature changes; rusting and corrosion.
  • Planning: independent, dependent and control variables, a sensible range of values, apparatus choice, safety precautions, how to record and process results.
  • Readings: read scales to the nearest half division where needed, record in a table with units in the headings, repeat readings, spot anomalous results.
  • Evaluation: sources of error and specific improvements (“use a gas syringe instead of collecting over water”, not just “be more careful”).

Topic 12 (Experimental Techniques) covers the apparatus, separation methods, titrations, chromatography and all the tests in detail.

Answer habits that earn marks

  • Equations: balance them and add state symbols when asked; check that ionic half-equations balance in charge too.
  • Particle language: say particles, atoms, ions, molecules or electrons precisely. “Molecules” in an ionic solid is wrong.
  • Observations: describe what you would see (bubbles, colour change from blue to colourless, white precipitate), not the name of the product.
  • Working: write each step of a calculation. A wrong final answer can still earn method marks; a bare wrong number earns nothing.
  • Comparisons: use comparative words (higher, faster, more reactive) and refer to both things.

Quick Checklist

  • I know which three papers I am sitting, how long each one is and how many marks it has.
  • I can tell “state”, “describe”, “explain”, “suggest” and “deduce” apart and answer each correctly.
  • I know what data is given in the exam (Periodic Table, 24 dm3 per mole of gas) and what I must learn.
  • I can use every relationship in the key relationships table, with units.
  • I can plan an experiment, record results in a table and suggest specific improvements.
Topic 1 · Particles & Structure

States of Matter

How the arrangement, movement and energy of particles explain the properties of solids, liquids and gases, and why substances change state or spread out by diffusion. These ideas underpin almost every later topic, and examiners reward precise particle language.

The three states

All matter is made of tiny particles (atoms, molecules or ions). The kinetic particle theory says these particles are always moving, and the amount of movement depends on how much kinetic energy they have.

Changes of state

Changes of state are physical changes: no new substance is made and they are reversible.

Melting
Solid → liquid, at the melting point
Freezing
Liquid → solid, at the same temperature as melting
Boiling
Liquid → gas throughout the liquid, at the boiling point
Evaporation
Liquid → gas at the surface only, below the boiling point
Condensation
Gas → liquid on cooling
Sublimation
Solid → gas directly (e.g. solid CO2, iodine). Background: not named in the 0620 syllabus.

Melting, boiling and evaporating need energy to be taken in (heating); freezing and condensing happen when energy is given out (cooling).

Supplement Explaining changes of state with particles

  • Melting: heating gives particles more kinetic energy; they vibrate more until they overcome some of the forces holding them in fixed positions and can move past each other.
  • Boiling: particles gain enough energy to overcome the attractive forces between them completely and escape as a gas; bubbles of gas form throughout the liquid.
  • Evaporation: only the most energetic particles at the surface escape, so it can happen at any temperature. It is faster when the liquid is warmer, has a larger surface area, or when air moves over it.
  • Condensing and freezing: particles lose kinetic energy, slow down and the attractive forces pull them closer together.
Common Mistake
Do not say particles themselves “expand”, “melt” or “get bigger” when heated. The particles stay the same size; it is the spacing and movement that change. Also avoid saying bonds break when a simple molecular substance boils — it is the forces between molecules that are overcome.

Supplement Heating and cooling curves

If a pure solid is heated steadily and its temperature is recorded against time, the graph shows sloping sections and flat sections.

1
Solid warming upTemperature rises; particles vibrate faster.
2
Flat section at melting pointTemperature stays constant. Energy supplied is used to overcome forces of attraction between particles, not to raise kinetic energy. Solid and liquid are both present.
3
Liquid warming upTemperature rises again; particles move faster.
4
Flat section at boiling pointTemperature constant while energy overcomes the remaining attractive forces. Liquid and gas both present. This plateau is longer than the melting one because more energy is needed.
5
Gas warming upTemperature rises as gas particles move faster.
  • A cooling curve is the reverse: the flat sections occur at the boiling (condensing) point and freezing point, where energy is released as forces of attraction form, so the temperature stays constant.
  • A pure substance melts and boils at a sharp, fixed temperature, so its curve has truly flat sections. An impure substance melts over a range of temperatures, starting below the pure melting point, and boils over a range above the pure boiling point (purity is covered again in Topic 12).
Examiner Tip
For the flat sections, the credited explanation is that energy is used to overcome the forces of attraction between particles (or is released as these forces form during cooling). Simply writing “the substance is changing state” describes but does not explain.

Temperature, pressure and gas volume

  • Higher temperature (pressure kept constant): the volume of a gas increases (the gas expands).
  • Higher pressure (temperature kept constant): the volume of a gas decreases (the gas is compressed).

Supplement Explaining these effects with kinetic particle theory

  • Temperature: heated particles gain kinetic energy and move faster, so they hit the container walls harder and more often. If the pressure is to stay the same, the gas must expand so that the particles spread further apart — the volume increases.
  • Pressure: a higher external pressure pushes the particles closer together, so the volume decreases. Gases can be compressed because there is a lot of empty space between their particles.
  • In a sealed container of fixed volume, raising the temperature increases the pressure instead, because the particles collide with the walls more frequently and with more force.

Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, as a result of their random motion. It happens in gases and liquids (more slowly in liquids) but not noticeably in solids.

Explaining it with kinetic particle theory: particles of a gas or liquid move constantly and randomly, colliding with each other and changing direction. Over time this random movement spreads them out until they are evenly mixed. No stirring or energy input is needed. Diffusion is slower in liquids because the particles are closer together and move more slowly, so they collide more often and travel less far between collisions.

Evidence for diffusion

  • A purple crystal of potassium manganate(VII) placed in water: the colour slowly spreads through all the water without stirring.
  • A smell (e.g. perfume) spreads across a room.
  • Bromine gas in a gas jar spreads upwards into a jar of air above it until the brown colour is even throughout.

Supplement Relative molecular mass and diffusion rate

  • At the same temperature, gas particles with a lower relative molecular mass (Mr) move faster on average, so they diffuse faster.
  • Higher temperature also increases the rate of diffusion because all particles move faster.

The ammonia and hydrogen chloride experiment

1
Set upPlace cotton wool soaked in concentrated ammonia solution at one end of a long glass tube, and cotton wool soaked in concentrated hydrochloric acid at the other. Seal both ends with bungs.
2
ObserveAfter a few minutes a white ring (smoke) of ammonium chloride forms inside the tube: NH3(g) + HCl(g) → NH4Cl(s)
3
ConcludeThe ring forms closer to the HCl end. NH3 (Mr = 17) has a lower Mr than HCl (Mr = 36.5), so ammonia molecules move faster and travel further in the same time.
Model answer
In the experiment above, explain why the white solid forms nearer to the hydrochloric acid end of the tube. [3]
Both gases diffuse along the tube by the random movement of their particles. Ammonia has a lower relative molecular mass (17) than hydrogen chloride (36.5), so ammonia molecules move faster at the same temperature. Ammonia therefore travels further along the tube before the two gases meet and react to form ammonium chloride, so the ring is closer to the hydrochloric acid end.
Common Mistake
Writing that ammonia is “lighter” or “less dense” is too vague. Use the term lower relative molecular mass and link it to the particles moving faster.

  • I can describe the separation, arrangement and motion of particles in solids, liquids and gases.
  • I can describe melting, boiling, evaporating, freezing and condensing, and the difference between boiling and evaporation.
  • I can explain changes of state using kinetic particle theory (Supplement).
  • I can interpret heating and cooling curves and explain the flat sections (Supplement).
  • I can describe how temperature and pressure affect the volume of a gas.
  • I can explain those effects using kinetic particle theory (Supplement).
  • I can describe and explain diffusion using kinetic particle theory and give evidence for it.
  • I can explain how relative molecular mass affects the rate of diffusion using the NH3/HCl experiment (Supplement).

Atoms, Elements and Compounds

What atoms are made of, how electrons are arranged, and how atoms join by ionic, covalent and metallic bonding. Structure and bonding questions appear on every paper, and explaining properties from structure is a favourite source of 3–4 mark questions.

Elements, compounds and mixtures

Element
A substance made of only one type of atom
Compound
A substance made of two or more elements chemically combined
Mixture
Two or more substances not chemically combined
  • A compound has different properties from the elements it contains and a fixed composition; it can only be separated by a chemical reaction.
  • A mixture keeps the properties of its components and can be separated by physical methods (filtration, distillation, chromatography).

Atomic structure

An atom has a tiny central nucleus containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons in shells.

ParticleRelative chargeRelative massLocation
Proton+11Nucleus
Neutron01Nucleus
Electron−11/1840 (negligible)Shells around the nucleus
  • Proton number (atomic number, Z): the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.
  • Mass number (nucleon number, A): the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.
  • Number of neutrons = mass number − proton number. Atoms are neutral, so number of electrons = number of protons.
  • Notation: 2311Na has 11 protons, 11 electrons and 12 neutrons.

Electronic configuration (first 20 elements)

  • Shells fill in order: first shell holds up to 2, second up to 8, third up to 8 (for elements 1–20).
  • Examples: C 2,4; O 2,6; Na 2,8,1; Cl 2,8,7; Ca 2,8,8,2.
  • Ions: add electrons for a negative ion, remove them for a positive ion. Na+ 2,8; Mg2+ 2,8; Al3+ 2,8; O2− 2,8; Cl− 2,8,8; Ca2+ 2,8,8. Ions of elements 1–20 usually have a noble gas configuration.
  • The number of occupied shells = the period number.
  • The number of outer-shell electrons = the group number (Groups I–VII).
  • Noble gases (Group VIII) have a full outer shell, which makes them unreactive. Helium has 2 outer electrons; the others have 8.

Isotopes

Isotopes are different atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

  • Example: 3517Cl (18 neutrons) and 3717Cl (20 neutrons).
  • Reading symbols: top number = mass number, bottom number = proton number. For an ion, the charge tells you how many electrons were gained or lost: 3517Cl− has 17 protons, 35 − 17 = 18 neutrons and 17 + 1 = 18 electrons; 2412Mg2+ has 12 protons, 12 neutrons and 12 − 2 = 10 electrons.
  • Supplement Isotopes of the same element have the same chemical properties because they have the same number of electrons and therefore the same electronic configuration (chemical reactions involve electrons, not neutrons).

Supplement Calculating relative atomic mass

Relative atomic mass (Ar) is the weighted average mass of the isotopes of an element, using their relative abundances.

Worked example
Chlorine is 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37. Calculate Ar.
Ar = (35 × 75 + 37 × 25) ÷ 100
= (2625 + 925) ÷ 100
= 3550 ÷ 100
= 35.5
Worked example
Boron has two isotopes: boron-10 (relative abundance 20%) and boron-11 (80%). Calculate Ar to one decimal place.
Ar = (10 × 20 + 11 × 80) ÷ 100
= (200 + 880) ÷ 100
= 10.8
The answer is closer to 11 because boron-11 is the more abundant isotope (the Periodic Table rounds it to 11). Ar has no units.

Ions and ionic bonding

  • An ion is a charged particle formed when an atom gains or loses electrons.
  • Cations are positive ions, formed when atoms (usually metals) lose electrons. Anions are negative ions, formed when atoms (usually non-metals) gain electrons.
  • An ionic bond is a strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
  • Ionic bonds form between metals and non-metals. Electrons are transferred from the metal atom to the non-metal atom so both ions end up with a full outer shell (noble gas configuration).
  • A Group I atom loses 1 electron to form a 1+ ion (e.g. Na+, K+); a Group VII atom gains 1 electron to form a 1− ion (e.g. Cl−, Br−). Non-metal anions take the ending -ide: chloride, oxide.
1
Sodium atom, Na 2,8,1Loses its one outer electron to become Na+ (2,8).
2
Chlorine atom, Cl 2,8,7Gains that electron to become Cl− (2,8,8).
3
Dot-and-cross diagramDraw the ions in square brackets with the charge outside the top right; show the transferred electron as a cross among the dots of the chloride ion. Usually only outer shells need to be shown.
  • Core: dot-and-cross diagrams for Group I metals with Group VII non-metals (e.g. NaCl, KBr).
  • Supplement Dot-and-cross diagrams for other metal/non-metal combinations, e.g. MgO (Mg2+ and O2−, two electrons transferred) and CaCl2 (one Ca2+ and two Cl−).
  • Supplement An ionic compound has a giant lattice structure: a regular arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions, e.g. sodium chloride.

Properties of ionic compounds

  • High melting and boiling points.
  • Good electrical conductivity when molten or in aqueous solution; poor when solid.
  • Supplement Explanation: many strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions in the giant lattice need a lot of energy to overcome. When solid the ions are held in fixed positions; when molten or dissolved the ions are free to move and carry charge.

Simple molecules and covalent bonds

  • A covalent bond forms when a pair of electrons is shared between two atoms, giving each a noble gas electronic configuration. Covalent bonding occurs between non-metal atoms.
  • Core dot-and-cross diagrams: H2, Cl2, H2O, CH4, NH3, HCl.
  • Supplement Also: CH3OH, C2H4 (one C=C double bond), O2 (double bond), CO2 (two C=O double bonds), N2 (triple bond).

Properties of simple molecular substances

  • Low melting and boiling points (many are gases or liquids at room temperature).
  • Poor electrical conductivity: there are no ions or free electrons to carry charge.
  • Supplement Explanation: the covalent bonds within molecules are strong, but the intermolecular forces (attractive forces between molecules) are weak, so little energy is needed to separate the molecules. Poor conductivity: molecules are neutral and have no free electrons or ions, so nothing charged can move through the substance. You do not need to name types of intermolecular force.
Common Mistake
Saying water has a low boiling point because “covalent bonds are weak” loses the mark. Covalent bonds are strong; it is the weak forces between molecules that are overcome on boiling.

Giant covalent structures

FeatureDiamondGraphite
Bonding per carbonEach C covalently bonded to 4 others, tetrahedralEach C covalently bonded to 3 others in flat hexagonal layers
Between layersNo layers; 3D networkWeak forces between layers
HardnessVery hardSoft and slippery; layers slide over each other

  • Both have very high melting points because many strong covalent bonds must be broken.

Linking structure to uses

  • Graphite as a lubricant: the forces between layers are weak, so the layers slide over each other easily.
  • Graphite as an electrode: the delocalised electrons can move along the layers and carry charge, and its very high melting point means it survives high temperatures (e.g. in electrolysis of molten compounds).
  • Diamond in cutting tools: every carbon atom is held by four strong covalent bonds in a rigid three-dimensional network, so diamond is extremely hard.
  • Supplement Silicon(IV) oxide, SiO2, has a giant covalent structure similar to diamond: each Si atom is covalently bonded to 4 O atoms and each O atom to 2 Si atoms, in a three-dimensional network.
  • Supplement Similar properties to diamond, because both are 3D networks of strong covalent bonds with no free electrons or ions: very hard, very high melting point, and does not conduct electricity.

Supplement Metallic bonding

  • Metallic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between the positive ions in a giant metallic lattice and a “sea” of delocalised electrons.
  • Good electrical conductivity: the delocalised electrons are free to move through the structure and carry charge.
  • Malleable (can be hammered into shape) and ductile (can be drawn into wires): the positive ions are arranged in layers that can slide over each other when a force is applied. The delocalised electrons move with them and still attract the ions, so the metallic bonding is not broken and the metal does not shatter.
Examiner Tip
When explaining conductivity always name the mobile charge carrier: ions for molten or dissolved ionic compounds, delocalised electrons for metals and graphite. Never say ions move in a metal or electrons move in an electrolyte.
Model answer
Explain why sodium chloride conducts electricity when molten but not when solid. [3]
Sodium chloride is made of Na+ and Cl− ions held in a giant ionic lattice. In the solid, the ions are held in fixed positions by strong electrostatic attractions and cannot move. When molten, the lattice breaks down and the ions are free to move, so they can carry charge through the liquid.

Writing formulae and balanced equations, and using relative masses and the mole to calculate amounts of substances. Calculation questions carry many marks on the Extended paper, and method marks are awarded for clear working even if the final answer is wrong.

Formulae

  • Learn the formulae of the elements and compounds named in the syllabus. Hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen and the halogens exist as diatomic molecules: H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, I2. Metals, carbon and the noble gases are written as single symbols (Na, C, He).
  • The molecular formula is the number and type of different atoms in one molecule, e.g. C2H6 for ethane.
  • Supplement The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of the different atoms or ions in a compound, e.g. CH3 for ethane.
  • You should be able to deduce the formula of a simple compound from a model or diagram by counting the relative numbers of each type of atom, e.g. a ball-and-stick model with 1 nitrogen joined to 3 hydrogens is NH3.
  • Supplement Deduce the formula of an ionic compound from the relative numbers of ions in a diagram (e.g. a lattice with twice as many F− as Ca2+ is CaF2) or from the charges on the ions: the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge.
Positive ionsNegative ions
Na+, K+, H+, NH4+, Ag+Cl−, Br−, OH−, NO3−
Mg2+, Ca2+, Cu2+, Zn2+, Fe2+O2−, S2−, SO42−, CO32−
Al3+, Fe3+N3−, PO43−

Examples: Al3+ + O2− gives Al2O3; Ca2+ + NO3− gives Ca(NO3)2. Roman numerals give the charge: iron(III) chloride is FeCl3.

Equations

3

4

  • Supplement Ionic equations show only the particles that change; spectator ions (ions present in the same form on both sides) are left out. Neutralisation: H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l). Precipitation: Ag+(aq) + Cl−(aq) → AgCl(s).
  • Supplement You may be asked to deduce a balanced symbol equation, with state symbols, from information given in words.
Worked example (Supplement)
Zinc powder is added to aqueous copper(II) sulfate. Copper metal forms and the solution becomes zinc sulfate. Write the symbol equation and the ionic equation.
Full equation: Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s)
Split the aqueous ionic compounds into ions: Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) + SO42−(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + SO42−(aq) + Cu(s)
SO42− is unchanged, so it is a spectator ion. Cross it out:
Ionic equation: Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Cu(s)
Check: atoms balance and the total charge is 2+ on both sides.

Relative masses

  • Relative atomic mass, Ar: the average mass of the isotopes of an element compared to 1/12th of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.
  • Relative molecular mass, Mr: the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in one molecule. For ionic compounds (which are not made of molecules) the term relative formula mass is used instead; it has the same symbol, Mr, and is worked out the same way from the formula.
  • Example: Mr of CaCO3 = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100; Mr of Ca(OH)2 = 40 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 74. Relative masses have no units. Use the Ar values on the Periodic Table you are given (e.g. Cl = 35.5, Cu = 64).
  • Calculate reacting masses in simple proportions, e.g. if 100 g CaCO3 gives 56 g CaO, then 25 g gives 14 g. Mass is conserved in reactions.
Worked example
Magnesium burns in oxygen: 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO. What mass of magnesium oxide is made from 6.0 g of magnesium?
From the equation, 2 × 24 = 48 g of Mg makes 2 × (24 + 16) = 80 g of MgO.
6.0 g is 48 ÷ 8, so the mass of MgO = 80 ÷ 8 = 10 g.
(The extra 4.0 g is the mass of oxygen that combined with the magnesium.)

Concentration

  • Concentration can be measured in g/dm3 or mol/dm3.
  • 1 dm3 = 1000 cm3; divide cm3 by 1000 to convert to dm3.

Supplement The mole and the Avogadro constant

  • The mole (mol) is the unit of amount of substance. One mole contains 6.02 × 1023 particles (the Avogadro constant).
  • The mass of one mole of a substance in grams equals its Ar or Mr (the molar mass, in g/mol).
Moles from mass
amount (mol) = mass (g) ÷ molar mass (g/mol)
Gas volume at r.t.p.
amount (mol) = volume (dm3) ÷ 24
Solutions
amount (mol) = concentration (mol/dm3) × volume (dm3)
Particles
number = amount (mol) × 6.02 × 1023
Molar gas volume
24 dm3 (24 000 cm3) at room temperature and pressure
g/dm3 ↔ mol/dm3
g/dm3 = mol/dm3 × Mr

Moles, mass, molar mass and particles

Worked example
(a) How many moles are in 9.0 g of water? (b) How many molecules and how many atoms is that?
Mr of H2O = (2 × 1) + 16 = 18, so molar mass = 18 g/mol
(a) amount = 9.0 ÷ 18 = 0.50 mol
(b) molecules = 0.50 × 6.02 × 1023 = 3.01 × 1023
Each molecule has 3 atoms, so atoms = 3 × 3.01 × 1023 = 9.03 × 1023
Worked example
0.125 mol of a metal oxide has a mass of 10.0 g. Find its molar mass and its relative formula mass. The oxide has the formula XO; identify X.
molar mass = mass ÷ amount = 10.0 ÷ 0.125 = 80 g/mol
So Mr = 80 (same number, no units)
Ar of X = 80 − 16 = 64, so X is copper and the oxide is CuO.

Supplement Reacting masses and gas volumes

Worked example
What mass of calcium oxide forms when 20.0 g of calcium carbonate is heated? What volume of CO2 is produced at r.t.p.?
CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g)
Moles CaCO3 = 20.0 ÷ 100 = 0.200 mol
Mole ratio CaCO3 : CaO : CO2 = 1 : 1 : 1
Mass CaO = 0.200 × 56 = 11.2 g
Volume CO2 = 0.200 × 24 = 4.80 dm3

Supplement Limiting reactant

The limiting reactant is the one that is completely used up; it controls the amount of product. The other reactant is in excess.

Worked example
2.4 g of magnesium is added to 0.10 mol of hydrochloric acid. Which reactant is limiting, and what mass of MgCl2 forms?
Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)
Moles Mg = 2.4 ÷ 24 = 0.10 mol
0.10 mol Mg needs 0.20 mol HCl, but only 0.10 mol is present
So HCl is limiting; Mg is in excess
Moles MgCl2 = 0.10 ÷ 2 = 0.050 mol (use the limiting reactant)
Mr of MgCl2 = 24 + (2 × 35.5) = 95
Mass MgCl2 = 0.050 × 95 = 4.75 g = 4.8 g (2 s.f., matching the data)
Volume of H2 at r.t.p. = 0.050 × 24 = 1.2 dm3 (1200 cm3)

Supplement Volumes and concentrations of solutions

Worked example
What volume of 0.200 mol/dm3 hydrochloric acid reacts exactly with 1.00 g of calcium carbonate?
CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)
Moles CaCO3 = 1.00 ÷ 100 = 0.0100 mol
Moles HCl = 2 × 0.0100 = 0.0200 mol
Volume = moles ÷ concentration = 0.0200 ÷ 0.200 = 0.100 dm3 = 100 cm3
Worked example
4.00 g of sodium hydroxide is dissolved in water and made up to 250 cm3 of solution. Calculate the concentration in g/dm3 and in mol/dm3.
Volume = 250 ÷ 1000 = 0.250 dm3
Concentration = 4.00 ÷ 0.250 = 16.0 g/dm3
Mr of NaOH = 23 + 16 + 1 = 40
Concentration = 16.0 ÷ 40 = 0.400 mol/dm3

Supplement Titration calculations

Worked example
25.0 cm3 of sodium hydroxide solution is neutralised by 20.0 cm3 of 0.100 mol/dm3 sulfuric acid. Find the concentration of the NaOH.
H2SO4(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na2SO4(aq) + 2H2O(l)
Moles H2SO4 = 0.100 × (20.0 ÷ 1000) = 0.00200 mol
Moles NaOH = 2 × 0.00200 = 0.00400 mol
Concentration NaOH = 0.00400 ÷ (25.0 ÷ 1000) = 0.160 mol/dm3
In g/dm3: 0.160 × 40 = 6.40 g/dm3
Examiner Tip
In a real titration, use the mean of your concordant titres (results within 0.10 cm3 of each other) as the volume, not the rough first run.

Supplement Empirical and molecular formulae

Worked example
A hydrocarbon contains 85.7% carbon and 14.3% hydrogen by mass. Its Mr is 56. Find its empirical and molecular formulae.
C: 85.7 ÷ 12 = 7.14; H: 14.3 ÷ 1 = 14.3
Divide by smallest: C = 7.14 ÷ 7.14 = 1; H = 14.3 ÷ 7.14 = 2
Empirical formula = CH2 (mass 14)
56 ÷ 14 = 4
Molecular formula = C4H8
Worked example
2.16 g of aluminium reacts completely with oxygen to form 4.08 g of aluminium oxide. Find the empirical formula of the oxide.
Mass of oxygen = 4.08 − 2.16 = 1.92 g
Al: 2.16 ÷ 27 = 0.0800 mol; O: 1.92 ÷ 16 = 0.120 mol
Divide by smallest: Al = 1; O = 0.120 ÷ 0.0800 = 1.5
Not whole numbers, so multiply both by 2: Al = 2, O = 3
Empirical formula = Al2O3

Supplement Percentage yield, composition and purity

  • Percentage yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100
  • Percentage composition by mass of an element = (total Ar of that element in the formula ÷ Mr) × 100
  • Percentage purity = (mass of pure substance ÷ mass of impure sample) × 100
Worked example
In the calcium carbonate example above, only 9.8 g of CaO was collected. Calculate the percentage yield.
Theoretical yield = 11.2 g
Percentage yield = (9.8 ÷ 11.2) × 100
= 87.5% (88% to 2 s.f.)
Worked example
Calculate the percentage by mass of nitrogen in ammonium nitrate, NH4NO3.
Mr = 14 + (4 × 1) + 14 + (3 × 16) = 80
Mass of N in the formula = 2 × 14 = 28
% N = (28 ÷ 80) × 100 = 35.0%
Worked example
A 12.5 g sample of impure calcium carbonate reacts with excess acid and gives 4.40 g of carbon dioxide. The impurities do not react. Calculate the percentage purity of the sample.
CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)
Moles CO2 = 4.40 ÷ 44 = 0.100 mol
Moles CaCO3 = 0.100 mol (1 : 1 ratio)
Mass of pure CaCO3 = 0.100 × 100 = 10.0 g
Percentage purity = (10.0 ÷ 12.5) × 100 = 80.0%
Examiner Tip
Use the mole-ratio step every time: moles of known → ratio from the balanced equation → moles of unknown → convert. Write units on every line and keep at least 3 significant figures in your working; round only the final answer, to the same number of significant figures as the data (as in the limiting-reactant example above). Rounding too early is a common way to lose the final mark.
Common Mistake
Forgetting to convert cm3 to dm3 before using concentration or the 24 dm3 molar volume. Another frequent error is ignoring the mole ratio (e.g. treating H2SO4 : NaOH as 1 : 1 instead of 1 : 2).

Electrolysis: key terms

Electrolysis is the decomposition of an ionic compound, when molten or in aqueous solution, by the passage of an electric current.

Electrolyte
The molten or aqueous ionic compound that conducts and is decomposed
Cathode
Negative electrode; attracts cations
Anode
Positive electrode; attracts anions
Inert electrodes
Graphite (carbon) or platinum; do not react
External circuit
Wires and power supply connecting the electrodes

A solid ionic compound does not conduct: its ions are held in a lattice and cannot move. Melting it or dissolving it in water frees the ions, so it can be electrolysed.

PANIC: Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode. OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain of electrons.

Supplement Transfer of charge during electrolysis

1
External circuitElectrons move through the wires from the anode to the cathode, pushed by the power supply.
2
ElectrolyteIons move: cations towards the cathode, anions towards the anode. Ions, not electrons, carry the current in the electrolyte.
3
At the cathodeCations gain electrons (reduction).
4
At the anodeAnions lose electrons (oxidation), and these electrons enter the external circuit.

Predicting products

  • Molten binary compound (two elements, inert electrodes): the metal forms at the cathode and the non-metal at the anode.
  • In general, metals or hydrogen form at the cathode and non-metals (other than hydrogen) form at the anode.

Supplement Aqueous solutions

  • Aqueous solutions also contain H+ and OH− ions from water, so these compete to be discharged.
  • At the cathode: hydrogen forms if the metal is more reactive than hydrogen (e.g. sodium, potassium); the metal forms if it is less reactive (e.g. copper, silver).
  • At the anode: with a concentrated halide solution the halogen forms; with a dilute halide solution, or a sulfate, oxygen forms.
Worked example
Predict the products when molten zinc chloride, ZnCl2, is electrolysed with inert electrodes.
It is a binary compound (two elements) and molten, so the only ions are Zn2+ and Cl−.
Cathode (metal): zinc. Anode (non-metal): chlorine.
Worked example (Supplement)
Predict the products at each electrode, using inert electrodes, for (a) concentrated aqueous potassium bromide and (b) dilute aqueous copper(II) chloride.
(a) Ions present: K+, Br−, H+, OH−. Potassium is more reactive than hydrogen, so hydrogen forms at the cathode. The solution is a concentrated halide, so bromine forms at the anode (the solution near the anode turns orange-brown).
(b) Ions present: Cu2+, Cl−, H+, OH−. Copper is less reactive than hydrogen, so copper forms at the cathode. The halide solution is dilute, so oxygen forms at the anode.

Reduction happens at the cathode (electrons gained); oxidation happens at the anode (electrons lost). Balance atoms and charges: the total charge on each side must be the same.

Writing the chlorine anode equation as Cl− → Cl + e−. Chlorine is diatomic, so it must be 2Cl− → Cl2 + 2e−. Also check the charges balance on both sides, and that electrons appear on the left at the cathode and on the right at the anode.

Electroplating

Electroplating uses electrolysis to coat one metal with a thin layer of another metal.

1
CathodeThe object to be plated (e.g. a steel spoon), cleaned first so the coating sticks evenly.
2
AnodeA piece of the plating metal (e.g. silver).
3
ElectrolyteAn aqueous solution containing ions of the plating metal (e.g. silver nitrate).
4
ResultMetal ions are reduced and deposited on the object (e.g. Ag+(aq) + e− → Ag(s)); the anode dissolves to replace them (Ag(s) → Ag+(aq) + e−), so the electrolyte concentration stays constant.
  • Uses: to improve appearance (e.g. silver or gold plating on jewellery) and to resist corrosion (e.g. chromium on steel taps, tin on steel cans).
Background · not in the 0620 syllabusCopper is purified in the same way: impure copper as the anode, a thin sheet of pure copper as the cathode, aqueous copper(II) sulfate as the electrolyte. Copper moves from anode to cathode; impurities collect below the anode.
Model answer
Aqueous copper(II) sulfate is electrolysed using carbon electrodes. Explain what is seen at each electrode and why the colour of the solution changes. [4]
At the cathode a pink-brown solid forms, because Cu2+ ions gain electrons and are deposited as copper: Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu. Copper is less reactive than hydrogen, so copper is discharged instead of hydrogen. At the anode bubbles of oxygen form, from hydroxide ions losing electrons: 4OH− → O2 + 2H2O + 4e−. The blue colour fades because Cu2+ ions, which cause the blue colour, are removed from the solution and not replaced.

Chemical Energetics

Exothermic and endothermic reactions

Energy is conserved in every reaction. What changes is where it is stored: some energy is held in the chemical bonds (the system), and some is thermal energy in everything around the reaction (the surroundings — the solvent, test tube, air and thermometer).

  • Exothermic reaction: a reaction that transfers thermal energy to the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings increases.
  • Endothermic reaction: a reaction that takes in thermal energy from the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings decreases.
Exothermic examplesEndothermic examples
Combustion of fuels, e.g. CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)Thermal decomposition, e.g. CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g)
Neutralisation, e.g. HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)Photosynthesis (energy taken in as light)
Reactive metals with water or acid, e.g. Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)Dissolving ammonium nitrate or ammonium chloride in water (strictly a physical change, but it takes in thermal energy in the same way)
RespirationCitric acid reacting with sodium hydrogencarbonate

Measuring the temperature change in the lab

  • Carry out the reaction in an insulated container (a polystyrene cup with a lid) to reduce thermal energy loss to the air.
  • Record the starting temperature, mix, stir, and record the highest (or lowest) temperature reached.
  • A rise in temperature shows an exothermic reaction; a fall shows an endothermic reaction.
Common Mistake
Saying “an endothermic reaction gets hotter because it takes in heat”. The reaction mixture and the container actually feel colder, because thermal energy is being drawn out of the surroundings into the chemical bonds of the products.

Reaction pathway diagrams

A reaction pathway diagram (energy level diagram) plots the energy of the chemicals on the vertical axis against the progress of the reaction on the horizontal axis.

Exothermic
Products lower than reactants; energy released to surroundings
Endothermic
Products higher than reactants; energy taken in from surroundings
The “hump”
Energy barrier the reactants must get over: the activation energy
Axes
y: energy; x: progress of reaction
Worked example
On a reaction pathway diagram the reactants are at 200 kJ/mol, the top of the curve is at 290 kJ/mol and the products are at 120 kJ/mol. Is the reaction exothermic or endothermic? Find the activation energy and the enthalpy change (Supplement).
Products (120) are lower than reactants (200), so the reaction is exothermic: energy is given out to the surroundings.
Supplement: Ea = 290 − 200 = 90 kJ/mol (always measured up from the reactants).
ΔH = 120 − 200 = −80 kJ/mol (products minus reactants; negative because exothermic).

Supplement Enthalpy change and activation energy

  • Enthalpy change, ΔH: the transfer of thermal energy during a reaction is called the enthalpy change of the reaction. Units: kJ/mol.
  • For an exothermic reaction ΔH is negative (the system loses energy).
  • For an endothermic reaction ΔH is positive (the system gains energy).
  • Activation energy, Ea: the minimum energy that colliding particles must have in order to react.

Drawing and labelling a pathway diagram (Supplement)

2

Examiner Tip
The activation energy arrow must start at the reactants line, not at the baseline or the products line. Arrows with a head at both ends, or arrows that do not touch the lines, commonly lose the mark.

Supplement Bond breaking and bond making

  • Bond breaking is endothermic — energy must be supplied to pull atoms apart.
  • Bond making is exothermic — energy is released when new bonds form.
  • If more energy is released making bonds than is taken in breaking bonds, the reaction is exothermic overall.
  • If more energy is taken in breaking bonds than is released making bonds, the reaction is endothermic overall.
Breaking In (energy in), Making Out (energy out) — “BIMO”

Calculating ΔH from bond energies

ΔH = total energy needed to break bonds in reactants − total energy released making bonds in products.

Worked example
Calculate ΔH for H2(g) + Cl2(g) → 2HCl(g). Bond energies (kJ/mol): H–H 436, Cl–Cl 242, H–Cl 431.
Bonds broken: 1 × H–H + 1 × Cl–Cl = 436 + 242 = 678 kJ
Bonds made: 2 × H–Cl = 2 × 431 = 862 kJ
ΔH = 678 − 862
ΔH = −184 kJ/mol (exothermic, because more energy is released than taken in)
Worked example
Calculate ΔH for the combustion of methane: CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(g). Bond energies (kJ/mol): C–H 412, O=O 496, C=O 743, O–H 463.
Bonds broken: 4 × C–H = 1648; 2 × O=O = 992; total = 2640 kJ
Bonds made: 2 × C=O = 1486; 4 × O–H (two in each H2O) = 1852; total = 3338 kJ
ΔH = 2640 − 3338
ΔH = −698 kJ/mol (exothermic)
Worked example
Calculate ΔH for the decomposition of hydrogen bromide: 2HBr(g) → H2(g) + Br2(g). Bond energies (kJ/mol): H–Br 366, H–H 436, Br–Br 193.
Bonds broken: 2 × H–Br = 2 × 366 = 732 kJ
Bonds made: 1 × H–H + 1 × Br–Br = 436 + 193 = 629 kJ
ΔH = 732 − 629
ΔH = +103 kJ/mol (endothermic: more energy is taken in breaking bonds than is released making them; keep the + sign)
Common Mistake
Forgetting to count every bond. Draw out the displayed formula of each molecule first, then multiply by the balancing number: 2H2O contains four O–H bonds, and CO2 contains two C=O double bonds. Also always include the sign in the final answer.
Model answer
The combustion of hydrogen is exothermic. Explain why, in terms of bond breaking and bond making. [3]
Energy is taken in to break the bonds in the reactants (H–H and O=O), so bond breaking is endothermic. Energy is released when the new O–H bonds in water form, so bond making is exothermic. More energy is released in making the new bonds than is taken in breaking the old ones, so overall thermal energy is given out and ΔH is negative.
Examiner Tip
When asked whether a reaction is exothermic or endothermic from data, always give the comparison (“more energy released making bonds than absorbed breaking bonds”), not just the name. The comparison carries the mark.

Chemical Reactions

This topic covers how to tell physical and chemical changes apart, what controls the rate of a reaction, reversible reactions and equilibrium (including the Haber and Contact processes), and redox. Rate graphs, collision theory explanations and equilibrium predictions appear on almost every paper.

Physical and chemical changes

  • Physical change: no new substance is made and the change is usually easy to reverse, e.g. melting ice, dissolving salt, boiling water.
  • Chemical change: one or more new substances are formed; often accompanied by an energy change, a colour change, fizzing or a precipitate, and usually hard to reverse, e.g. burning magnesium, rusting.
  • The key difference: in a physical change the particles stay the same substance and only their arrangement or movement changes; in a chemical change atoms are rearranged into new substances (a chemical equation can be written).

Rate of reaction

Rate measures how quickly reactants are used up or products are formed. The five factors on the syllabus (the reverse change has the opposite effect, e.g. lowering the temperature decreases the rate):

Change madeEffect on rateCollision theory reason (Supplement)
Increase concentration of a solutionIncreasesMore particles per unit volume, so more frequent collisions
Increase pressure of a gasIncreasesParticles are closer together, so more particles per unit volume and more frequent collisions
Increase surface area (smaller pieces or powder)IncreasesMore particles exposed at the surface, so more frequent collisions
Increase temperatureIncreasesParticles have more kinetic energy, move faster so collide more frequently, and a greater proportion of collisions have energy ≥ activation energy
Add a catalyst (including an enzyme); removing it lowers the rate againIncreasesProvides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy, so a greater proportion of collisions are successful
  • Catalyst: a substance that increases the rate of a reaction and is unchanged in mass and chemical composition at the end of the reaction.
  • Enzymes are proteins that act as biological catalysts. Background They work best at an optimum temperature and pH; high temperatures denature them.
  • Fine powders (e.g. flour, coal dust) suspended in air can burn so fast that they cause explosions — a hazard in mills and mines.

Supplement Collision theory in detail

Collision theory explains rate using four ideas:

  • Number of particles per unit volume: more reacting particles in the same volume (higher concentration, higher gas pressure) means they meet more often.
  • Frequency of collisions: the number of collisions per second. Rate depends on the frequency of successful collisions.
  • Kinetic energy of particles: faster-moving particles collide more often and hit harder. Kinetic energy rises with temperature.
  • Activation energy, Ea: only collisions with energy equal to or greater than Ea lead to reaction. These are successful collisions.
  • Temperature has two effects: collisions become more frequent, and a larger proportion of particles have energy ≥ Ea. The second effect is the more important one.
  • A catalyst decreases the activation energy, Ea, of a reaction, so a larger proportion of collisions are successful at the same temperature. The catalyst is unchanged at the end.
Common Mistake
Writing “more collisions”. Examiners want more frequent collisions or more collisions per second. Rate is about how often collisions happen in a given time, so “more collisions” without “per second” (or “more frequent”) does not explain a faster rate.

Measuring rate and rate graphs

1
Volume of gas producedCollect the gas in a gas syringe (or an inverted measuring cylinder over water) and record the volume at regular time intervals. Suits e.g. Mg + HCl producing H2.
2
Loss of massPlace the flask on a balance with a cotton wool plug (lets gas out, keeps acid spray in) and record the mass at regular intervals. Suits reactions giving off a dense gas such as CO2 from CaCO3 + HCl.
3
Time for a precipitate to obscure a markSodium thiosulfate + hydrochloric acid produces a sulfur precipitate; time how long until a cross under the flask disappears. Rate is proportional to 1/time.
4

Interpreting rate graphs

  • On a graph of volume of gas (or loss of mass) against time, the gradient shows the rate.
  • The curve is steepest at the start (highest concentration of reactants) and levels off when one reactant is used up.
  • A faster reaction gives a steeper initial curve. If the amounts of reactants are the same, it levels off at the same final volume, just sooner.
  • Halving the amount of the limiting reactant halves the final volume of gas.
  • Average rate = change in volume (or mass) ÷ time taken, in cm3/s or g/s.
  • Supplement Find the rate at one moment by drawing a tangent to the curve at that time and working out its gradient.
Worked example
Magnesium reacts with excess hydrochloric acid. 36 cm3 of hydrogen is collected in the first 30 s. The reaction stops at 150 s, when the total volume is 60 cm3. Calculate (a) the average rate over the first 30 s and (b) the average rate over the whole reaction.
(a) 36 ÷ 30 = 1.2 cm3/s
(b) 60 ÷ 150 = 0.40 cm3/s
The rate is higher at the start because the acid is most concentrated then; it falls as reactants are used up.
Supplement A tangent drawn to the curve at t = 0 passes through (0 s, 0 cm3) and (20 s, 40 cm3). Initial rate = gradient = 40 ÷ 20 = 2.0 cm3/s.
Model answer
Explain why magnesium powder reacts faster with hydrochloric acid than the same mass of magnesium ribbon. [3]
The powder has a larger surface area. More magnesium atoms are exposed to the acid particles, so there are more frequent collisions between acid particles and magnesium atoms. This gives more frequent successful collisions, so the rate is higher.

Reversible reactions

  • Some chemical reactions are reversible: they can go in both directions. This is shown by the symbol ⇌. Changing the conditions can change the direction.
  • Heating blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate drives off water and leaves white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate. Adding water turns it blue again:
    CuSO4·5H2O(s) ⇌ CuSO4(s) + 5H2O(l)
  • Pink hydrated cobalt(II) chloride becomes blue when heated (anhydrous) and turns pink again when water is added:
    CoCl2·6H2O(s) ⇌ CoCl2(s) + 6H2O(l)
  • These colour changes are used as chemical tests for water (anhydrous CuSO4 white → blue; anhydrous CoCl2 blue → pink). To test whether water is pure, check that it boils at exactly 100 °C and freezes at 0 °C.

Supplement Equilibrium

  • A reversible reaction in a closed system reaches equilibrium when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction and the concentrations of reactants and products no longer change.
  • Equilibrium is dynamic: both reactions keep happening.
  • When a condition is changed, the position of equilibrium shifts in the direction that opposes the change. Use the equation and ΔH you are given to predict which way.

Explaining the chosen conditions

  • Temperature: a low temperature would give a higher equilibrium yield (forward reaction is exothermic) but the rate would be too slow. 450 °C is a compromise between rate and yield.
  • Pressure (Haber): high pressure moves equilibrium to the right (4 moles of gas → 2) and increases the rate, but very high pressures need expensive, thick-walled equipment, use a lot of energy and risk explosions; 20 000 kPa balances yield against cost and safety.
  • Pressure (Contact): higher pressure would favour SO3 (3 moles of gas → 2), but the equilibrium already lies well to the right at 200 kPa, so the small gain in yield is not worth the extra cost and risk.
  • Catalyst: increases the rate so equilibrium is reached faster, which allows a reasonable rate at a lower, higher-yield temperature; it does not change the position of equilibrium.
Examiner Tip
For “explain the temperature used” questions, you need both sides: why not lower (rate too slow) and why not higher (yield too low because the forward reaction is exothermic). Mention the word “compromise”.

Redox

  • Oxidation is gain of oxygen; reduction is loss of oxygen.
  • Redox reactions involve simultaneous oxidation and reduction, e.g. in CuO + H2 → Cu + H2O, copper(II) oxide is reduced and hydrogen is oxidised.
  • Roman numerals show the oxidation number of an element in a compound, e.g. iron(II) sulfate contains iron with oxidation number +2 (Fe2+); iron(III) chloride contains iron with oxidation number +3 (Fe3+).
  • Example: 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO. Magnesium gains oxygen, so magnesium is oxidised; oxygen is reduced.

Supplement Redox: electrons and oxidation numbers

  • Oxidation is loss of electrons and an increase in oxidation number. Reduction is gain of electrons and a decrease in oxidation number.
  • Rules: an uncombined element has oxidation number 0; a monatomic ion has oxidation number equal to its charge; in a compound the oxidation numbers add up to 0; in an ion they add up to the charge on the ion. Oxygen is usually −2 and hydrogen usually +1 in compounds.
  • An oxidising agent oxidises another substance and is itself reduced. A reducing agent reduces another substance and is itself oxidised.
  • Example: Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Cu(s)
    Zn → Zn2+ + 2e− (oxidation; Zn is the reducing agent)
    Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu (reduction; Cu2+ is the oxidising agent)
  • Example using oxidation numbers: Fe2O3 + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO2. Iron: +3 → 0 (decrease, reduced). Carbon: +2 in CO → +4 in CO2 (increase, oxidised). So CO is the reducing agent and Fe2O3 is the oxidising agent.
  • Tests using colour changes: acidified potassium manganate(VII) is an oxidising agent and turns from purple to colourless when it reacts with a reducing agent. Aqueous potassium iodide is a reducing agent and turns from colourless to brown (iodine forms) when it reacts with an oxidising agent.
OIL — Oxidation Is Loss (of electrons), RIG — Reduction Is Gain
Worked example (Supplement)
Work out the oxidation number of (a) manganese in KMnO4 and (b) sulfur in the sulfate ion, SO42−.
(a) K is +1 and each O is −2. The compound adds up to 0: (+1) + Mn + 4(−2) = 0, so Mn = +7. This is why it is called potassium manganate(VII).
(b) The ion adds up to its charge: S + 4(−2) = −2, so S = +6.

Acids, Bases and Salts

This topic covers the typical reactions of acids and bases, the pH scale and indicators, the classification of oxides, and how to prepare pure, dry samples of salts. Salt preparation methods are a favourite extended-writing question on both core and extended papers.

Properties of acids

  • Aqueous solutions of acids contain hydrogen ions, H+(aq).
  • Acids have a pH below 7, turn blue litmus red, turn methyl orange red and leave thymolphthalein colourless.
  • Acids react with metals, bases and carbonates (table below). With a metal or a carbonate you see fizzing as a gas is given off.

Properties of bases and alkalis

  • Bases are oxides or hydroxides of metals; they neutralise acids to form a salt and water (e.g. copper(II) oxide, calcium hydroxide).
  • Alkalis are soluble bases (e.g. sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide). Aqueous solutions of alkalis contain hydroxide ions, OH−(aq).
  • Alkalis have a pH above 7, turn red litmus blue, turn methyl orange yellow and turn thymolphthalein blue.
  • Bases react with ammonium salts when warmed, releasing ammonia gas:

The pH scale and neutralisation

  • The pH scale runs from 0 to 14. Below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral, above 7 is alkaline.
  • To measure pH, dip a glass rod in the solution, touch it on universal indicator paper and match the colour to the pH colour chart (a pH meter gives a more precise reading).
  • The lower the pH, the higher the hydrogen ion concentration and the more acidic the solution: pH 1 (red) is more acidic than pH 5 (yellow). The higher the pH above 7, the more alkaline: pH 13 (purple) is more alkaline than pH 9 (blue). Green (pH 7) means neutral.
  • Neutralisation is the reaction between an acid and an alkali to produce water: H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l)

Supplement Proton transfer and strength of acids

  • An acid is a proton (H+) donor; a base is a proton acceptor.
  • A strong acid is completely dissociated in aqueous solution, e.g. hydrochloric acid: HCl(aq) → H+(aq) + Cl−(aq)
  • A weak acid is only partially dissociated in aqueous solution, e.g. ethanoic acid: CH3COOH(aq) ⇌ H+(aq) + CH3COO−(aq)
  • At the same concentration, a strong acid has a higher H+ concentration than a weak acid, so it has a lower pH and reacts faster (e.g. with magnesium).
Background · not in the 0620 syllabusEach fall of 1 pH unit means a tenfold increase in H+ concentration, so pH 2 has ten times the H+ concentration of pH 3.
Common Mistake
Confusing strong with concentrated. Strength is about how completely the acid dissociates; concentration is about how much acid is dissolved per dm3. You can have a dilute strong acid or a concentrated weak acid.

Oxides

  • Acidic oxides are oxides of non-metals, e.g. carbon dioxide, CO2, and sulfur dioxide, SO2. They react with bases (alkalis) to form a salt and water.
  • Basic oxides are oxides of metals, e.g. copper(II) oxide, CuO, and calcium oxide, CaO. They react with acids to form a salt and water.
  • So whether an oxide is acidic or basic follows the element’s metallic or non-metallic character: across a period, oxides go from basic (left, metals) to acidic (right, non-metals).
  • Supplement Amphoteric oxides react with both acids and bases to produce a salt and water, e.g. aluminium oxide, Al2O3, and zinc oxide, ZnO.

The Periodic Table

The Periodic Table organises the elements so that their properties can be predicted from their position. You need to explain trends in Group I, Group VII, the transition elements and the noble gases, and predict the properties of elements you have never met — a common source of “suggest” marks.

Topical past papers for this topic17 real exam questions on The Periodic Table, sorted by sub-topic, with model answers.Practise →

Arrangement of elements

  • The Periodic Table arranges the elements in periods (horizontal rows) and groups (vertical columns), in order of increasing proton number / atomic number.
  • Across a period, elements change from metallic to non-metallic character (e.g. period 3: Na, Mg, Al are metals; Si has properties in between; P, S, Cl and Ar are non-metals).
  • The group number equals the number of outer-shell electrons (Group I has 1, Group VII has 7). Group VIII (Group 0) noble gases have a full outer shell.
  • The period number equals the number of occupied electron shells, e.g. chlorine (2,8,7) is in period 3, Group VII.
  • Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of outer-shell electrons, and chemical reactions involve the outer electrons.
  • The group number tells you the charge on the ion: metals lose their outer electrons, non-metals gain enough to fill the outer shell. Group I forms 1+ ions, Group II 2+, Group III 3+, Group V 3−, Group VI 2− and Group VII 1−. Group IV elements do not usually form simple ions, and noble gases form none.
  • So an element’s position predicts its properties: left-hand side → metal, forms positive ions, basic oxide; right-hand side → non-metal, forms negative ions or covalent compounds, acidic oxide; same group → similar reactions to the other members.

General chemical properties

  • With dilute acids: metal + acid → salt + hydrogen, e.g. Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)
  • With cold water: reactive metals give a metal hydroxide + hydrogen, e.g. 2Na(s) + 2H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g)
  • With steam: metals that react only very slowly with cold water, such as magnesium, react with steam to give a metal oxide + hydrogen, e.g. Mg(s) + H2O(g) → MgO(s) + H2(g)
  • With oxygen: metal + oxygen → metal oxide, e.g. 2Cu(s) + O2(g) → 2CuO(s). Metal oxides are basic.

Alloys

  • An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements (metals or non-metals such as carbon).
  • Brass is a mixture of copper and zinc.
  • Stainless steel is a mixture of iron with other elements such as chromium, nickel and carbon.
  • Alloys can be harder and stronger than the pure metals, which is why they are more useful.
  • Stainless steel is used in cutlery because it is hard and resistant to rusting.
  • Supplement In a pure metal the atoms are all the same size and arranged in regular layers that can slide over one another when a force is applied. In an alloy, the different sized atoms disrupt the regular layers, so the layers can no longer slide over each other easily. This makes the alloy harder and stronger.
  • You should be able to identify an alloy from a diagram of its structure: a regular arrangement of metal atoms with some atoms of a different size mixed in.

Metal displacement reactions

  • A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a solution of its salt or from its oxide.
  • Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s): blue solution fades, pink-brown copper forms, temperature rises.
  • Thermite (background example): 2Al(s) + Fe2O3(s) → Al2O3(s) + 2Fe(l)
  • You can use displacement results (and reactions with water and acid) to put unfamiliar metals into order of reactivity.

Deducing a reactivity order

Worked example
Three metals X, Y and Z are added to dilute hydrochloric acid. X fizzes rapidly, Y does not react, Z fizzes slowly. X is added to a solution of Z’s nitrate and a coating of Z forms. Put the metals in order of reactivity.
Fastest reaction with acid = most reactive, so X > Z > Y. The displacement result agrees: X displaces Z from its salt, so X is more reactive than Z. Y does not react with acid, so it is below hydrogen.
Order (most reactive first): X, Z, Y.
Common Mistake
Saying "copper reacts with dilute acid slowly". Copper is below hydrogen, so it does not react with dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid at all. Also do not say aluminium is unreactive: it is reactive, but protected by its oxide layer.

Corrosion of metals

  • Rusting is the corrosion of iron and steel. It needs both water and oxygen. Rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide.
  • Classic experiment: iron nails in three tubes: (1) air and water → rusts; (2) boiled water with an oil layer (no oxygen) → no rust; (3) dry air with anhydrous calcium chloride (no water) → no rust.
  • Salt water speeds up rusting (background).

Preventing rusting

  • Barrier methods: painting, greasing (or oiling) and coating with plastic. They prevent rusting by excluding oxygen or water from the surface of the iron. They work only while the coating is unbroken.
  • Supplement Galvanising: coating iron or steel with a layer of zinc. It is an example of both a barrier method and sacrificial protection (see below).
  • Supplement Sacrificial protection: a more reactive metal (zinc or magnesium) is attached to the iron. It is higher in the reactivity series, so it loses electrons (is oxidised) in preference to iron and corrodes instead; the iron does not rust. Used on ship hulls and pipelines. Zn(s) → Zn2+(aq) + 2e−
  • Supplement Galvanising protects in two ways: the zinc acts as a barrier, and if scratched the zinc still corrodes sacrificially instead of the iron.
Model answer
Explain why blocks of zinc attached to a steel ship hull stop the hull rusting, even where the paint is scratched. [3]
Zinc is more reactive than iron.
Zinc loses electrons / is oxidised more readily than iron, so the zinc corrodes instead of the iron (sacrificial protection).
Electrons from the zinc flow to the iron, so iron does not form ions / is not oxidised.

Extraction of metals

  • The ease of obtaining a metal from its ore depends on its position in the reactivity series: the more reactive the metal, the more stable its compounds and the harder (and more expensive) it is to extract.
  • Metals above carbon (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al) are extracted by electrolysis of the molten compound.
  • Metals below carbon (Zn, Fe, Cu) are extracted by reduction with carbon (or carbon monoxide).
  • Very unreactive metals such as gold are found uncombined (as the element), so they only need separating from rock.

Aluminium from bauxite

  • The main ore of aluminium is bauxite. It is purified to aluminium oxide, Al2O3 (details of the purification are not required).
  • Aluminium is too reactive to be reduced by carbon, so it is extracted by electrolysis.
  • Supplement Role of cryolite: aluminium oxide is dissolved in molten cryolite. Pure aluminium oxide melts above 2000 °C; the solution in cryolite is molten at about 950 °C, so the operating temperature is lower and less energy is needed. The ions are free to move in the molten electrolyte, so it conducts.
  • Supplement Cathode (−, carbon lining of the cell): Al3+ + 3e− → Al. Aluminium ions are reduced; molten aluminium collects at the bottom and is tapped off.
  • Supplement Anode (+, carbon blocks): 2O2− → O2 + 4e−. Oxide ions are oxidised to oxygen.
  • Supplement Why the anodes are replaced: at the high temperature the oxygen reacts with the carbon anodes to form carbon dioxide, C + O2 → CO2, so the anodes burn away and must be replaced regularly.
Examiner Tip
In the blast furnace, the main reducing agent is carbon monoxide, not coke. Coke's job is to provide heat and to make the CO. Limestone's job is to remove the acidic impurity silicon(IV) oxide as slag — say "removes impurities" alone and you may miss the mark.

Fertilisers

  • Ammonium salts are made by neutralising ammonia with an acid, e.g. NH3(aq) + HNO3(aq) → NH4NO3(aq).

Supplement How greenhouse gases cause warming

  • Energy from the Sun passes through the atmosphere. Some is reflected back into space (by clouds, ice and the surface); the rest is absorbed and warms the Earth's surface.
  • The warm surface emits thermal energy (infrared radiation) back towards space.
  • Greenhouse gases (carbon dioxide, methane) absorb some of this thermal energy and then emit it again in all directions, including back towards the Earth.
  • This reduces thermal energy loss to space. Higher levels of carbon dioxide and methane mean even less energy escapes, so the average temperature rises (global warming), which leads to climate change.

Reducing climate change and acid rain

Climate change

  • Planting trees: trees remove carbon dioxide from the air by photosynthesis.
  • Reducing livestock farming: fewer cattle means less methane from digestion.
  • Decreasing use of fossil fuels: less carbon dioxide from combustion.
  • Increasing use of hydrogen and renewable energy (e.g. wind, solar): hydrogen burns to form only water, and wind and solar power release no carbon dioxide when generating electricity.

Acid rain

  • Catalytic converters in vehicles remove oxides of nitrogen from exhaust gases.
  • Low-sulfur fuels: less sulfur in the fuel means less sulfur dioxide when it burns.
  • Flue gas desulfurisation with calcium oxide in power stations: the basic oxide reacts with acidic sulfur dioxide in the waste gases, e.g. CaO(s) + SO2(g) → CaSO3(s).

Supplement Oxides of nitrogen and catalytic converters

  • Formation: air contains nitrogen and oxygen. Nitrogen is very unreactive, but the very high temperature inside a car engine provides enough energy for it to react with oxygen, e.g. N2(g) + O2(g) → 2NO(g). Nitrogen monoxide then reacts with more oxygen to form nitrogen dioxide: 2NO(g) + O2(g) → 2NO2(g).
  • Removal: a catalytic converter (containing metals such as platinum and rhodium) makes oxides of nitrogen react with carbon monoxide in the exhaust, forming harmless nitrogen and carbon dioxide:
  • 2CO(g) + 2NO(g) → 2CO2(g) + N2(g)
  • 4CO(g) + 2NO2(g) → 4CO2(g) + N2(g)
  • Remaining carbon monoxide is also oxidised: 2CO(g) + O2(g) → 2CO2(g)

Photosynthesis

  • Photosynthesis is the reaction between carbon dioxide and water to produce glucose and oxygen, in the presence of chlorophyll and using energy from light.
  • Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen
  • (Supplement) Symbol equation: 6CO2(g) + 6H2O(l) → C6H12O6(aq) + 6O2(g)
  • Photosynthesis removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, which is why deforestation adds to climate change.

  • Supplement In a substitution reaction one atom or group of atoms is replaced by another atom or group of atoms.
  • Supplement Chlorine substitution is a photochemical reaction: ultraviolet light provides the activation energy, Ea. One hydrogen atom is replaced by a chlorine atom (monosubstitution): CH4 + Cl2 → CH3Cl + HCl, giving chloromethane and hydrogen chloride. With ethane: C2H6 + Cl2 → CH3CH2Cl + HCl (chloroethane).

Alkenes

  • The bonding in alkenes includes a carbon–carbon double covalent bond (C=C); alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons.
  • Alkenes and hydrogen are manufactured by cracking larger alkane molecules, using a high temperature and a catalyst. For example C10H22 → C8H18 + C2H4 (a shorter alkane and an alkene), or C2H6 → C2H4 + H2 (an alkene and hydrogen).
  • Reasons for cracking: supply of long-chain fractions exceeds demand, while demand for shorter chains (petrol) and alkenes (for polymers) is greater than supply.
  • Test for unsaturation: add aqueous bromine. Alkenes turn it from orange to colourless; alkanes leave it orange.
  • Supplement Alkenes undergo addition reactions: in an addition reaction only one product is formed. The C=C opens and the two new atoms or groups join on, one to each carbon.

Alcohols

  • Ethanol is made by fermentation of aqueous glucose at 25–35 °C with yeast, in the absence of oxygen: C6H12O6(aq) → 2C2H5OH(aq) + 2CO2(g)
  • Or by catalytic addition of steam to ethene at 300 °C and 6000 kPa / 60 atm with an acid catalyst: C2H4(g) + H2O(g) ⇌ C2H5OH(g).
  • Combustion: ethanol burns with a clean blue flame, releasing energy; complete combustion forms carbon dioxide and water: C2H5OH(l) + 3O2(g) → 2CO2(g) + 3H2O(l)
  • Uses of ethanol: as a solvent and as a fuel.

Carboxylic acids

  • Ethanoic acid is a weak acid and shows typical acid reactions, forming ethanoate salts:
  • With metals: 2CH3COOH + Mg → (CH3COO)2Mg + H2 (magnesium ethanoate)
  • With bases: CH3COOH + NaOH → CH3COONa + H2O (sodium ethanoate)
  • With carbonates: 2CH3COOH + Na2CO3 → 2CH3COONa + H2O + CO2 (sodium ethanoate; effervescence)
  • The salts are ethanoates containing the CH3COO− ion: sodium ethanoate CH3COONa, magnesium ethanoate (CH3COO)2Mg, calcium ethanoate (CH3COO)2Ca.
  • Supplement Ethanoic acid forms by oxidation of ethanol: (a) with acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII), which turns from purple to colourless as it oxidises the ethanol, or (b) by bacterial oxidation during vinegar production (when ethanol in wine is left open to the air).

Common Mistake
Naming esters the wrong way round. The product of ethanol and propanoic acid is ethyl propanoate, not propyl ethanoate. Also, in formulae remember the C of –COOH is counted in the chain: ethanoic acid is CH3COOH (two carbons).

Polymers

  • Polymers are large molecules built up from many small units called monomers. Plastics are made from polymers.
  • Addition polymerisation: many alkene monomers join together into one long chain; the C=C bond in each monomer opens to make new C–C bonds to its neighbours. Many ethene molecules form poly(ethene): n CH2=CH2 → –[CH2–CH2]n–.
  • Plastics are unreactive and most are non-biodegradable (microorganisms cannot break them down), which is useful while they are in use but makes disposal difficult.
  • Environmental challenges: they take up space and persist for centuries in landfill sites; they accumulate in oceans, where animals swallow them or get entangled; burning them forms toxic gases (e.g. carbon monoxide, and hydrogen chloride from PVC).

Acid–base titrations

1

2
Add indicatorAdd a few drops of a suitable indicator, e.g. methyl orange or thymolphthalein.
3
Fill the buretteRinse and fill the burette with acid; record the initial reading.
4
TitrateAdd acid while swirling, then dropwise near the end-point, until the indicator just changes colour permanently. Record the final reading.
5
RepeatThe first run is a rough titration. Repeat until you have concordant results (within 0.10 cm3) and average them.
  • Identifying the end-point: the end-point is when the indicator just changes colour (one drop of acid makes the change). Methyl orange is yellow in alkali and red in acid, and the end-point is reached when it turns orange; thymolphthalein goes from blue to colourless as acid is added to alkali.
  • Titre = final reading − initial reading. To make a pure salt, repeat without indicator using the known volumes, then crystallise.

Chromatography

  • Paper chromatography separates mixtures of soluble coloured substances using a suitable solvent (one that dissolves the substances; water or ethanol are common).
  • Method: draw a start line in pencil (graphite does not dissolve); spot the samples on it; stand the paper in solvent with the solvent level below the start line (so the spots do not dissolve into the bulk solvent); cover and allow the solvent to rise; remove and mark the solvent front.
  • A pure substance gives one spot; an impure substance (mixture) gives two or more spots.
  • Identify unknowns by comparing their spots with those of known substances run on the same paper: spots at the same height match.
  • Each substance moves a different distance because it has a different solubility in the solvent and a different attraction to the paper.
  • Supplement Mixtures of soluble colourless substances (e.g. amino acids or sugars) are separated in the same way, using a suitable solvent; the dried chromatogram is then sprayed with a locating agent, which makes the spots visible. (You do not need to name specific locating agents.)
  • Supplement Rf = distance travelled by substance ÷ distance travelled by solvent, both measured from the start line. Rf has no units and is always less than 1. A substance has the same Rf in the same solvent, so Rf values can be compared with known values to identify it.
Worked example
Supplement A spot moves 3.6 cm from the start line. The solvent front is 8.0 cm from the start line. Calculate the Rf value.

Rf = 3.6 ÷ 8.0

Supplement Example 1: Reading a heating curve

Practice question · [6 marks]

A pure solid, Z, is heated at a steady rate and its temperature is recorded every 30 seconds. (a) State the melting point and the boiling point of Z. (b) State the physical state or states of Z between B and C, and between D and E. (c) Explain, using the kinetic particle theory, why the temperature does not change between B and C even though Z is still being heated. (d) Suggest why section DE lasts longer than section BC.

Answer:

  1. (a) Melting point = 80 °C (1); boiling point = 218 °C (1). Read them from the two flat sections, not from the ends of the line.
  2. (b) B to C: solid and liquid together (Z is melting); D to E: liquid and gas together (Z is boiling) (1).
  3. (c) The thermal energy supplied is used to overcome the forces of attraction between the particles (1), not to increase the kinetic energy of the particles, so the temperature stays the same until all the solid has melted (1).
  4. (d) Z is heated at a steady rate, so a longer flat section means more energy is needed. Boiling needs more energy than melting because the particles must be separated completely and move far apart, whereas on melting they stay close together and only begin to slide past each other (1).
Examiner Tip
A cooling curve is the same shape read backwards. The flat sections sit at the same temperatures (Z condenses at 218 °C and freezes at 80 °C), and the temperature stays constant there because energy is released as the forces of attraction form again. A pure substance has a sharp, flat melting section; an impure one melts over a range of temperatures.

Supplement Example 2: Dot-and-cross diagrams

Practice question · [7 marks]

(a) Draw dot-and-cross diagrams to show the bonding in magnesium oxide and in carbon dioxide. Show outer-shell electrons only. (b) Magnesium oxide melts at about 2850 °C; carbon dioxide turns into a gas at −78 °C. Use the structure and bonding to explain this difference.

Answer:

  1. (a) Magnesium oxide: each magnesium atom (2,8,2) loses its two outer electrons to an oxygen atom (2,6) (1). This forms Mg2+ and O2−, both with the electronic configuration 2,8 of a noble gas (1). The ions are drawn in square brackets with their charges, and O2− shows six dots and two crosses (1).
  2. (a) Carbon dioxide: the carbon atom shares two pairs of electrons with each oxygen atom, so there are two C=O double bonds (1). Each oxygen keeps two non-bonding pairs, and every atom ends with eight outer electrons (1).
  3. (b) Magnesium oxide is a giant ionic lattice: a regular arrangement of alternating Mg2+ and O2− ions held by strong electrostatic attraction in all directions, so a very large amount of energy is needed to overcome it (1). Carbon dioxide is made of simple molecules; only the weak forces between molecules must be overcome, which needs little energy. The covalent bonds inside each molecule do not break (1).
Common Mistake
Drawing CO2 with a single shared pair between each pair of atoms. Count the electrons: carbon then has only six outer electrons and each oxygen only seven. Also, do not draw the two electrons magnesium has lost still sitting on the Mg2+ ion. Its outer shell is now the full second shell.

Supplement Example 3: Reacting masses and gas volumes

Practice question · [6 marks]

3.25 g of zinc reacts completely with excess dilute sulfuric acid: Zn(s) + H2SO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + H2(g). Calculate (a) the mass of zinc sulfate formed, (b) the volume of hydrogen produced at r.t.p., in cm3, and (c) the number of hydrogen molecules produced. [Ar: Zn = 65, S = 32, O = 16; molar gas volume = 24 dm3 at r.t.p.; Avogadro constant = 6.02 × 1023]

Answer:

  1. Amount of zinc = 3.25 ÷ 65 = 0.0500 mol (1)
  2. Mole ratio Zn : ZnSO4 : H2 = 1 : 1 : 1, so 0.0500 mol of each product forms (1)
  3. (a) Mr of ZnSO4 = 65 + 32 + (4 × 16) = 161; mass = 0.0500 × 161 = 8.05 g (1 + 1)
  4. (b) Volume = 0.0500 × 24 = 1.20 dm3 = 1200 cm3 (1)
  5. (c) Number of molecules = 0.0500 × 6.02 × 1023 = 3.01 × 1022 (1)

Example 4: Electrolysis of brine

Practice question · [8 marks]

Concentrated aqueous sodium chloride is electrolysed using carbon electrodes, as shown. (a) Name the product at each electrode and give a test for each. (b) Explain why sodium is not produced at the cathode. (c) (Supplement) Write ionic half-equations for the reactions at the cathode and at the anode, and state which is oxidation. (d) Name the solution left at the end.

Answer:

  1. (a) Cathode: hydrogen, which gives a squeaky pop with a lighted splint (1 + 1). Anode: chlorine, which bleaches damp litmus paper (1 + 1).
  2. (b) Sodium is more reactive than hydrogen, so the hydrogen ions (from the water) gain electrons instead of the sodium ions (1).
  3. (c) Cathode: 2H+ + 2e− → H2 (1). Anode: 2Cl− → Cl2 + 2e−; this is oxidation, because chloride ions lose electrons (1).
  4. (d) The Na+ and OH− ions are left behind, so the solution becomes aqueous sodium hydroxide (1).
Examiner Tip
The word concentrated matters. With a concentrated halide solution, the halogen forms at the anode. With a dilute solution of a halide, oxygen forms at the anode instead (Supplement). Read the question for the concentration before you name the anode product.

Supplement Example 5: Pathway diagram and bond energies

Practice question · [7 marks]

Ammonia is made from nitrogen and hydrogen: N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g). Bond energies in kJ/mol: N≡N 945, H–H 436, N–H 391. (a) Calculate the enthalpy change, ΔH, for the reaction. (b) Draw a labelled reaction pathway diagram for the reaction, showing ΔH and the activation energy, Ea. (c) State the effect of an iron catalyst on Ea and on ΔH.

Answer:

  1. (a) Bonds broken (energy in): 1 × N≡N + 3 × H–H = 945 + (3 × 436) = 945 + 1308 = 2253 kJ (1)
  2. Bonds made (energy out): 6 × N–H (two NH3 molecules with three N–H bonds each) = 6 × 391 = 2346 kJ (1)
  3. ΔH = 2253 − 2346 = −93 kJ/mol; negative, so the reaction is exothermic (1)
  4. (b) Products drawn lower than reactants, both labelled with formulae (1); Ea arrow pointing up from the reactant level to the top of the curve (1); ΔH arrow pointing down from the reactant level to the product level (1).
  5. (c) The catalyst lowers Ea (dashed curve) but ΔH does not change, because the reactant and product levels stay the same (1).
Common Mistake
Counting only three N–H bonds. The equation makes two ammonia molecules, so six N–H bonds form. Always multiply the bonds in one molecule by the number in front of its formula, and remember the triple bond in N2 counts as one N≡N bond with its own bond energy.

Example 6: Reading a rate graph

Practice question · [8 marks]

Excess lumps of calcium carbonate are added to 50.0 cm3 of 0.200 mol/dm3 hydrochloric acid, and the volume of carbon dioxide is measured. Experiment A is done at 20 °C and experiment B at 40 °C, with everything else the same. CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g). (a) (Supplement) Show that the final volume of gas is 120 cm3 at r.t.p. (b) Use the tangents to find the initial rate of each experiment. (c) Explain why both curves level off at the same volume. (d) (Supplement) Use collision theory to explain why B is faster than A.

Answer:

  1. (a) Amount of HCl = 0.200 × 50.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0100 mol (1). Mole ratio HCl : CO2 = 2 : 1, so amount of CO2 = 0.00500 mol (1). Volume = 0.00500 × 24 000 = 120 cm3 (1).
  2. (b) (Supplement: drawing a tangent is Extended only.) Gradient of each tangent: B: tangent passes through (0, 0) and P (25 s, 105 cm3), so rate = 105 ÷ 25 = 4.2 cm3/s (1). A: through (0, 0) and Q (50 s, 90 cm3), so rate = 90 ÷ 50 = 1.8 cm3/s (1).
  3. (c) The hydrochloric acid is the limiting reactant. It is used up in both experiments, and both start with the same amount, so the same amount of gas forms (1).
  4. (d) At the higher temperature the particles have more kinetic energy and move faster, so they collide more frequently (1). A greater proportion of the collisions have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy, so there are more frequent successful collisions (1).
Examiner Tip
Rate questions often ask you to sketch a new curve. Change the temperature, the surface area or add a catalyst and only the steepness changes, while the final volume stays the same. Change the amount of the limiting reactant (for example half the volume of acid) and the final volume changes too.

Answer:

  2. (b) Ignore the rough titre. Titres 1, 2 and 3 are concordant (all within 0.10 cm3): (20.30 + 20.20 + 20.25) ÷ 3 = 20.25 cm3 (1)
  3. (c) Amount of H2SO4 = 0.0500 × 20.25 ÷ 1000 = 1.0125 × 10−3 mol (1)

Common Mistake
Treating the reaction as 1 : 1. Sulfuric acid has two hydrogen ions to give, so each mole reacts with two moles of NaOH; a 1 : 1 answer (0.0405 mol/dm3) is out by a factor of two. Also keep the unrounded amounts in your calculator and round only the final answer to 3 significant figures.

Example 8: Making copper(II) sulfate crystals

Practice question · [8 marks]

A student makes hydrated copper(II) sulfate crystals, CuSO4•5H2O, from 40.0 cm3 of 1.00 mol/dm3 dilute sulfuric acid and copper(II) oxide. CuO(s) + H2SO4(aq) → CuSO4(aq) + H2O(l). (a) Explain why the copper(II) oxide is added in excess. (b) Use the flow chart to explain the purpose of steps 2, 3 and 5. (c) (Supplement) Calculate the minimum mass of copper(II) oxide needed. (d) (Supplement) The student obtains 7.50 g of crystals. Calculate the percentage yield. [Ar: Cu = 64, S = 32, O = 16, H = 1]

Answer:

  1. (a) So that all the sulfuric acid reacts and none is left in the salt solution; the unreacted solid is easy to remove by filtering (1).
  2. (b) Step 2 removes the excess (unreacted) copper(II) oxide as the residue (1). Step 3 evaporates some of the water to make a saturated solution, so crystals form on cooling (1). Step 5 separates the crystals, and the small amount of cold water rinses away the remaining solution without dissolving many crystals (1).
  3. (c) Amount of H2SO4 = 1.00 × 40.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0400 mol (1). Ratio 1 : 1, so 0.0400 mol CuO; Mr of CuO = 64 + 16 = 80; mass = 0.0400 × 80 = 3.20 g (1).
  4. (d) Mr of CuSO4•5H2O = 64 + 32 + (4 × 16) + (5 × 18) = 250. Maximum mass = 0.0400 × 250 = 10.0 g (1). Percentage yield = 7.50 ÷ 10.0 × 100 = 75.0% (1).
Examiner Tip
Do not evaporate the solution to dryness. Hydrated crystals need their water of crystallisation, and heating them dry leaves a white powder of anhydrous copper(II) sulfate. Write “heat until crystals start to form on a cold glass rod, then leave to cool”.

Example 9: Chromatogram and Rf values

Practice question · [6 marks]

A food colouring, X, is compared with four known dyes, A to D, by paper chromatography. (a) Give two reasons why the start line is drawn in pencil and placed above the level of the solvent. (b) Identify the dyes in X. (c) Which known dye is not a pure substance? Explain. (d) (Supplement) Calculate the Rf value of dye A.

Answer:

  1. (a) Pencil is insoluble in the solvent, so the line does not run up the paper (1). If the start line were below the solvent level, the spots would dissolve into the solvent in the beaker instead of moving up the paper (1).
  2. (b) X contains A and C: its two spots are at the same heights as the spots of A and C (1).
  3. (c) D, because it separates into two spots; a pure substance gives only one spot (1).
  4. (d) Rf = distance travelled by substance ÷ distance travelled by solvent = 6.3 ÷ 9.0 (1) = 0.70 (1). Rf has no units and is always less than 1.
Common Mistake
Measuring to the top of the paper instead of the solvent front, or measuring from the bottom edge instead of the start line. Both distances in the Rf fraction start at the start line, and the spot distance is taken to the centre of the spot.

Supplement Example 10: From monomer to repeat unit

Practice question · [5 marks]

Propene, CH2=CHCH3, forms the addition polymer poly(propene). (a) Draw the displayed formula of the repeat unit of poly(propene). (b) The repeat unit of another addition polymer, poly(chloroethene), is shown. Deduce the displayed formula of its monomer. (c) Explain why addition polymerisation forms only one product.

Answer:

  1. (a) Two carbon atoms joined by a single bond, with extension bonds on each side through square brackets and a subscript n (1). Three H atoms and one CH3 group, with the CH3 as a side group, not part of the main chain (1).
  2. (b) Remove the brackets and extension bonds and put the double bond back: chloroethene, CH2=CHCl, drawn with a C=C double bond, three H atoms and one Cl atom (1 + 1).
  3. (c) The monomers simply join together as the C=C double bond in each one opens, so no atoms are lost and the polymer is the only product (1).
Why this scores
Every addition polymer has a two-carbon backbone in its repeat unit, whatever is hanging off it. Copy the four groups around the C=C exactly, then change the double bond to a single bond and add the extension bonds. To go backwards, do the reverse.
