Exam Technique: How Business Answers Are Marked

Business Studies feels easy until the results come back. The content is common sense; the marks are not. Examiners award marks for four specific skills, and an answer that skips one of them is capped however “correct” it sounds. This section shows you what each question part wants and how to build answers that hit every mark.

The two papers

Paper 1
Short answer & data response · 1 h 30 min · 80 marks · 50%
Paper 1 layout
4 questions × 20 marks, each on a short business scenario
Each Paper 1 question
(a) 2 · (b) 2 · (c) 4 · (d) 6 · (e) 6
Paper 2
Case study · 1 h 30 min · 80 marks · 50%
Paper 2 layout
One long case in an insert, with appendices of data · 4 questions × 20 marks
Each Paper 2 question
(a) 8 · (b) 12
Check your syllabus year
This layout is for Cambridge IGCSE Business Studies 0450. Always check the specimen paper for your exam year in case Cambridge changes the format. Sitting Edexcel International GCSE (4BS1) or OxfordAQA International GCSE (9225) instead? The content in these notes covers almost all of those syllabuses too, but the question styles differ: see “Other exam boards” at the end of this section.

The four skills examiners mark

Every mark you earn is for one of four assessment objectives. Learn them, because the longer questions are built to test all four in a fixed order.

K
Knowledge & understandingA correct business point, term or idea. “Batch production lets a firm make products in groups.”
A
ApplicationUsing the case: the product, the numbers, the people, the problem. “Mei’s bakery could bake trays of 60 croissants before switching the ovens to muffins.” Just writing the business name does not count.
An
AnalysisThe “so what?”: the effect on costs, revenue, profit, customers, workers or reputation. “…so average cost per item falls, raising profit per item.”
E
EvaluationA judgement with a reason, comparing the options. “Batch is better than job because the bakery’s demand is now high and steady, so the lower unit cost matters more than customisation.”

What each question part needs

PartTypical wordingMarks come fromHow to write it
P1 (a) [2]Define / What is meant by…K onlyOne precise sentence using the key words. No example needed, no case needed.
P1 (b) [2]Identify two… / Calculate…K onlyTwo short points, or the formula, the working and the answer with units.
P1 (c) [4]Identify and explain… / Outline two…K + AppTwo points, each with one sentence linking it to this business.
P1 (d) [6]Explain two reasons / advantages / ways…K + App + An for each pointTwo paragraphs: point → case link → consequence.
P1 (e) [6]Do you think…? Justify your answer.K, An, EArgue both options with consequences, then decide and say why the other option is weaker.
P2 (a) [8]Explain two/four… (often “using Appendix 2”)K + App + AnEach point clearly labelled, applied to the case, with developed consequences.
P2 (b) [12]Consider the advantages and disadvantages of… Recommend which… Justify your answer.K, App, An, E (levels)Each option: one advantage and one disadvantage, each developed. Finish with a comparative, justified recommendation.

The “so what?” chain

Most lost marks come from stopping too early. After every point, ask “So what does that mean for this business?” and keep going until you reach one of the business outcomes: costs, revenue, profit, cash flow, market share, reputation, motivation, productivity or survival.

Point what it is → Case who, what, which number → This means… first effect → As a result… effect on the business outcome
One point, three versions

Weak (K only): “Training improves skills.”

Better (K + App): “Training would improve the skills of the factory workers who assemble the e-scooters.”

Full marks (K + App + An): “Training would improve the skills of the factory workers who assemble the e-scooters. This means they make fewer errors, so the 45 faults per 1,000 scooters should fall. As a result, fewer bikes are returned, which cuts the cost of repairs and protects the brand’s reputation and future sales.”

Getting application marks

  • Underline the case details as you read: product, location, size, numbers, owner’s aims, problems. Each one is a ready-made application mark.
  • Use the numbers. “Returns of 4% are four times the industry average” is application; “returns are high” is barely so.
  • Make the answer impossible to copy into another case. If your paragraph would work word for word for a bakery and a car factory, it has no application.
  • Do not use the same case detail to earn two different application marks.

The 12-mark recommendation

Paper 2 (b) is marked in levels. Lists of points sit in the bottom level; balanced, applied analysis of every option sits in the middle; a recommendation that compares the options and explains why the others are worse reaches the top.

1
Option 1: advantage + disadvantageEach developed with the case and a consequence.
2
Option 2 (and 3): advantage + disadvantageGive each option equal weight; a lopsided answer looks unbalanced.
3
Recommendation“I recommend X because…” plus a new reason from the case.
4
Why not the others“Y is less suitable because…” This rejection is what most students forget.
5
It depends on…One condition that would change your mind: the size of the loan, whether demand stays high, how workers react.
Common Mistake
Writing two conclusions that recommend the same thing in different words, or concluding “X is better because it has more advantages”. A justified conclusion weighs the most important factor for this business, and explains why it outweighs the drawback you identified.

Calculations

  • Write the formula, then the numbers, then the answer. A slip in the arithmetic can still earn a method mark.
  • Always give units: $, %, units, months, and the currency used in the case (JOD, AED, RM).
  • Show a loss or negative figure with a minus sign or brackets: (2,700) or −2,700.
  • Say whether a change is a rise or a fall. “2.7 million” is half an answer; “profit fell by $2.7 million” is the full answer.
  • After calculating in Paper 2, interpret: is the figure good or bad, and what should the business do about it? See the Key Formulas page.

Timing

PaperPlan
Paper 1 (90 min)About 20 minutes per question: (a) 2 min · (b) 2 min · (c) 4 min · (d) 6 min · (e) 6 min. Leave 10 minutes to check.
Paper 2 (90 min)10 minutes reading and annotating the insert, then 20 minutes per question: about 8 min for (a) and 12 min for (b).

Common mistakes

Common Mistake
Giving more points than asked. If the question says “Identify two”, only the first two are marked. A third point never rescues a wrong one.
  • Defining a term with the word itself (“a stakeholder is someone with a stake”).
  • Explaining the advantage of something in general instead of for the business in the case.
  • Repeating the case text back to the examiner without saying what it means.
  • Recommending without rejecting the alternative, or sitting on the fence.
  • Leaving out units, or confusing profit with revenue.

Other exam boards

BoardWhat’s different
OxfordAQA International GCSE Business (9225)Questions use State, Explain, Analyse (6 marks) and a 12-mark “analyse … and recommend” based on several Items. “Analyse” wants developed chains of reasoning on the case data; the 12-mark recommendation is marked like Cambridge Paper 2 (b).

Whichever board you sit, the K → App → An → E technique above is exactly what the long questions reward.

Quick Checklist

  • I know the structure of Paper 1 and Paper 2 and the marks for each part.
  • I can label each sentence of my answer as K, App, An or E.
  • I keep asking “so what?” until I reach profit, costs, revenue, reputation or motivation.
  • I quote numbers and details from the case to earn application.
  • I write formula → working → answer with units for every calculation.
  • My recommendations compare the options and reject the alternative with a reason.
Topic 1 · 1.1–1.5

Understanding Business Activity

Why businesses exist, how they are classified, why some grow and others fail, the different legal forms they can take, and who they are trying to please. Questions on business types and objectives appear on almost every paper.

1.1 Business activity

Needs, wants and scarcity

  • Need: a good or service essential for survival, such as food, water and shelter.
  • Want: a good or service people would like to have but can live without, such as a new phone.
  • Scarcity: there are not enough resources to satisfy everyone’s unlimited wants, so choices must be made.
  • Opportunity cost: the next best alternative given up when a choice is made. A café that spends its savings on a new coffee machine gives up the chance to use that money for a delivery scooter.

Factors of production

FactorMeaningExampleReward
LandNatural resourcesPalm oil plantations, water, mineralsRent
LabourHuman effort, physical and mentalCashiers, engineers, designersWages / salaries
CapitalMan-made goods used to make other goodsMachinery, vans, computersInterest
EnterpriseThe skill and risk-taking of bringing the other factors togetherThe founder of a bubble-tea chainProfit

Specialisation and the division of labour

Specialisation is when people or businesses concentrate on what they do best. The division of labour splits production into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Workers become skilled and fast at one task, so output per worker (productivity) rises.
Less time is lost moving between tasks.
Training is quicker and cheaper.		 Repetitive work becomes boring, lowering motivation and quality.
If one worker is absent, the whole process can stop.
Workers have narrow skills, so they are harder to move to other jobs.

Why businesses exist and added value

Businesses combine the factors of production to make goods and services that satisfy needs and wants. Added value is the difference between the selling price and the cost of bought-in materials and components.

Example
A florist buys flowers, paper and ribbon for RM25 and sells the finished bouquet for RM80. Added value = RM80 − RM25 = RM55. Added value is not profit: wages, rent and other costs still have to be paid out of it.

Ways to increase added value: raise the price through branding, better packaging, a premium location or excellent service; or cut the cost of materials by finding cheaper suppliers or reducing waste. Each has a risk: higher prices may lose customers, and cheaper materials may lower quality.

1.2 Classification of businesses

Economic sectors

Primary
Extracting natural resources: farming, fishing, mining, oil
Secondary
Manufacturing and construction: turning raw materials into products
Tertiary
Services: banking, retail, tourism, transport, tutoring

As a country develops, the share of output and jobs moves from primary to secondary (industrialisation) and then towards tertiary (de-industrialisation). This happens because incomes rise and people spend more on services, and because manufacturing moves to countries with lower costs.

Private and public sector

  • Private sector: businesses owned by individuals or shareholders, usually aiming for profit.
  • Public sector: organisations owned and controlled by the government, such as public hospitals, state schools and some utilities. Their aim is usually to provide a service rather than make a profit.
  • A mixed economy has both.

1.3 Enterprise, business growth and size

Entrepreneurs

An entrepreneur organises the factors of production and takes the risk of starting a business. Useful characteristics: hard-working, creative, a risk-taker, self-confident, good at communicating and able to bounce back from failure.

Why people start businesses: to be their own boss, to turn a hobby or idea into income, to fill a gap in the market, or after redundancy.

Business plans

A business plan is a document describing the business, its objectives, its market and its financial forecasts. It helps the owner think through the idea and set targets, and it is usually needed to persuade a bank to lend money. However, it takes time to write and its forecasts may turn out to be wrong.

Government support for start-ups

Governments support new firms because they create jobs, add competition, can grow into large firms and pay taxes. Support includes grants and cheap loans, business advice, low-rent premises (business parks and incubators) and training.

Measuring business size

MethodLimitation
Number of employeesA capital-intensive firm can be large with few workers.
Value of output / revenueA firm with high prices may look larger than one selling many cheap items.
Capital employedA labour-intensive service firm may have little capital but many staff.
Market shareOnly useful within the same market.
Common Mistake
Using profit to measure size. Profit tells you about performance, not size: a huge firm can make a loss in a bad year.

How businesses grow

  • Internal (organic) growth: opening new branches, launching new products, selling in new markets. It is slower but lower-risk and keeps control and culture.
  • External growth: a merger (two firms agree to join) or a takeover (one firm buys a majority of the other’s shares). It is quick but can cause culture clashes and job losses.
Type of integrationJoins with…ExampleMain benefit
HorizontalA firm at the same stage in the same industryOne supermarket chain buys anotherBigger market share, less competition, economies of scale
Vertical backwardA supplierA chocolate maker buys a cocoa farmSecure, cheaper supplies; control of quality
Vertical forwardA customer business further along the chainA clothing factory buys a chain of shopsControls how products are sold; keeps the retailer’s profit
ConglomerateA firm in an unrelated industryA property company buys a hotel chainSpreads risk across markets

Problems of growth and why small firms survive

Growth can bring communication problems, weaker control, cash-flow strain and diseconomies of scale. Many firms stay small because the owner wants control or a manageable workload, the market is small or local, or customers value personal service.

Why businesses fail

  • Poor management and planning, especially of cash flow.
  • Failure to adapt to changes in tastes, technology or competition.
  • Over-expansion: growing too fast and running out of cash.
  • New businesses are especially at risk: little experience, no established customers, limited finance and no brand reputation.

  • Unlimited liability: the owner is personally responsible for all business debts, so personal assets such as a house or car can be taken to pay them.
  • Limited liability: shareholders can only lose the amount they invested. Their personal assets are protected.
  • Incorporation: a company is a separate legal entity from its owners. It can sue and be sued and continues even if shareholders change.
Examiner Tip
When explaining unlimited liability, finish the chain: “…so if the business cannot repay its loan, the owner may have to sell their own house or car to pay the debt.” The cause of debt (e.g. falling sales, a large loan) is application; the loss of personal assets is the analysis.
Common Mistake
Saying shareholders “cannot lose money” with limited liability. They can lose everything they invested; what is protected is their personal wealth beyond that.

1.5 Business objectives and stakeholders

Why set objectives?

  • They give everyone a clear target, so efforts are co-ordinated and staff can be motivated.
  • They guide decision-making: managers can test each choice against the objective.
  • They allow performance to be measured, showing whether a strategy is working.
  • They help persuade banks and investors that the business is well planned.

Good objectives are SMART: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic and Time-bound. “Sell 150 units per month by December” is SMART; “grow the business” is not.

Common objectives

Survival
Key for new firms and in recessions
Profit
Reward for owners; funds for reinvestment
Growth
Economies of scale, more security, higher status
Market share
Market power and brand recognition
Service to society
Social enterprises and the public sector

Objectives change over time: survival first, then profit, then growth. A social enterprise has social and environmental objectives alongside financial ones and reinvests most of its profit in its cause (sometimes called the triple bottom line: profit, people, planet).

Stakeholders

A stakeholder is any individual or group with an interest in, or affected by, the activities of a business.

StakeholderMain objectives
Owners / shareholders (internal)High profit, dividends, rising share price
Managers and workers (internal)Good pay, job security, safe conditions, promotion
CustomersGood quality, low prices, safe products, good service
SuppliersRegular orders, fair prices, being paid on time
Lenders (banks)Repayment with interest on time
Local communityJobs, low pollution, little traffic and noise
GovernmentTaxes paid, jobs created, laws followed, economic growth

Conflict between stakeholders is common. Moving production to cheaper machinery may raise owners’ profit but cost workers their jobs; a new factory brings jobs to the community but also pollution. Businesses usually have to compromise, and in questions you should say which stakeholder’s view should matter most in the case and why.

Objectives in the public and private sector

Private sector firms usually focus on profit and growth. Public sector organisations focus on providing a service, keeping costs within budget and meeting social aims, such as keeping bus fares affordable.

Quick Checklist

  • I can define scarcity and opportunity cost and give a business example.
  • I can calculate added value and explain two ways to increase it.
  • I can classify a business by sector and explain why sectors change as countries develop.
  • I can explain the four types of integration with an example and a benefit for each.
  • I can compare sole traders, partnerships, Ltds, plcs, franchises and joint ventures.
  • I can explain limited and unlimited liability with the full consequence.
  • I can explain why objectives matter and identify conflicts between stakeholders.
Topic 2 · 2.1–2.4

People in Business

How businesses motivate, organise, recruit, train and communicate with their workers. This is the most common topic for Paper 2 recommendation questions: “which method of motivation…”, “should the business delayer…”, “internal or external recruitment…”.

2.1 Motivating employees

Motivation is the set of reasons that make someone want to work hard and do the job well. Motivated workers are more productive, make fewer mistakes, are absent less often and are less likely to leave (lower labour turnover), which cuts recruitment and training costs.

Motivation theories

Maslow levelHow a business can meet it
PhysiologicalEnough pay to afford food and housing; breaks
SecurityPermanent contracts, safe working conditions, pension
SocialTeam working, staff events, friendly workplace
EsteemPraise, awards, job titles, promotion
Self-actualisationChallenging work, chances to develop and reach full potential
  • Taylor: workers are motivated mainly by money, so pay them according to output (piece rate). Works for simple, measurable tasks but ignores social and esteem needs, and can lower quality as workers rush.
  • Herzberg: hygiene factors (pay, conditions, supervision, job security) do not motivate, but if they are poor they cause dissatisfaction. Motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility, interesting work, promotion) create real motivation. Fix hygiene factors first, then add motivators.

Financial methods

MethodHow it worksStrengthWeakness
Wages (time rate)Paid per hour, usually weeklyEasy to calculate; pay is secureNo reward for working harder
SalaryFixed annual amount, paid monthlySecurity; suits jobs where output is hard to measureNo direct link to performance
Piece ratePaid per unit producedEncourages speed; cost linked to outputQuality may fall; no pay if machines break
Commission% of value of sales madeMotivates sales staffPushy selling; unstable income
BonusExtra lump sum for meeting a targetRewards achievementResentment if targets seem unfair
Profit sharingShare of profit to all staffWorkers share the business’s goalsSmall sums; no link to individual effort
Fringe benefitsNon-cash extras: car, health insurance, discountsValued, help attract staffCostly; may be taken for granted

Non-financial methods

  • Job rotation: moving between tasks at the same level. Reduces boredom and creates flexible workers, but the tasks may be equally dull.
  • Job enlargement: adding more tasks of a similar level to a job.
  • Job enrichment: giving more challenging tasks and responsibility. Meets esteem and self-actualisation needs, but needs training and not everyone wants responsibility.
  • Team working: groups take responsibility for a section of work. Meets social needs and can improve ideas and quality.
  • Training and opportunities for promotion.
  • Recognition and praise, such as employee of the month.
Examiner Tip
In a “which method” question, match the method to the workers in the case. Piece rate suits factory workers making identical items but not nurses or teachers; commission suits sales staff; job enrichment suits skilled, experienced staff who are bored rather than underpaid.

2.2 Organisation and management

Organisational structure

An organisational structure shows the levels of management and who reports to whom. It makes clear each person’s role, who they are accountable to and how communication flows, which supports delegation and accountability.

  • Level of hierarchy: a level of management or employees with the same authority.
  • Chain of command: the route through which instructions pass down the hierarchy.
  • Span of control: the number of subordinates directly controlled by one manager.
Tall structure (narrow span)Flat structure (wide span)
CommunicationSlow; messages pass through many levels and can be distortedFaster and clearer between top and bottom
SupervisionClose supervision and controlLess supervision; staff must be trusted and skilled
CostsMany managers’ salariesFewer managers, lower costs
MotivationMore promotion stepsMore delegation and responsibility, but fewer promotion chances and managers may be overloaded

Delayering removes one or more levels of management, making the structure flatter. It saves salary costs and speeds up communication and decisions, but may cause redundancies, lower morale among remaining staff, and wider spans that managers struggle to supervise.

The role of management

PlanningOrganisingCo-ordinatingCommandingControlling

Delegation is giving a subordinate the authority to carry out a task. It frees the manager’s time for important decisions and motivates staff through trust and responsibility, but the manager remains accountable and loses some control, and the task may be done less well if staff lack skills.

Leadership styles

StyleFeaturesBest whenDrawback
AutocraticLeader decides alone; one-way communicationQuick decisions needed, emergencies, unskilled staffDemotivates skilled staff; no use of workers’ ideas
DemocraticWorkers’ views sought; two-way communication; leader makes final decisionSkilled, experienced staff; changes that need supportSlower decisions
Laissez-faireWorkers make their own decisions within broad limitsCreative, highly skilled, self-motivated teamsLack of direction and co-ordination

Trade unions

A trade union is a group of workers who join together to protect their interests, such as pay, working conditions and job security. Members gain collective bargaining power, legal advice and support if disciplined. Unions may take industrial action (overtime ban, go-slow, strike). For the business, a union gives one point of contact for negotiating with many workers, but strikes can stop production and raise costs.

2.3 Recruitment, selection and training

The recruitment and selection process

1
Identify the vacancyA worker leaves, the business grows, or a new skill is needed.
2
Job analysis and job descriptionThe job description lists the title, duties, responsibilities and who the worker reports to.
3
Person specificationThe qualifications, experience, skills and qualities the ideal candidate should have, often split into essential and desirable.
4
Advertise the jobInternally or externally (job websites, social media, recruitment agencies, newspapers).
5
Applications and shortlistingApplication forms or CVs are compared with the person specification, and the best candidates are shortlisted.
6
SelectionInterviews, tests (skills, aptitude, personality), references; the best candidate is offered the job and signs a contract.
Internal recruitmentExternal recruitment
AdvantagesCheaper and quicker; the candidate already knows the business; managers know their ability; motivates staff through promotion chancesWider choice of candidates; brings in new ideas and experience
DisadvantagesSmall choice; no new ideas; creates another vacancy; may cause jealousyMore expensive and slower; candidate is unknown; needs more induction

Interviews let the business judge communication skills and personality and ask follow-up questions, but they take managers’ time, some candidates perform well in interviews but not in the job, and the interviewer may be biased towards people similar to themselves.

Part-time and full-time workers

Part-time staff give flexibility (cover busy periods such as weekends) and cost less, but may be less committed and harder to communicate with. Full-time staff are more committed and easier to manage but less flexible and more expensive.

Training

TypeWhat it isAdvantagesDisadvantages
InductionIntroduces new staff to the business, its rules, layout and colleaguesNew staff settle quickly and become productive soonerCosts time; worker not yet productive
On-the-jobLearning by watching and working alongside an experienced workerCheap; trainee produces while learning; specific to the jobBad habits passed on; trainer’s output falls
Off-the-jobCourses at a college or training centre, away from the workplaceExpert trainers; wider skills; no disruption to productionExpensive; staff may use new skills to get a better job elsewhere

Reducing the size of the workforce

  • Dismissal (being fired): because of the worker’s fault, such as misconduct or poor performance.
  • Redundancy: the job is no longer needed (falling demand, new machinery, relocation, delayering). It is not the worker’s fault, so redundancy pay is usually given.
  • Alternatives: natural wastage (not replacing workers who leave), early retirement, offering part-time contracts.

Legal controls on employment

Laws protect workers from unfair dismissal and discrimination (gender, race, religion, age, disability), set a minimum wage, limit working hours and require safe working conditions. They raise costs for businesses, but they also protect the firm’s reputation and help attract good workers.

2.4 Internal and external communication

Effective communication means the message is received and understood by the receiver, who gives feedback. Every communication has a sender, a medium, a receiver and feedback.

  • Internal: within the business (manager to staff). External: with people outside (customers, suppliers, banks, government).
  • One-way: no feedback expected (a notice). Two-way: the receiver responds (a meeting).
MethodAdvantagesDisadvantages
Verbal (meetings, phone, video call)Quick; instant feedback; tone and body language help understandingNo written record; long meetings waste time; details forgotten
Written (email, letters, reports, notices)Permanent record; can be referred back to; detailedNo immediate feedback; may be ignored or misunderstood; email overload
Visual (charts, videos, posters)Makes data and instructions easy to understandMay need explanation; not good for detailed or complex messages

Barriers to communication and how to reduce them

  • Jargon or language the receiver does not understand → use simple language and check understanding.
  • Long chain of command distorts messages → flatter structure, direct messages.
  • Wrong medium (complex instructions by phone) → choose written or visual methods for detail.
  • Noise and distractions, or a receiver who does not trust the sender → quiet settings, build good relationships, ask for feedback.

Quick Checklist

  • I can explain why motivation matters using productivity and labour turnover.
  • I can outline Maslow, Taylor and Herzberg and apply them to a workforce.
  • I can choose and justify financial and non-financial methods for different workers.
  • I can compare tall and flat structures and analyse the effects of delayering.
  • I can compare autocratic, democratic and laissez-faire leadership.
  • I can describe each stage of recruitment and selection, and compare internal and external recruitment.
  • I can compare induction, on-the-job and off-the-job training.
  • I can distinguish dismissal from redundancy and explain barriers to communication.
Topic 3 · 3.1–3.4

Marketing

How businesses find out what customers want, segment their market, and use the four Ps (product, price, place, promotion) to sell to them, at home and abroad. Expect pricing-method and promotion questions in Paper 1 and “which marketing strategy” recommendations in Paper 2.

3.1 Marketing, competition and the customer

Marketing is identifying customers’ needs and wants and satisfying them profitably. Its roles: identify and anticipate customer needs, satisfy them, keep customer loyalty, build relationships, and gain information about the market.

Why markets change

  • Customers’ spending patterns change with income, fashion, technology and lifestyle (for example, growing demand for plant-based food).
  • Competition grows, including from overseas firms and online sellers.

Businesses respond by keeping close to customers (research), improving products and efficiency, and developing new products. Firms that do not adapt lose sales and market share.

Niche and mass markets

Niche marketMass market
MeaningA small, specialised segment of a larger marketA large market with similar products bought by most customers
ExampleHandmade halal skincare; vintage camerasBottled water; toothpaste
AdvantagesLess competition; can charge higher prices; loyal customers; suits small firmsHuge sales volume; economies of scale; spreads risk

Market segmentation

A market segment is an identifiable sub-group of a market whose members share similar characteristics. Markets can be segmented by age, gender, income, location, lifestyle, use of the product or ethnicity/religion. Segmenting lets a firm target the right customers with the right product and promotion, so marketing spending is not wasted, and it can reveal gaps in the market.

3.2 Market research

Market research is gathering information about customers, competitors and market trends. It reduces the risk of launching a product nobody wants.

  • Market-orientated businesses research customers first, then make what they want.
  • Product-orientated businesses make a product they believe is good, then try to sell it (common for innovative or basic products).
Primary (field) researchSecondary (desk) research
MeaningNew data collected first-hand for a specific purposeData that already exists, collected by someone else
MethodsQuestionnaires (online, face-to-face), interviews, focus groups, observation, test marketingGovernment statistics, trade publications, newspapers, competitors’ websites and reports, internal sales records
AdvantagesUp to date, relevant to exactly what the firm needs; competitors do not have itCheap or free, quick to obtain, large data sets
DisadvantagesExpensive and time-consuming; risk of bias in sample or questionsMay be out of date, not exactly relevant, available to competitors

Sampling: it is too costly to ask everyone, so a sample is chosen. A random sample gives everyone an equal chance of selection; a quota sample picks set numbers from each group (e.g. 30 women aged 18–25). A sample that is too small or unrepresentative gives misleading results.

Accuracy of research depends on sample size and selection, question design (leading questions cause bias), honesty of respondents and how old the data is.

Presenting data: tables, bar charts, pie charts and line graphs. Line graphs show trends over time; pie charts show shares of a total such as market share.

3.4 Marketing strategy

A marketing strategy is a plan combining the four Ps to achieve a marketing objective. A good strategy is based on research into the target market and fits the budget. A luxury watch brand would use high prices, exclusive retailers and glossy magazine adverts; a new budget snack would use penetration pricing, supermarkets and sales promotions.

Legal controls on marketing

Laws protect consumers against misleading descriptions and advertising, unsafe or faulty products, and unfair contracts. Businesses must make sure products match their description and are safe, which can raise costs but builds trust.

Entering new foreign markets

Opportunities: a much larger market, extra growth when the home market is saturated, spreading risk across countries.
Problems: different languages, cultures and tastes; lack of local knowledge; different laws; import tariffs and transport costs; exchange-rate changes; strong local competitors.

Methods of reducing the risks: a joint venture with a local firm (local knowledge), licensing a local firm to make the product, franchising, or selling through local agents and distributors.

Topic 4 · 4.1–4.4

Operations Management

How goods and services are produced efficiently, what it costs, how many units are needed to break even, how quality is kept high and where a business should locate. Break-even calculations and “job or batch or flow” questions are favourites.

4.1 Production of goods and services

Production is turning inputs (factors of production) into outputs (goods and services). Productivity measures how efficiently inputs are used:

Formula
Labour productivity = total output ÷ number of workers (in a given time)

Ways to increase productivity: better training, more motivated staff, improved technology and automation, better organisation of the workplace. Higher productivity lowers the cost per unit, which can mean higher profit or lower prices.

Inventory (stock) management

Businesses hold inventory of raw materials, work-in-progress and finished goods. Holding buffer inventory means production and sales can continue if deliveries are late or demand suddenly rises. But inventory costs money: storage space, insurance, risk of theft, damage or going out of date, and cash tied up that could be used elsewhere.

A business reorders when inventory falls to the reorder level, allowing for the lead time (days for delivery).

Lean production

Lean production aims to cut waste of all kinds (time, materials, inventory, movement, defects) to raise efficiency.

  • Kaizen: continuous small improvements suggested by workers, often in team meetings. Cheap to introduce and motivating, but improvements are gradual.
  • Just-in-time (JIT): materials arrive exactly when needed and finished goods are sent out immediately, so almost no inventory is held. This saves storage costs and frees cash, but needs very reliable suppliers: a late delivery stops production completely.

Technology in production

Automation, robots, computer-aided design (CAD) and computer-aided manufacturing (CAM), electronic point-of-sale (EPOS) systems and contactless payments raise productivity and accuracy and lower unit costs. However, technology is expensive to buy, needs training, quickly goes out of date and may cause redundancies and lower morale.

4.2 Costs, scale of production and break-even

Types of cost

  • Fixed costs do not change with output in the short run: rent, salaries, insurance, loan interest.
  • Variable costs change directly with output: raw materials, packaging, piece-rate wages.
  • Total cost = fixed costs + total variable costs.
  • Average cost (unit cost) = total cost ÷ output.

Economies and diseconomies of scale

Economies of scale are the factors that lower average cost as a business grows.

  • Purchasing: bulk-buying discounts.
  • Marketing: advertising costs spread over more units; can afford its own vehicles.
  • Financial: banks lend more easily and at lower interest to large firms.
  • Managerial: can employ specialist managers.
  • Technical: can afford advanced, efficient machinery.

Diseconomies of scale raise average cost when a business becomes too large: poor communication through many levels, low morale as workers feel unimportant, and weak co-ordination and slow decisions.

Break-even

The break-even point is the level of output at which total revenue equals total cost, so there is neither profit nor loss.

Formulas
Contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit
Break-even output = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit
Margin of safety = actual (or planned) output − break-even output
Worked example
A phone-repair stall pays RM2,400 a month for rent and a worker’s wage. Each repair uses RM20 of parts and is sold for RM80.
Contribution per repair = 80 − 20 = RM60
Break-even = 2,400 ÷ 60 = 40 repairs a month
If it does 55 repairs: margin of safety = 55 − 40 = 15 repairs; profit = 15 × RM60 = RM900.
Common Mistake
Dividing fixed costs by the price instead of the contribution. 2,400 ÷ 80 = 30 repairs ignores the RM20 of parts each repair uses, so the business would actually make a loss at 30 repairs. Always subtract variable cost first. If the answer is not a whole number, round up (39.2 → 40 units), because 39 units would still be a loss.

Uses of break-even: shows how many units must be sold to avoid a loss; shows the effect of changing price or costs; supports applications for loans. Limitations: assumes every unit made is sold, that price and variable cost stay the same at every output, and that fixed costs never change. It is only as accurate as the forecasts behind it.

Changes to the break-even point: a higher price or lower variable cost raises contribution and lowers break-even; higher fixed costs (a new loan, bigger premises) raise break-even.

4.3 Achieving quality production

Quality means producing a good or service that meets customers’ expectations and is fit for purpose. Good quality builds reputation and loyalty, allows higher prices and cuts the cost of waste, repairs and returns. Poor quality leads to complaints, returns, lost sales and a damaged brand.

5.1 Business finance: needs and sources

Why businesses need finance

  • Start-up capital: premises, equipment and first stock before any revenue arrives.
  • Working capital: day-to-day costs such as wages, materials and bills.
  • Expansion: new branches, machinery, research and development, buying other firms.

Capital expenditure is spending on fixed assets that last more than a year (machinery, vehicles, buildings). Revenue expenditure is spending on day-to-day running costs (wages, rent, fuel).

5.3 Income statements

5.4 Statement of financial position (balance sheet)

Shows what a business owns (assets) and owes (liabilities) on a particular date.

  • Non-current assets: kept for more than a year: buildings, machinery, vehicles.
  • Current assets: held for less than a year: inventory, trade receivables (money owed by customers), cash.
  • Current liabilities: debts due within a year: trade payables (money owed to suppliers), overdraft.
  • Non-current liabilities: debts due after more than a year: long-term bank loans.
  • Shareholders’ equity: share capital + retained earnings.
The balance
Total assets − total liabilities = shareholders’ equity

Worked example
Using the income statement above: gross profit margin = 80,000 ÷ 200,000 × 100 = 40%; profit margin = 30,000 ÷ 200,000 × 100 = 15%. If the gross margin is healthy but the profit margin falls year on year, overheads such as rent or salaries are rising faster than sales.

Limitations of ratios: they are based on past data; one year alone means little, so compare with previous years and competitors; they ignore non-financial factors such as staff morale and reputation; and different firms may calculate figures differently.

Common Mistake
Confusing liquidity with profitability. A firm can have a high profit margin and still have a current ratio of 0.6, which means it may struggle to pay its suppliers next month.

External Influences on Business Activity

The things a business cannot control: the economy, government policy, the environment, ethics, globalisation and exchange rates. The key skill is explaining the chain from the outside change to the business’s revenue, costs and profit.

6.1 Economic issues

The business cycle

6.2 Environmental and ethical issues

Externalities

  • External costs: costs of business activity paid by third parties, not the business: air and water pollution, traffic congestion, noise, loss of habitats.
  • External benefits: gains to third parties: new jobs, better roads built for a factory, training that workers take to other jobs.

Sustainable development means meeting today’s needs without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs: using renewable energy, recycling, cutting waste and packaging.

Pressure groups and government

Pressure groups try to change business behaviour through boycotts, protests and social media campaigns. Bad publicity can cut sales quickly, so firms often respond. Governments can control harmful activity with laws (limits on emissions), taxes and fines on pollution, and permits to pollute that can be traded.

Ethical decisions

An ethical decision is based on moral principles, doing what is right rather than only what is profitable: not using child labour, paying suppliers fairly (fair trade), being honest in advertising, protecting the environment.

6.3 Business and the international economy

Globalisation

Globalisation is the increasing freedom of movement of goods, capital and people around the world. It is driven by free-trade agreements, cheaper transport and the internet.

  • Opportunities: new markets abroad; cheaper materials and production; access to foreign skills and technology.
  • Threats: more competition from foreign firms with lower costs; staff may be attracted to foreign firms.

Governments may use protectionism (tariffs and import quotas) to shield domestic firms. This helps local producers but raises prices for consumers and for firms that use imported materials, and other countries may retaliate.

Multinational companies (MNCs)

A multinational has factories or service operations in more than one country.

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. When a currency appreciates it buys more foreign currency; when it depreciates it buys less.

Case: KL Scoot (12-mark recommendation)

Case (written for these notes)
KL Scoot assembles electric scooters. Its factory has five levels of hierarchy: managing director → factory manager → 4 section managers → 16 team leaders → 240 production workers. Last year it switched to the cheapest parts supplier and cut staff training by half. Exam-style · [12 marks]

Analyse the benefits and drawbacks of delayering for KL Scoot. Recommend whether KL Scoot should delayer the factory’s structure. Justify your answer.

Benefit: lower costs. Removing the section managers and 8 team leaders means KL Scoot no longer pays 12 management salaries. As returns of 3.5% are already adding repair costs, these savings could help restore profit, or be spent on better parts or the training that was cut.

Benefit: faster communication. Information from the production line would pass through three levels instead of four before reaching the managing director. 0450/12/M/J/25 Q2(e) · May/June 2025 · [6 marks]

Do you think job rotation is the best method for a large business to use to motivate its employees? Justify your answer.

For job rotation: Moving employees between tasks at the same level, such as from the till to stock control to customer service, makes the work less repetitive. Workers who are less bored are less likely to be absent or to leave, so absenteeism and labour turnover fall. In a large business with thousands of staff, even a small fall in labour turnover saves a lot in recruitment and training costs. Rotation also creates multi-skilled workers who can cover for absent colleagues, so work is not held up.

Against job rotation: The tasks are often just as routine as each other, so the boredom simply moves from one job to the next and any rise in motivation may not last. Workers must be trained for each task and are slower while they learn, which raises costs and can lead to more mistakes.

Alternative: job enrichment. Giving employees more challenging work and responsibility, such as letting an experienced worker organise a team’s schedule, meets Herzberg’s motivators of responsibility and achievement. This gives longer-lasting motivation, although it also needs training and not every worker wants extra responsibility.

Decision: I do not think job rotation is the best method. It reduces boredom but does not make the work any more challenging, so its effect tends to be short-lived. Job enrichment is better for a large business because genuine responsibility motivates staff over the long term, raising productivity and lowering labour turnover. Job rotation is still useful alongside it where tasks are simple and the business needs workers who can cover absences.

Why this scores
Two knowledge marks (less boredom, multi-skilling; training needed), two analysis marks (lower absenteeism and turnover costs; slower work and mistakes) and two for a decision justified by comparing with job enrichment. This is a general question, so there are no application marks. The examiner report warns that “it increases motivation” repeats the question, and that some students confused job rotation with job enrichment or thought it meant extra rest breaks.

On-the-job training. Advantage: The 4 new employees learn from Tim and ES’s 12 skilled employees, so they are taught exactly how ES builds boats to each customer’s design. Do you think lack of feedback is the main barrier to effective communication in a business? Justify your answer.

Lack of feedback: Without feedback, the sender cannot check that the message has been received or understood. Workers may act on a misunderstood instruction, for example producing the wrong quantity, which leads to mistakes, wasted materials and delays.

Other barriers: A long chain of command means a message passes through many people, so it can be distorted or lost before it reaches the receiver. Jargon or technical language means the receiver cannot understand the message at all, and choosing the wrong medium, such as giving complicated instructions by phone, means details are forgotten.

Decision: I do not think lack of feedback is always the main barrier. In a large business with many levels of hierarchy, distortion through a long chain of command is likely to cause more damage, because even feedback has to travel back through the same levels. However, lack of feedback is especially serious because it hides the other barriers: if feedback is asked for, misunderstandings caused by jargon or the wrong medium can be spotted and corrected. So which barrier matters most depends on the size and structure of the business.

0450/12/F/M/23 Q2(e) · February/March 2023 · [6 marks]

Do you think it is always better for a business to sell to a mass market? Justify your answer.

Selling to a mass market gives a business the chance of very high sales. Because it produces in large quantities, it can benefit from economies of scale, such as bulk-buying discounts, so its average cost per unit falls. It can then charge lower prices than smaller rivals and still make a profit on each item, and high sales volume can mean high total profit. Selling to many types of customer also spreads risk: if sales fall in one area, sales elsewhere may make up for it.

However, a mass market usually has a lot of competition, so businesses are under pressure to cut prices, which reduces profit margins. A niche market has fewer competitors, and because the product closely meets the needs of a small group of customers, the business can charge a higher price and build strong customer loyalty, giving a higher profit per item. This suits small businesses that could never compete with the costs of large firms.

I do not think it is always better to sell to a mass market. It is better for a large business that can produce in high volume, because only then are economies of scale big enough for its low costs to beat competitors. For a small business, the mass market is worse: it would face intense price competition without the low costs to survive it, whereas in a niche it can earn a higher margin from loyal customers. The best choice depends on the size of the business and whether its product can be made cheaply in large numbers.

Why this scores
Two knowledge points (economies of scale/high sales; higher prices in a niche), each developed with analysis, and a decision that explains when each market is better and why the other is weaker earns both evaluation marks. The examiner report says weaker answers simply repeated earlier points instead of evaluating; this is a general question, so there are no application marks.

Do you think retailers are the best channel of distribution for a manufacturing business to use? Justify your answer.

Using retailers gives a manufacturer wide distribution: its products are on shelves in many shops in many towns, so far more customers can see and buy them than if it sold only from its own premises. Retailers also display and often promote the products, so the manufacturer’s sales rise without it paying for shops or shop staff, and it can hold less inventory itself.

Source material: PF operates in a very competitive market. Philip will need to make a decision about how to promote the new range of shoes in a mass market. … We use sustainable sources of raw materials unlike our competitors which use imported artificial rubber.

Disadvantage: many people see leaflets as junk and throw them away unread, so much of the cost is wasted. Billboards on the side of a main road. Advantage: a billboard is seen every day by a very large number of drivers and passengers, which suits a mass market. Do you think the advantages for a manufacturing business of using flow production are greater than the disadvantages? Justify your answer.

Advantages: flow production makes very large quantities of identical products continuously, so the business gains economies of scale, such as discounts for buying raw materials in bulk. This lowers the average cost of each unit, so the business can cut its price to win customers or keep the price and earn a higher profit margin. Production lines are often automated and can run 24 hours a day, so labour costs per unit are low.

Disadvantages: every product is the same, so the business cannot easily customise its products or respond to changing tastes, and it may lose sales to competitors who offer more variety. The work is repetitive and boring, which can demotivate employees and raise absenteeism and labour turnover. If one machine breaks down, the whole line stops, so output is lost while costs continue.

Decision: I think the advantages are greater for a manufacturer of everyday products with high, steady demand, such as soft drinks or paper cups. Lower unit costs let it charge competitive prices, and customers for these products do not expect variety, so the lack of flexibility matters little. The disadvantages only outweigh the advantages when demand is small or customers want individual designs; then an expensive line would stand idle and batch or job production would be better. The risk of breakdowns can be reduced with regular maintenance, so it is less important than the cost advantage.

Why this scores
Two marks for valid points (economies of scale, inflexibility), two for developing them (lower unit costs; customer needs not met) and two for a justified decision that weighs one against the other and says when it would change. There are no application marks on this general question, and the mark scheme credits economies of scale only when it is developed to lower unit or average costs, not just “lower costs”.

Using Appendix 1 and other information, consider the two new products JJ could produce. Which product should JJ choose? Justify your answer using break-even calculations. • Product A • Product B

Product A (bracelet). Contribution = $50 − $25 = $25 per bracelet. Break-even = $50 000 ÷ $25 = 2000 bracelets a week. Margin of safety = 2500 − 2000 = 500 bracelets. Forecast profit = 500 × $25 = $12 500 a week.

Advantage: Product A gives the higher forecast profit, $12 500 a week, which fits JJ’s objective of increasing profit every year. Disadvantage: JJ must sell 2000 bracelets every week just to cover its costs, a high volume in a market with many competitors. Making 2500 extra items a week may also stretch its 60 full-time and 25 part-time workers, raising costs if overtime is needed.

Each unsold ring has also cost $40 to make.

It earns $2 500 a week more profit than the ring, which directly meets JJ’s objective of increasing profit, and its larger margin of safety gives more protection. The ring needs fewer sales to break even, but this advantage is outweighed by the rising unemployment: customers with falling incomes are more likely to keep buying a $50 bracelet than a $120 ring, so the ring’s small margin of safety could easily disappear. The bracelet also fits JJ’s affordable brand, while the ring does not. This depends on the forecast of 2500 bracelets a week being accurate; the forecasts were made before the rise in unemployment, so JJ should check the forecast and make sure its factory can produce the extra volume before launching.

Top level: both products are discussed in detail, the break-even, margin of safety and profit calculations are used as evidence inside the argument, and every point is applied to JJ (its profit objective, affordable brand, workforce and the unemployment forecast). Do you think quality assurance is the best way for a business to achieve quality production? Justify your answer.

Quality assurance. Workers check quality at every stage of production, so a fault is found where it happens instead of after the product is finished. This means less money is wasted reworking or scrapping finished goods. Giving workers responsibility for their own quality can also increase their motivation, leading to higher output. However, every worker has to be trained, which raises costs, and checking at each stage can slow production down so productivity falls.

Quality control. Inspectors check finished products, so production does not stop while checks are made and only the inspectors need training. However, faults are found only at the end, so the materials and labour used on a faulty product are already wasted, and the business still does not know where in the process the fault started. Inspectors add to labour costs, and as not every product can be checked, some faulty items may still reach customers and damage the business’s reputation.

Decision. I think quality assurance is the best way, because it prevents faults rather than just finding them. Quality control only stops faulty goods being sold; it does nothing to stop the same fault happening again tomorrow, so waste continues. The training cost of quality assurance is paid once, while the waste from quality control carries on. However, this depends on the workforce: if workers are unskilled or labour turnover is high, they may not check their work properly, and a business would need some final inspection as well.

Why this scores
This is a general question, so there are no application marks: the answer develops points for and against quality assurance (K, An) and uses quality control as the comparison, then makes a decision that explains why the alternative is weaker (E). For example, if ROCE was 10% in 2019, the fall to 8% shows the capital invested in its clothing factory is earning less profit. This warns managers to act, for example by cutting costs or dropping fashion lines that young customers no longer buy, before profitability falls further.

Compare with competitors. Managers can compare PJA’s ratios with those of its multinational rivals. PJA’s gross profit margin is 3 330 ÷ 7 400 × 100 = 45%. 0450/12/M/J/24 Q2(e) · May/June 2024 · [6 marks]

Do you think banks are the main external users of the accounts of a business? Justify your answer.

Banks. Before lending, a bank studies a business’s accounts to see whether it makes enough profit, and has enough liquidity, to repay a loan with interest. The accounts help the bank decide whether to lend, how much, and at what interest rate, since a riskier business is charged more.

Suppliers. Suppliers that sell on credit look at the current and acid test ratios to judge whether the business can pay its trade payables on time. If liquidity is poor, they may refuse credit or ask for cash on delivery, which protects them from bad debts.

Decision. I think banks are usually the main external users, because a bank loan is often far larger than the credit any one supplier gives, so the bank has more to lose and studies the accounts in more detail. Its decision also has the biggest effect on the business, as refusing a loan can stop an expansion. However, it depends on the business: a firm with no loans that buys most of its materials on credit from one supplier would find that supplier is its main external user.

Why this scores
Two marks for identifying users and what they look for, two for developing why, and two for a judgement that compares banks with another user. This mirrors the mark scheme’s reasoning that banks often lend large sums, while suppliers may care less if orders are small; an answer about banks alone cannot reach the evaluation marks.

Source material: The restaurant will serve a wide range of vegan meals and Jemi will purchase ingredients, including fruit and vegetables, directly from local suppliers. Jemi is going to use her own capital of $50 000 to buy the franchise, but she will also need a source of external finance to help set up the business.

Consider why the profitability and liquidity ratios for her restaurant will be important to Jemi. Which is likely to be the most important for the success of Jemi’s restaurant? Justify your answer.

Profitability ratios. The gross profit margin shows whether Jemi is controlling the cost of her ingredients. Appendix 3 says reliable fruit and vegetable suppliers are getting harder to find and restaurants are buying from further away, so her cost of sales may rise; a falling gross margin would warn her to change suppliers or raise menu prices. The profit margin shows whether expenses such as the chef’s wages and the other 11 staff are under control. ROCE compares her profit with the $50 000 she has invested: if it is lower than she could earn by saving the money, the restaurant is not worth the risk. However, these ratios use past figures, and a new restaurant has no previous year to compare with, so in the first year they may simply show low profit while the business builds up customers.

Liquidity ratios. The current ratio and acid test ratio show whether the restaurant can pay its short-term debts: fresh ingredients from local suppliers, wages for 12 employees and repayments on her external finance. Fresh fruit and vegetables cannot be kept for long, so the acid test ratio, which leaves out inventory, is a useful check. A current ratio of about 1.5 to 2 would show she can pay comfortably, and the lender of her external finance will look at these ratios before and after lending. If the ratios fall below 1, suppliers may stop delivering and the restaurant could be forced to close even if it is popular. However, liquidity ratios only show the position on one date, and very high liquidity can mean cash is lying idle instead of being used to grow the business.

Recommendation. I think liquidity ratios are the most important for the success of Jemi’s restaurant, at least in its first year. She has put all $50 000 of her own money into the franchise and must also repay external finance, so cash will be tight while she has daily bills for ingredients and wages. A new restaurant can survive a year of low profit, but not a month in which it cannot pay its suppliers, especially when reliable suppliers are already scarce. Profitability ratios are less important at the start because new businesses often make little profit while they build a customer base, and the rising trend in vegan eating (10% to 25%) suggests profit should follow. This depends on how large her external finance and repayments are; once the restaurant is established, ROCE becomes more important in deciding whether the franchise is a good use of her $50 000.

Why this scores
Level 3 (9–12) needs detailed discussion of both types of ratio applied to Jemi (her $50 000, external finance, fresh ingredients from local suppliers, a new restaurant) and a conclusion that explains why the other was rejected. The examiner report says this was the lowest-scoring question on the paper: most candidates confused profit with profitability, and only said liquidity is “about paying debts” without developing it.

Do you think a reduction in the level of unemployment always benefits businesses? Justify your answer.

Yes, it can benefit businesses. When unemployment falls, more people have a regular income, so household spending rises. Businesses such as shops and restaurants see higher demand for their products, so their revenue and profit can increase.

No, it can create problems. With fewer people looking for work, it becomes harder for a business to recruit, especially skilled workers. Vacancies may stay empty, so output cannot rise to meet the extra demand and orders may be lost. To attract and keep staff, firms may also have to offer higher wages, which raises labour costs and squeezes profit margins.

Decision. I do not think it always benefits businesses. A retailer selling to consumers is likely to gain, because the rise in spending directly increases its revenue while it needs few extra staff. But a labour-intensive manufacturer that needs many skilled workers to expand may find that staff shortages and a higher wage bill outweigh the extra sales. It depends on how much the business relies on recruiting new workers.

Two marks for identifying both sides (higher sales; recruitment difficulty and higher wages), two for developing them to revenue and costs, and two for a decision that explains when the benefit outweighs the drawback. Source material: Sam’s cookies are high-quality and often purchased by customers for special occasions such as birthdays or weddings. … The government has announced that interest rates will decrease from 1st November 2024. … Taxes will also change and will increase from 1st April 2025.

Cheaper borrowing also makes it more affordable to buy extra specialised baking equipment for the new cookies he plans to sell to other retail shops, raising output and possibly lowering average costs.

Decrease in interest rates: limited effect. CC is a small business with one shop, so if Sam borrows only a modest amount the saving in interest will be small compared with his total costs. CC’s cookies are bought for birthdays and weddings, which customers do not usually pay for with loans, so cheaper borrowing may do little to increase demand. The cut in interest rates may therefore change profit only slightly.

Increase in taxes: negative effect. If income taxes rise, consumers have less disposable income. The interest rate cut matters less because it only lowers the cost of a loan Sam has not yet taken out, so the saving is small for a business of CC’s size. However, this depends on how much Sam borrows: if he takes a large loan to buy new equipment and supply other retail shops, the lower interest rate could have the bigger effect.

Tim already plans to sell to customers in other countries, and ES has a reputation for high-quality boats built to customers’ designs, so it could win orders well beyond the market in country X. Higher sales would raise revenue and spread risk if demand at home falls.

Opportunity: cheaper component parts. Tariffs and quotas on component parts have also been removed, so ES can import parts more cheaply. This lowers the cost of each boat, so ES can either cut prices to compete abroad or keep its prices and increase its profit margin.

Threat: more competition at home. Removing tariffs in country X also lets foreign boat builders, including large multinationals, sell freely in ES’s home market, possibly at lower prices because of their economies of scale. ES is a sole trader with 12 employees and is only the third best boat builder in country X, so it could lose market share and revenue in the market it depends on today.

Threat: lack of experience abroad. ES has only ever sold in country X, so Tim does not know foreign customers’ tastes, competitors or laws, and selling abroad adds transport costs and exchange-rate risk. Mistakes could be expensive, and with an acid test ratio of only 0.5, ES has little cash to absorb losses while it learns.

Recommendation. I think the opportunities are likely to be greater than the threats for ES. Its revenue increased by 50% in 2024 and it has a strong reputation for high-quality, custom-built boats, which large mass-producing rivals find hard to copy, so ES can compete on quality rather than price both at home and abroad. The threat of foreign competition is real, but it will affect ES whether or not it expands, so taking the opportunity to sell abroad is the better response. However, this depends on how carefully Tim enters new markets: with an acid test ratio of only 0.5, he should research and start with one or two countries, or the threats could outweigh the gains.

Why this scores
Level 3 needs detailed, applied discussion of both opportunities and threats and a well-justified conclusion. Each point uses ES’s facts (tariffs and quotas removed, sole trader with 12 employees, third best boat builder, 50% revenue growth, acid test ratio 0.5), and the conclusion explains why the threats are outweighed and what the decision depends on.
